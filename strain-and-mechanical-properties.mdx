A machine tool that cuts every part 0.05 mm out of size produces scrap. Often the cause is a shaft that is strong enough but stretches too far under the cutting force. Strength and stiffness are different properties, and a stronger steel is not a stiffer steel. This lesson shows how to size a shaft for both, how to find the stretch of a rod whose thickness changes along its length, how to read the stress-strain curve of a tensile test, and how far a rubber mount moves in shear. #AxialStress #MaterialSelection #TaperedBars #CNCDesign

## Real-World System Problem: CNC Z-axis actuator shaft

<InArticleAd />


In a CNC machine (a computer-controlled machine tool) the Z-axis actuator moves the tool up and down. Its shaft carries the cutting force. If the shaft stretches under that force, the tool is no longer where the program says it is, and the part is cut to the wrong size.

### The shaft selection problem

> **Engineering Question:** Which material and which shaft diameter keep the stretch of the shaft within 0.05 mm under an axial load of 5,000 N?

Two separate checks answer this. The strength check: the stress must stay below the stress that the design allows. The stiffness check: the stretch must stay within the limit. Each check gives a smallest diameter, and the larger of the two is the design.

## Fundamental Theory: Strain, Hooke's law, and the stress-strain curve

<InArticleAd />


**What this lesson takes from the earlier one.** Stress is force over area, $\sigma = F/A$, in MPa. The yield strength $\sigma_{yield}$ is the stress above which a metal does not return to its first shape. The safety factor (SF), also called the factor of safety, is the yield strength divided by the stress. See [Fundamental Stress Concepts](/education/mechanics-of-materials/fundamental-stress-concepts/).

### Axial strain and deformation

Strain is the change in length of a bar divided by its original length. A rubber band 100 mm long that is pulled to 110 mm has a strain of 0.1. While a material is elastic (it returns when the load is removed), stress and strain rise together: $\sigma = E\epsilon$, which is Hooke's law. $E$ is Young's modulus, the stiffness of the material. The two give the stretch of a bar.

<Card title="Axial strain and deflection" icon="document">
$$\epsilon = \frac{\delta}{L_0}, \qquad \delta = \frac{F L_0}{A E}$$

Where:
- $\epsilon$ (epsilon) = axial strain (a ratio, no unit)
- $\delta$ (delta) = change in length (mm)
- $L_0$ = original length (mm)
- $F$ = axial force (N)
- $A$ = cross-sectional area (mm²)
- $E$ = Young's modulus (GPa; in a calculation, 1 GPa = 1,000 MPa = 1,000 N/mm²)

In words: more force or more length gives more stretch, and more area or a stiffer material gives less.
</Card>

### Bars of varying cross-section

$\delta = FL/(AE)$ holds only when $F$, $A$ and $E$ are the same at every section. Many parts are not uniform: a pull-rod is tapered to save weight, a shaft has steps for its bearings, and a long hanging bar carries its own weight.

Think of such a bar as a line of short springs joined end to end. Each spring stretches by its own amount, a thin one more than a thick one, and the stretch of the bar is the sum. A real bar has no joints, so the springs become slices of zero thickness and the sum becomes an integral.

<Card title="The general rule for any non-uniform bar" icon="document">
Take a slice of thickness $dx$ at distance $x$ from the fixed end. It is so short that the internal force $N(x)$, in newtons, and the area $A(x)$ do not change across it. So the uniform-bar formula gives the stretch of the slice:

$$d\delta = \frac{N(x)\,dx}{A(x)\,E}$$

The total extension is the sum over all the slices from $x = 0$ to $x = L$:

$$\boxed{\;\delta = \int_0^L \frac{N(x)\,dx}{A(x)\,E}\;}$$

For a uniform bar, $N$ and $A$ are constants and the integral returns $\delta = NL/(AE)$.
</Card>

:::note[The four-step recipe]
1. **Write $A(x)$**: how the area changes with the distance from one end.
2. **Write $N(x)$**: the internal force. For a bar loaded at its end it is the load $W$ at every section. For a bar under its own weight it is the weight of the material below the cut.
3. **Integrate** $N(x)\,dx / (A(x)E)$ from $0$ to $L$.
4. **Find the maximum stress separately.** It is at the smallest area and needs no integral: $\sigma_{max} = N/A_{min}$.
:::

#### Case A: Circular bar tapering linearly (the standard case)

![A round bar that tapers from diameter D1 at the fixed left end to the smaller diameter D2 at the right end, where the load W pulls. A thin slice of thickness dx is marked at distance x from the fixed end, where the diameter is d(x)](./images/tapered-bar-axial.svg)

A round bar tapers evenly from diameter $D_1$ at the fixed end to $D_2$ at the loaded end, over a length $L$. It carries an axial load $W$, in newtons.

<Steps>

1. **Geometry.** The diameter falls off linearly with $x$:

   $$d(x) = D_1 - \frac{(D_1 - D_2)}{L}x = D_1 - kx, \qquad k = \frac{D_1 - D_2}{L}$$

   so the area at any section is $A(x) = \dfrac{\pi}{4}\left(D_1 - kx\right)^2$.

2. **Internal force.** The bar is loaded only at its end, so $N(x) = W$ at every section.

3. **Substitute into the general rule:**

   $$\delta = \int_0^L \frac{W\,dx}{\frac{\pi}{4}(D_1 - kx)^2 E} = \frac{4W}{\pi E}\int_0^L \frac{dx}{(D_1 - kx)^2}$$

4. **Do the integral.** Since $\dfrac{d}{dx}\left[\dfrac{1}{k(D_1-kx)}\right] = \dfrac{1}{(D_1-kx)^2}$:

   $$\delta = \frac{4W}{\pi E}\left[\frac{1}{k(D_1 - kx)}\right]_0^L = \frac{4W}{\pi E k}\left(\frac{1}{D_2} - \frac{1}{D_1}\right)$$

5. **Back-substitute** $k = (D_1 - D_2)/L$ and combine the fractions, $\dfrac{1}{D_2}-\dfrac{1}{D_1} = \dfrac{D_1 - D_2}{D_1 D_2}$:

   $$\delta = \frac{4WL}{\pi E (D_1 - D_2)} \cdot \frac{D_1 - D_2}{D_1 D_2}$$

   The taper difference cancels, leaving the standard result:

   $$\boxed{\;\delta = \frac{4WL}{\pi E D_1 D_2}\;}$$

</Steps>

A uniform bar of diameter $\sqrt{D_1 D_2}$ stretches exactly as much as this taper.

:::caution[The most common mistake]
Do **not** find the extension from the average area or from the area at mid-length. The thin slices stretch much more than the thick slices save, so the answer is too small: 11% too small for a 2:1 taper, and more for a steeper one. The mid-length diameter is correct for one thing only: the stress at mid-length.
:::

#### Case B: Flat bar of constant thickness and tapering width

A plate of constant thickness $t$ tapers in width from $b_1$ to $b_2$ over length $L$ under axial load $W$. Its area is $A(x) = t\left(b_1 - \frac{(b_1-b_2)}{L}x\right)$. Here the area itself changes in a straight line (for the round bar it was the diameter), so the integral gives a natural logarithm, $\ln$:

$$\delta = \frac{W}{Et}\int_0^L \frac{dx}{b_1 - \frac{(b_1-b_2)}{L}x} = \frac{WL}{Et(b_1 - b_2)}\ln\!\left(\frac{b_1}{b_2}\right)$$

The uniform plate that stretches as much has the width $b_{eq} = (b_1-b_2)/\ln(b_1/b_2)$. The maximum stress is again at the narrow end, $\sigma_{max} = W/(t\,b_2)$.

#### Case C: Stepped bar (the same idea with a few thick slices)

If the section changes in steps, each segment is uniform, and the integral becomes a sum over the segments:

$$\delta = \sum_i \frac{N_i L_i}{A_i E_i}$$

Check the stress in each segment: the critical one has the largest $N_i/A_i$. [Compound Bars and Composite Systems](/education/mechanics-of-materials/compound-bars-and-composite-systems/) uses this sum for segments of different materials.

#### Case D: When the internal force varies (self-weight)

A hanging bar carries its own weight. The integral is the same, but now $N(x)$ changes along the bar: a section near the bottom carries little weight, and a section at the top carries all of it.

![Panel (a): a uniform bar hanging from a ceiling, with a slice dx at height x above its free end; a triangle beside it shows the internal force, zero at the free end and largest at the support. Panel (b): a cone hanging from its wide base, point down](./images/self-weight-extension.svg)

- **Uniform bar hanging under its own weight** ($\rho$, rho, = density in kg/m³, $g$ = 9.81 m/s²). At a height $x$ above the free end the section carries the weight below it, $N(x) = \rho g A x$, so

  $$\delta = \int_0^L \frac{\rho g A x\,dx}{AE} = \frac{\rho g L^2}{2E} = \frac{W_{total}L}{2AE}$$

  This is half the extension that the total weight would give if it hung at the free end. The largest stress is at the support, $\sigma_{max} = \rho g L$.

- **Conical bar hanging from its base.** The weight below a cut is that of a small cone, $N(x) = \rho g A(x) x/3$, and the same integral gives $\delta = \rho g L^2/(6E)$. This is one third of the value for the uniform bar, because most of the material is near the support.

### The stress-strain curve

Bend a paper clip a little and it springs back. Bend it far and it stays bent. The first behaviour is elastic and the second is plastic. A tensile test finds the stress at which a metal passes from one to the other.

The test pulls a standard test piece, called a coupon, until it breaks. The force divided by the original area is the stress, and the stretch divided by the original length is the strain. The plot of one against the other is the stress-strain curve.

![Panel (a): the complete stress-strain curve of a steel. It rises steeply, bends over, reaches a peak marked ultimate tensile strength, then falls through a part marked necking to the fracture point. Panel (b): the first small part of the same curve, magnified. A straight elastic line of slope E ends at the proportional limit. A parallel line that starts at a strain of 0.002 meets the curve at the yield strength](./images/stress-strain-offset-construction.svg)

The two panels show the same curve at two scales. The elastic part, which decides every stretch calculated in this lesson, is so narrow in panel (a) that it looks vertical, so panel (b) magnifies it. The axes have no numbers, because metals share the shape and not the values. Application 3 gives the values for one steel.

<Card title="Key points on the curve" icon="document">
- **Elastic line:** the straight first part. Its slope is Young's modulus $E$. Remove the load here and the coupon returns to its first length.
- **Proportional limit:** the stress at which the curve leaves the straight line.
- **Yield strength (0.2% offset), $\sigma_y$:** many metals show no sharp yield point, so one is defined. Draw a line parallel to the elastic line, starting at a strain of 0.002. The stress where it meets the curve is the yield strength.
- **Ultimate tensile strength (UTS):** the highest stress on the curve. After it the coupon becomes thin at one place (necking), and the stress falls.
- **Fracture point:** where the coupon breaks. The strain here measures the ductility: how far the metal stretches before it breaks.
- **Unloading:** from any point past yield, the stress falls along a line parallel to the elastic line. The strain $\sigma/E$ returns, and the rest stays as permanent strain. At the yield strength the permanent strain is exactly 0.002.
</Card>

:::note[Strength and stiffness are different]
Young's modulus $E$, the slope of the elastic line, is the stiffness. Yield strength and UTS are strengths: how much stress the material can carry. Heat treatment and alloying can double the yield strength of a steel, but its $E$ stays near 200 GPa. So a stronger steel does not stretch less.
:::

### Modulus of resilience and modulus of toughness

It takes work to stretch a part, and the material takes in that work as energy. The energy taken in by each unit of volume is the area under the curve (1 MPa times a strain of 1 is 1 MJ/m³). Two areas have names.

<Card title="Energy absorption measures" icon="document">
$$u_r = \frac{\sigma_y^2}{2E} \quad \text{(modulus of resilience, J/m}^3\text{)}$$

$$u_T \approx \text{area under the full stress-strain curve} \quad \text{(modulus of toughness, J/m}^3\text{)}$$

Resilience is the energy stored up to yield: the triangle under the elastic line, half of $\sigma_y$ times the strain $\sigma_y/E$. The material gives it back when the load is removed, as a spring does. Toughness is all the energy taken in before fracture. Most of it is plastic work, which is not given back.
</Card>

![Panel (a): the whole area under the stress-strain curve up to fracture is shaded and labelled modulus of toughness. Panel (b): in the magnified first part of the curve, only the small triangle under the elastic line up to the yield strength is shaded and labelled modulus of resilience](./images/stress-strain-energy-areas.svg)

For a metal that stretches far before it breaks, the elastic triangle is hundreds of times smaller than the whole area.

### Shear modulus

Put a thick book on a table and push its top cover sideways. The pages slide over each other and the book leans. This is shear. The shear strain $\gamma$ (gamma) is the sideways movement of the top divided by the thickness of the book. The shear modulus $G$ is the stiffness in shear, as $E$ is the stiffness in tension. It follows from $E$ and Poisson's ratio $\nu$, the ratio that says how much a bar thins as it stretches.

<Card title="Shear modulus and its relation to E and Poisson's ratio" icon="document">
$$G = \frac{E}{2(1 + \nu)}$$

$$\tau = G \gamma, \qquad \gamma = \frac{\tau}{G}, \qquad \delta_{shear} = \gamma \, h$$

Where:
- $G$ = shear modulus (MPa)
- $\tau$ (tau) = shear stress, the force along a surface divided by the area of that surface (MPa)
- $\gamma$ = shear strain (no unit; it is the angle of lean in radians)
- $h$ = thickness of the sheared layer (mm)
- $\delta_{shear}$ = sideways movement of the top of the layer (mm)

For metals, $G$ is between $0.38E$ and $0.40E$. For rubber, with $\nu \approx 0.49$, $G \approx E/3$.
</Card>

## Application 1: CNC actuator shaft, steel vs aluminum

The CNC Z-axis shaft must carry an axial force of up to 5,000 N and stretch no more than 0.05 mm over its 400 mm length.

:::note[System Problem Statement]
- **Component:** CNC Z-axis actuator shaft, circular cross-section
- **Maximum axial force:** $F = 5{,}000$ N (tension)
- **Shaft length:** $L = 400$ mm
- **Deformation limit:** $\delta \leq 0.05$ mm
- **Safety factor:** $SF = 3$
- **Material Option A:** Aluminum 6061-T6, $E = 70$ GPa, $\sigma_{yield} = 270$ MPa
- **Material Option B:** Steel 1045, $E = 200$ GPa, $\sigma_{yield} = 530$ MPa
- **Task:** for each material, find the smallest shaft diameter that the strength check allows and the smallest that the stiffness check allows; say which check decides

**Key Question:** Should this shaft be aluminum or steel, and what diameter meets both the strength and the stiffness requirement?
:::

### Step 1: Minimum diameter from the strength criterion

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the strength calculations**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Allowable stress for each material.** This is the highest stress the design permits: the yield strength divided by the safety factor, $SF = 3$:

   Aluminum: $\sigma_{allow} = 270 / 3 = 90 \text{ MPa}$ ✅

   Steel: $\sigma_{allow} = 530 / 3 = 177 \text{ MPa}$ ✅

2. **Minimum area.** Stress is force over area, so the area must be at least force over allowable stress, $A_{min} = F / \sigma_{allow}$ (N divided by MPa gives mm²):

   Aluminum: $A_{min} = 5{,}000 / 90 = 55.6 \text{ mm}^2$ ✅

   Steel: $A_{min} = 5{,}000 / 177 = 28.2 \text{ mm}^2$ ✅

3. **Minimum diameter.** The area of a circle is $\pi d^2/4$, so $d_{min} = \sqrt{4 A_{min} / \pi}$:

   Aluminum: $d_{min} = \sqrt{4 \times 55.6 / \pi} = 8.41 \text{ mm}$ ✅

   Steel: $d_{min} = \sqrt{4 \times 28.2 / \pi} = 6.00 \text{ mm}$ ✅

   For strength alone, a thin shaft is enough in either material. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 2: Minimum diameter from the stiffness criterion

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the stiffness calculations**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Required area.** The stretch $\delta = FL/(AE)$ must not exceed 0.05 mm. Solve for the area: $A \geq FL/(\delta_{allow} \cdot E)$. Use N, mm and $E$ in MPa (70 GPa = 70,000 MPa), so that the area is in mm²:

   Aluminum: $A = \frac{5{,}000 \times 400}{0.05 \times 70{,}000} = \frac{2{,}000{,}000}{3{,}500} = 571 \text{ mm}^2$ ✅

   Steel: $A = \frac{5{,}000 \times 400}{0.05 \times 200{,}000} = \frac{2{,}000{,}000}{10{,}000} = 200 \text{ mm}^2$ ✅

2. **Required diameter** from $d = \sqrt{4A/\pi}$:

   Aluminum: $d = \sqrt{4 \times 571 / \pi} = \sqrt{727.4} = 27.0 \text{ mm}$ ✅

   Steel: $d = \sqrt{4 \times 200 / \pi} = \sqrt{254.6} = 15.95 \text{ mm} \approx 16.0 \text{ mm}$ ✅

3. **Compare the two criteria:**

   For aluminum: strength needs 8.4 mm, stiffness needs 27.0 mm. Stiffness decides, by a factor of 3.2. ✅

   For steel: strength needs 6.0 mm, stiffness needs 16.0 mm. Stiffness decides, by a factor of 2.7. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 3: Verify the final design and compare materials

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the design comparison**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Check the stretch with the chosen diameters**, from $\delta = FL/(AE)$. For the 27 mm aluminum shaft:

   $A = \pi (27)^2 / 4 = 572.6 \text{ mm}^2$ ✅

   $\delta = \frac{5{,}000 \times 400}{572.6 \times 70{,}000} = \frac{2{,}000{,}000}{40{,}082{,}000} = 0.0499 \text{ mm}$ (just within 0.05 mm) ✅

   For the 16 mm steel shaft:

   $A = \pi (16)^2 / 4 = 201.1 \text{ mm}^2$ ✅

   $\delta = \frac{5{,}000 \times 400}{201.1 \times 200{,}000} = \frac{2{,}000{,}000}{40{,}220{,}000} = 0.0497 \text{ mm}$ (just within 0.05 mm) ✅

2. **Estimate the mass of each shaft**, density times volume, $m = \rho A L$, with the area in m² ($1 \text{ mm}^2 = 10^{-6} \text{ m}^2$) and the length in m:

   Aluminum ($\rho = 2{,}700$ kg/m³): $m = 2{,}700 \times 572.6 \times 10^{-6} \times 0.4 = 0.618$ kg ✅

   Steel ($\rho = 7{,}850$ kg/m³): $m = 7{,}850 \times 201.1 \times 10^{-6} \times 0.4 = 0.631$ kg ✅

   The masses are nearly equal. Steel is 2.9 times as dense as aluminum, but it is also 2.9 times as stiff, so its shaft needs 2.9 times less area. ✅

3. **Design decision:** Steel 1045 at 16 mm diameter passes both checks. Its diameter is 41% smaller (16 mm against 27 mm), so it fits a tighter space, and its mass is about the same as the aluminum shaft. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

A steel shaft sized for strength alone, 6 mm, would be safe, and it would stretch seven times too far.

## Application 2: Tapered steel pull-rod on the machine spindle

<InArticleAd />


A pull-rod is held at one end and pulled at the other, and it moves with the machine axis, so every kilogram of it slows the axis. A taper saves mass. The rod has the shape drawn in Case A, with the sizes below.

:::note[System Problem Statement]
- **Component:** steel pull-rod, circular section, tapering linearly
- **Diameter at the fixed (anchor) end:** $D_1 = 50$ mm
- **Diameter at the loaded end:** $D_2 = 25$ mm
- **Length:** $L = 900$ mm
- **Axial tensile load:** $W = 25$ kN
- **Material:** Steel 1045, $E = 200$ GPa, $\sigma_{yield} = 530$ MPa
- **Task:** find the maximum stress and where it occurs, the total extension, the stress at mid-length, and the diameter of the uniform rod that would be equally stiff; then compare mass with a uniform rod

**Key Question:** How much does a rod that changes section along its length stretch, and how much material does the taper save?
:::

### Step 1: Maximum stress and where it acts

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the maximum stress calculation**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Find the critical section.** The rod is loaded only at its ends, so the internal force is $W = 25$ kN at every section. The stress $\sigma = W/A(x)$ is therefore largest where the area is smallest: at the loaded end, $D_2 = 25$ mm. No integral is needed. ✅

2. **Area of the small end:**

   $$A_2 = \frac{\pi D_2^{\,2}}{4} = \frac{\pi (25)^2}{4} = 490.9 \text{ mm}^2$$ ✅

3. **Maximum stress**, with 25 kN written as 25,000 N:

   $$\sigma_{max} = \frac{W}{A_2} = \frac{4W}{\pi D_2^{\,2}} = \frac{25{,}000}{490.9} = 50.93 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

4. **Compare with the large end:**

   $$A_1 = \frac{\pi (50)^2}{4} = 1{,}963.5 \text{ mm}^2, \qquad \sigma_{min} = \frac{25{,}000}{1{,}963.5} = 12.73 \text{ MPa}$$

   The stress at the small end is four times the stress at the large end, because twice the diameter is four times the area. The safety factor against yield is $530/50.93 = 10.4$. So strength is not the limit for this rod. As for the shaft in Application 1, stiffness is. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 2: Total extension of the tapered rod

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the extension calculation**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Choose the relation.** The area changes along the rod, so $\delta = WL/(AE)$ does not apply. Case A of the theory gave the result for a round taper:

   $$\delta = \frac{4WL}{\pi E D_1 D_2}$$

2. **Substitute, with every length in mm and $E$ in MPa = N/mm²**, so that the answer is in mm:

   $$\delta = \frac{4 \times 25{,}000 \times 900}{\pi \times 200{,}000 \times 50 \times 25} = \frac{90 \times 10^6}{7.854 \times 10^8} = 0.1146 \text{ mm}$$ ✅

3. **Check that the size is reasonable.** The rod is thinner than 50 mm and thicker than 25 mm, so its extension must lie between those of the two uniform rods:

   - Uniform 50 mm: $\delta = \dfrac{25{,}000 \times 900}{1{,}963.5 \times 200{,}000} = 0.0573$ mm
   - Uniform 25 mm: $\delta = \dfrac{25{,}000 \times 900}{490.9 \times 200{,}000} = 0.2292$ mm

   The taper result, 0.1146 mm, lies between the two. It is exactly $D_1/D_2 = 2$ times the uniform 50 mm value, as the formula says. ✅

   The mid-length area, $1{,}104.5$ mm², would give $0.1019$ mm, which is 11.1% too small.

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 3: Stress at mid-length

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the mid-length stress**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Diameter at mid-length.** The diameter changes in a straight line, so at $x = L/2$ it is midway between the end diameters:

   $$d_{mid} = \frac{D_1 + D_2}{2} = \frac{50 + 25}{2} = 37.5 \text{ mm}$$ ✅

2. **Area and stress there:**

   $$A_{mid} = \frac{\pi (37.5)^2}{4} = 1{,}104.5 \text{ mm}^2, \qquad \sigma_{mid} = \frac{25{,}000}{1{,}104.5} = 22.64 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

3. **Notice that it is not the average.** The mid-length stress (22.64 MPa) is well below the average of the end stresses ($(50.93 + 12.73)/2 = 31.83$ MPa). Stress goes as $1/d^2$, so it stays low over most of the rod and rises steeply only near the small end. This is why a taper wastes little: only a small part of the rod is highly stressed. ✅

4. **Local strain.** $\epsilon = \sigma/E$ gives $2.55 \times 10^{-4}$ at the small end and $6.37 \times 10^{-5}$ at the large end. The strain changes along the rod, and this is why the extension needed an integral. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 4: Equivalent uniform rod and the mass saved

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the equivalence and mass comparison**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Equivalent uniform diameter.** Set the extension of a uniform rod of diameter $d_{eq}$ equal to the extension of the taper:

   $$\frac{4WL}{\pi E d_{eq}^{\,2}} = \frac{4WL}{\pi E D_1 D_2} \;\Rightarrow\; d_{eq} = \sqrt{D_1 D_2} = \sqrt{50 \times 25} = 35.36 \text{ mm}$$ ✅

   Check: $A_{eq} = \pi(35.36)^2/4 = 981.7$ mm², and $\delta = \dfrac{25{,}000 \times 900}{981.7 \times 200{,}000} = 0.1146$ mm, the same as Step 2. ✅

2. **Volume of the tapered rod.** Its shape is a cone with the point cut off:

   $$V = \frac{\pi L}{12}\left(D_1^2 + D_1 D_2 + D_2^2\right) = \frac{\pi (900)}{12}\left(2{,}500 + 1{,}250 + 625\right) = 1.031 \times 10^6 \text{ mm}^3$$ ✅

   Mass at $\rho = 7{,}850$ kg/m³ ($10^6 \text{ mm}^3 = 10^{-3} \text{ m}^3$): $m = 7{,}850 \times 1.031 \times 10^{-3} = 8.09$ kg ✅

3. **Compare with the uniform 50 mm rod**, the rod you would use if the anchor end decides the diameter:

   $$V_{uniform} = 1{,}963.5 \times 900 = 1.767 \times 10^6 \text{ mm}^3, \qquad m = 13.87 \text{ kg}$$

   The taper saves 41.7% of the material and 5.8 kg of moving mass. The price is twice the extension: 0.115 mm against 0.057 mm. ✅

4. **Design decision.** If this rod may stretch 0.15 mm, the taper passes (0.115 mm) with a safety factor above 10, and it is the better design because the mass is on a moving axis. If the limit were 0.10 mm, the taper would fail on stiffness, and the small end would need to be larger. Solve $\delta = 4WL/(\pi E D_1 D_2)$ for $D_2$ with $D_1 = 50$ mm: $D_2 \geq 4WL/(\pi E D_1 \delta_{allow}) = 28.6$ mm, so choose a small end of 30 mm. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Variation: The same method on a flat tapered tie plate

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the flat-taper worked example**</summary>

An aluminum tie plate ($E = 70$ GPa) of constant thickness $t = 10$ mm tapers in width from $b_1 = 120$ mm at the anchor to $b_2 = 60$ mm at the pin, over $L = 600$ mm. It carries $W = 40$ kN.

<Steps>

1. **Maximum stress**, force over the smallest area, at the narrow end ($A_2 = 10 \times 60 = 600$ mm²):

   $$\sigma_{max} = \frac{40{,}000}{600} = 66.67 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

2. **Extension** from the flat-taper result of Case B:

   $$\delta = \frac{WL}{Et(b_1-b_2)}\ln\!\left(\frac{b_1}{b_2}\right) = \frac{40{,}000 \times 600}{70{,}000 \times 10 \times 60}\ln\!\left(\frac{120}{60}\right)$$

   $$\delta = \frac{24 \times 10^6}{42 \times 10^6} \times 0.6931 = 0.5714 \times 0.6931 = 0.396 \text{ mm}$$ ✅

3. **Equivalent uniform width:**

   $$b_{eq} = \frac{b_1 - b_2}{\ln(b_1/b_2)} = \frac{60}{0.6931} = 86.56 \text{ mm}, \qquad A_{eq} = 865.6 \text{ mm}^2$$

   Check: $\delta = \dfrac{40{,}000 \times 600}{865.6 \times 70{,}000} = 0.396$ mm ✅

   The equivalent width (86.6 mm) is below the mid-length width (90 mm), as it was for the round taper. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Practice: Four variations to work yourself

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the practice problems and their answers**</summary>

Work each one with the four-step recipe, then compare with the answers.

1. **Circular taper.** A steel bar ($E = 200$ GPa) tapers from $D_1 = 80$ mm to $D_2 = 40$ mm over $L = 1{,}200$ mm under $W = 45$ kN. Find the maximum stress, the total extension, and the stress at mid-length.

   *Answers:* $\sigma_{max} = 4(45{,}000)/(\pi \times 40^2) = 35.81$ MPa at the small end; $\delta = 4(45{,}000)(1{,}200)/(\pi \times 200{,}000 \times 80 \times 40) = 0.1074$ mm; $d_{mid} = 60$ mm so $\sigma_{mid} = 45{,}000/2{,}827.4 = 15.92$ MPa. ✅

2. **Flat taper.** An aluminum plate ($E = 70$ GPa), thickness 8 mm, tapers from 100 mm to 50 mm wide over 500 mm under 30 kN. Find the maximum stress and the extension.

   *Answers:* $\sigma_{max} = 30{,}000/(8 \times 50) = 75.0$ MPa; $\delta = \dfrac{30{,}000 \times 500}{70{,}000 \times 8 \times 50}\ln 2 = 0.5357 \times 0.6931 = 0.3713$ mm. ✅

3. **Design (inverse) problem.** A steel taper ($E = 200$ GPa) has $D_1 = 60$ mm, $L = 800$ mm, and carries $W = 35$ kN. The extension must not exceed 0.15 mm. What is the smallest permissible $D_2$, and what is the resulting maximum stress?

   *Answers:* rearranging, $D_2 \geq \dfrac{4WL}{\pi E D_1 \delta_{allow}} = \dfrac{4(35{,}000)(800)}{\pi (200{,}000)(60)(0.15)} = 19.81$ mm, so use 20 mm. Then $\delta = 0.1485$ mm ✅ and $\sigma_{max} = 4(35{,}000)/(\pi \times 400) = 111.4$ MPa, a safety factor of 4.8 against a 530 MPa yield. ✅

4. **Self-weight.** A uniform steel bar hangs vertically from its top end, $L = 12$ m, $\rho = 7{,}850$ kg/m³, $E = 200$ GPa, carrying no other load. Find the extension and the maximum stress.

   *Answers:* $\delta = \rho g L^2/(2E) = (7{,}850)(9.81)(144)/(2 \times 200 \times 10^9) = 2.77 \times 10^{-5}$ m $= 0.0277$ mm; $\sigma_{max} = \rho g L = 0.924$ MPa at the top support, where the whole weight is carried. Note the area never entered either answer. ✅

</details>

## Application 3: Interpreting a tensile test of a steel coupon

<InArticleAd />


The yield strength in every design calculation comes from a tensile test. Here is the record of one test.

:::note[System Problem Statement]
- **Component:** standard round steel coupon (ASTM E8, a standard for tensile tests)
- **Gauge length:** $L_0 = 50$ mm (the measured length of the coupon)
- **Diameter:** $d = 12.5$ mm, giving $A_0 = \pi(12.5)^2/4 = 122.7$ mm²
- **Material:** medium-carbon steel, $E = 200$ GPa
- **Curve data (read from the test record):**
  - Proportional limit: approximately 280 MPa
  - 0.2% offset yield strength: $\sigma_y = 350$ MPa
  - Ultimate tensile strength: $\sigma_{UTS} = 520$ MPa
  - Fracture stress: 410 MPa (after necking)
  - Elongation at fracture: 22% of gauge length
- **Task:** confirm the 0.2% offset construction, compute the modulus of resilience, estimate the modulus of toughness, and compute the percent elongation

**Key Question:** How much energy can this steel take in, and how far does it stretch before it breaks?
:::

### Step 1: Confirm the 0.2% offset yield construction

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the 0.2% offset construction**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **What "0.2% offset" means.** Draw a straight line with the slope $E$ (200 GPa) that starts on the strain axis at $\epsilon = 0.002$ and not at zero. The stress where that line meets the stress-strain curve is defined as the yield strength. A coupon loaded to this stress and unloaded keeps a permanent strain of 0.002, which is 0.2%.

2. **Find the strain at the yield point.** At $\sigma_y = 350$ MPa the elastic strain is stress over $E$:

   $$\epsilon_{elastic} = \frac{\sigma_y}{E} = \frac{350 \text{ MPa}}{200{,}000 \text{ MPa}} = 0.00175$$ ✅

   The total strain at the 0.2% offset yield point is the elastic strain plus the offset, $0.00175 + 0.002 = 0.00375$. This is where the offset line meets the curve. ✅

3. **Read the other points:**
   - Proportional limit $\approx 280$ MPa at a strain of $280/200{,}000 = 0.0014$ (the curve is still straight)
   - UTS = 520 MPa at a strain of about 12% (the top of the curve)
   - Fracture at a strain of 22% and 410 MPa (the stress is lower because the neck has made the coupon thinner, and the stress is still calculated with the original area)

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 2: Modulus of resilience

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the resilience calculation**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Formula.** The modulus of resilience is the area under the elastic part of the curve, taken as a triangle:

   $$u_r = \frac{\sigma_y^2}{2E}$$

2. **Calculation**, with both values in pascals, so that the answer is in J/m³:

   $$u_r = \frac{(350 \times 10^6)^2}{2 \times (200 \times 10^9)} = \frac{1.225 \times 10^{17}}{4.0 \times 10^{11}} = 306{,}250 \text{ J/m}^3 \approx 306 \text{ kJ/m}^3$$ ✅

3. **Meaning.** Each cubic metre of this steel can store 306 kJ and give it back. A material with a high resilience makes a good spring. A material with a high toughness (Step 3) is good at taking a blow without breaking. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 3: Estimate the modulus of toughness

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the toughness estimate**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Approach.** The modulus of toughness is the whole area under the stress-strain curve. For an estimate, add two areas: the elastic triangle, and a plastic part whose height is taken as the average of the yield strength and the UTS:

   $$u_T \approx u_r + \frac{(\sigma_y + \sigma_{UTS})}{2} \times (\epsilon_{fracture} - \epsilon_y^{*})$$

   where $\epsilon_y^{*}$ is the elastic strain at yield (0.00175) and $\epsilon_{fracture} = 0.22$.

2. **Calculation:**

   Elastic contribution: $u_r = 306{,}250$ J/m³ (from Step 2) ✅

   Average stress over plastic region: $(350 + 520)/2 = 435$ MPa ✅

   Plastic strain range: $0.22 - 0.00175 = 0.2183$ ✅

   Plastic contribution: $435 \times 10^6 \times 0.2183 = 94{,}961{,}000$ J/m³ $\approx 94.96$ MJ/m³ ✅

   Total: $u_T \approx 0.31 + 94.96 \approx 95.3$ MJ/m³ ✅

3. **Interpretation.** The steel takes in about 95 MJ per cubic metre before it breaks. Nearly all of it (99.7%) is plastic work, done during the large permanent stretch. This is why a medium-carbon steel suits parts that must take a blow without shattering. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 4: Percent elongation (ductility)

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the percent elongation**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Percent elongation** is the growth of the gauge length, as a percentage of the original gauge length. $L_f$ is the gauge length at fracture:

   $$\%EL = \frac{L_f - L_0}{L_0} \times 100 = \frac{(50 \times 1.22) - 50}{50} \times 100 = 22\%$$ ✅

   (The 22% was read from the curve. It means that the gauge length grew from 50 mm to 61 mm before the coupon broke.) ✅

2. **Classification.** A material with $\%EL > 5\%$ is called ductile. At 22% this steel is clearly ductile. An overloaded part stretches and necks where people can see it, well before it breaks. A brittle material, such as cast iron, breaks with no warning. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

## Application 4: Shear modulus and a bonded elastomer anti-vibration mount

<InArticleAd />


An anti-vibration mount is a layer of rubber (an elastomer) bonded between two metal plates. When the machine on top moves sideways, the rubber is sheared, as the book was. Its softness in shear keeps the vibration of the machine from the floor.

:::note[System Problem Statement]
- **Component:** bonded natural-rubber anti-vibration mount
- **Bonded pad dimensions:** $80\ \text{mm} \times 80\ \text{mm}$ (shear area $A = 6{,}400$ mm²)
- **Rubber layer thickness:** $h = 20$ mm
- **Material:** natural rubber, $E = 4$ MPa, $\nu = 0.49$
- **Applied horizontal shear force:** $F = 800$ N
- **Task:** compute $G$ from $E$ and $\nu$, then find shear stress $\tau$, shear strain $\gamma$, and the lateral shear deflection $\delta_{shear}$

**Key Question:** How far does the top of the mount move sideways under a horizontal load of 800 N, and is that a usual value for a mount of this kind?
:::

```text
            F = 800 N ────►
  ┌───────────────────────────┐   top plate, moves sideways
  ├───────────────────────────┤
  │    rubber, h = 20 mm      │   bonded area 80 mm × 80 mm
  ├───────────────────────────┤
  └───────────────────────────┘   bottom plate, fixed
```

### Step 1: Shear modulus from E and Poisson's ratio

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the shear modulus calculation**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Find $G$** from $E$ and $\nu$:

   $$G = \frac{E}{2(1 + \nu)} = \frac{4 \text{ MPa}}{2(1 + 0.49)} = \frac{4}{2 \times 1.49} = \frac{4}{2.98} = 1.342 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

2. **Context.** Rubber keeps almost the same volume when it is squeezed, so its $\nu$ is close to $0.50$. Then $2(1+\nu)$ is close to $3$, and $G \approx E/3$. The real difference is in size: the $E$ of rubber, 4 MPa, is 50,000 times smaller than the $E$ of steel. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 2: Shear stress in the rubber layer

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the shear stress calculation**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Shear stress** is the force divided by the area it slides along, here the bonded area:

   $$\tau = \frac{F}{A} = \frac{800 \text{ N}}{6{,}400 \text{ mm}^2} = 0.125 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

2. **Compare with the strength of the rubber in shear.** Natural rubber usually carries a shear stress of 1 to 2 MPa before it tears. At 0.125 MPa the mount is far below that. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 3: Shear strain and lateral deflection

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the shear strain and deflection**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Shear strain**, shear stress over $G$:

   $$\gamma = \frac{\tau}{G} = \frac{0.125 \text{ MPa}}{1.342 \text{ MPa}} = 0.0931 \text{ rad}$$ ✅

2. **Lateral shear deflection.** The shear strain is the sideways movement divided by the thickness, so the movement is strain times thickness. (This holds for small angles of lean, and 0.0931 rad is about 5°.)

   $$\delta_{shear} = \gamma \times h = 0.0931 \times 20 \text{ mm} = 1.86 \text{ mm}$$ ✅

3. **Interpret the result.** The top moves about 1.9 mm under 800 N, so the stiffness of the mount in shear is $k = F/\delta = 800/1.86 = 430$ N/mm. Mounts for light machines usually have between 200 and 800 N/mm, so this mount is in the usual range. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

## Design guidelines for strain and material properties

<InArticleAd />


1. Check stiffness and strength separately, and take the larger diameter.
2. Design against the yield strength. A design that uses the UTS as its limit accepts permanent stretch.
3. For a part loaded in shear (a pin, a glued joint, a rubber mount), use the shear modulus $G$, and not $E$.

:::tip[Instrument it]
You can measure the stiffness that sized the actuator shaft. Fix a dial indicator or an LVDT (a sensor that reads small movements) at the loaded end and read the real stretch under a known load. It is usually larger than the calculated one, because the joints and bearings also give a little. An [ESP32 or STM32](/education/sensor-actuator-interfacing-stm32/) that logs the LVDT lets you [trend the measured stiffness on siliconwit.io](https://siliconwit.io) and confirm that the shaft meets its tolerance.
:::

## Summary and Next Steps

<InArticleAd />


### Key concepts

1. **Axial deflection** of a uniform bar is $\delta = FL/(AE)$. Stiffness and strength are separate checks.
2. **Bars of changing section** follow $\delta = \int_0^L N(x)\,dx/(A(x)E)$. The maximum stress is $N/A_{min}$, and an average area never gives the extension.
3. **The tensile test curve** gives the stiffness, the yield strength, the UTS, the ductility and the energy the material takes in. Unloading follows a line parallel to the elastic line.
4. **Shear modulus** is $G = E/(2(1+\nu))$, and the sideways movement of a sheared layer is $\delta_{shear} = \gamma h$.
