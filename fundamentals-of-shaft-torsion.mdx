A shaft can be strong enough and still spoil the machine. The 25 mm crankshaft in this lesson carries its peak torque with a safety factor of 3.7. It also twists by 0.56°, more than the 0.5° that its indexing mechanism allows, so parts stop in the wrong place. Every shaft needs two checks: the shear stress (will it yield?) and the angle of twist (will it stay accurate?). This lesson gives you the formula for each, shows why the stress is zero at the centre and largest at the surface, turns the power and speed of a motor into torque, and shows why a tube carries the same torque with about half the metal. #Torsion #ShearStress #ShaftDesign

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

1. **Explain** the torsional shear stress formula and why the stress is largest at the outer surface
2. **Calculate** peak shear stress and angle of twist for solid and hollow circular shafts
3. **Convert** power and rotational speed into torque, and apply the result to a shaft design
4. **Compare** a hollow and a solid shaft carrying the same torque, and quantify the material saving

## Real-World System Problem: Geneva Mechanism Crankshaft

<InArticleAd />


A Geneva mechanism turns steady rotation into motion in steps: a wheel with slots moves on by one slot, stops, and moves again. It is used in rotary indexing tables and assembly machines. The drive crankshaft turns all the time, and each time its pin enters a slot, the crankshaft carries a short peak of torque.

### The torsional challenge

> **Engineering Question:** How do we size the crankshaft so it can transmit peak torque without yielding, and keep the angular twist small enough to preserve timing accuracy?

Both checks matter. A shaft that carries the stress but twists too far puts the wheel short of its position or past it. The mechanism then jams, or the machine works on the part in the wrong place.

## Fundamental Theory: Torsional Mechanics

<InArticleAd />


Torque, $T$, is a turning effort: a force times its distance from the axis, in N·m. A torque on a shaft makes each cross-section try to slide around the axis against the next one. The material resists that sliding with shear stress, $\tau$: a force that acts along a surface, divided by the area of the surface.

The shear stress is not the same across the section. Think of a turning wheel: a point on the rim travels a long arc, a point near the hub travels a short one, and the centre does not move. A twisted shaft deforms in the same way. The material is distorted in proportion to its distance from the axis, so the stress is zero at the centre and largest at the surface. This holds for a round shaft that stays elastic. It does not hold for a square or open section.

![Panel (a): a circular shaft cross-section with arrows for the shear stress, which rises in a straight line from zero at the centre to tau max at the outer surface. Panel (b): the same section with one thin ring at distance rho from the centre, of width d rho and area 2 pi rho d rho. Adding every ring times the square of its distance gives the polar second moment J, which is pi d to the fourth power over 32 for a solid shaft](./images/torsion-stress-and-polar-moment.svg)

The picture writes $\rho$ for the distance from the centre and keeps $r$ for the outer radius. The formulas below write $r$ for both.

Panel (b) shows where $J$ comes from. Each thin ring counts by its area times the square of its distance from the centre, so material near the surface counts far more than material near the centre. This is the reason that hollow shafts are efficient.

<Card title="Torsional shear stress" icon="document">
The stress at a point is the torque times the distance of the point from the centre, divided by $J$:

$$\tau = \frac{T \, r}{J}$$

Where:
- $\tau$ = shear stress (Pa)
- $T$ = applied torque (N·m)
- $r$ = distance from the shaft centre (m). The largest stress is at the surface, $r = d/2$
- $J$ = polar second moment of area (m$^4$)

No material property is in this formula. Two shafts of the same size under the same torque have the same stress, whatever they are made of.
</Card>

<Card title="Polar second moment of area" icon="document">
$J$ says how much of the area of the section lies far from the axis. For a solid shaft of diameter $d$ (m):

$$J_{solid} = \frac{\pi \, d^4}{32}$$

For a hollow shaft with outer diameter $d_o$ and inner diameter $d_i$:

$$J_{hollow} = \frac{\pi \left(d_o^4 - d_i^4\right)}{32}$$

The diameter is raised to the fourth power, so a shaft that is 10% larger in diameter has a $J$ that is 46% larger.
</Card>

<Card title="Angle of twist" icon="document">
The angle of twist is the angle by which one end of the shaft turns against the other end:

$$\varphi = \frac{T \, L}{G \, J}$$

Where:
- $\varphi$ = angle of twist (radians; multiply by $180/\pi$ for degrees)
- $L$ = shaft length (m)
- $G$ = shear modulus (Pa), the stiffness of the material against shear. For steel $G \approx 80$ GPa

More torque or more length gives more twist. A stiffer material or a larger $J$ gives less. A long or thin shaft can pass the stress check and still twist too far.
</Card>

![A shaft of length L and radius r, fixed at the left end and twisted by a torque T at the right end. A line that was straight along the surface is now slanted by the angle gamma, and the free end has turned by the angle phi. The same arc at the free end is r times phi and also L times gamma, which leads to phi equals T L over G J](./images/torsion-twist-geometry.svg)

The formula comes from this picture. The arc moved through by the end of the surface line can be measured in two ways: as $r\varphi$ from the rotation of the end, or as $L\gamma$ from the slant of the line, where $\gamma$ is the shear strain at the surface. So $\gamma = r\varphi/L$. With $\tau = G\gamma$ and $\tau = Tr/J$, this gives $\varphi = TL/(GJ)$.

<Card title="Power and torque" icon="document">
Motors are rated by power and speed, not by torque. Power is torque times angular speed, so a motor of power $P$ (W) at angular speed $\omega$ (rad/s) delivers:

$$T = \frac{P}{\omega}, \qquad \omega = \frac{2 \pi \, n}{60} \text{ (rad/s, with } n \text{ in rpm)}$$

Convert the nameplate data to torque before you use the torsion formulas.
</Card>

:::tip[Units]
This lesson works in SI base units: torque in N·m, lengths in m, $G$ in Pa and $J$ in m$^4$. Then $\varphi$ is in radians and $\tau$ is in Pa. Divide Pa by $10^6$ for MPa.
:::

## Application 1: Geneva Mechanism Crankshaft

<InArticleAd />


This is the crankshaft of the Geneva drive. Two questions are answered in order: is the 25 mm shaft strong enough, and does it twist little enough for the timing?

```text
  fixed end                        free end
    ░░│
    ░░├───────────────────────────────┐
    ░░│ solid steel shaft, d = 25 mm  │ ◄── torque T
    ░░├───────────────────────────────┘
    ░░│
      │◄───────── L = 200 mm ────────►│
```

The allowable shear stress, $\tau_{allow}$, is the largest shear stress the designer accepts. The safety factor (SF) is the allowable stress divided by the working stress.

:::note[System Problem Statement]
- **Component:** Geneva mechanism drive crankshaft, solid steel ($G = 80$ GPa, $\tau_{allow}$ = 180 MPa)
- **Shaft diameter:** 25 mm, **Length:** 200 mm
- **Continuous torque:** $T_{cont}$ = 50 N·m (normal running)
- **Peak engagement torque:** $T_{peak}$ = 150 N·m (slot entry)
- **Required safety factor:** 3.0 on peak torque
- **Precision requirement:** angular twist must stay within $\pm 0.5°$

**Key Question:** Can this 25 mm crankshaft safely handle 150 N·m peak torque with SF = 3.0, and will the angular twist stay within the $\pm 0.5°$ precision budget?
:::

### Step 1: Polar second moment for the 25 mm shaft

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the polar moment calculation**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Apply the solid-shaft formula.** Convert the diameter to metres, 25 mm = $25 \times 10^{-3}$ m:

   $$J = \frac{\pi d^4}{32} = \frac{\pi (25 \times 10^{-3})^4}{32} = \frac{\pi \times 3.906 \times 10^{-7}}{32} = 3.835 \times 10^{-8} \text{ m}^4$$ ✅

2. **Keep this value.** The stress and the twist both use it.

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 2: Torsional stress check

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the torsional stress analysis**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Continuous running stress** at the surface, $r = d/2 = 0.0125$ m. Torque in N·m times radius in m over $J$ in m$^4$ gives Pa:

   $$\tau_{cont} = \frac{T_{cont} \cdot r}{J} = \frac{50 \times 0.0125}{3.835 \times 10^{-8}} = 16.3 \times 10^{6} \text{ Pa} = 16.3 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

2. **Peak engagement stress:**

   $$\tau_{peak} = \frac{T_{peak} \cdot r}{J} = \frac{150 \times 0.0125}{3.835 \times 10^{-8}} = \frac{1.875}{3.835 \times 10^{-8}} = 48.9 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

   For a solid shaft, $Tr/J$ simplifies to the shorter form $\tau = 16T/(\pi d^3)$. It gives the same result, with $d$ in metres:

   $$\tau_{peak} = \frac{16 \times 150}{\pi \times (0.025)^3} = \frac{2{,}400}{\pi \times 1.563 \times 10^{-5}} = \frac{2{,}400}{4.909 \times 10^{-5}} = 48.9 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

3. **Safety factor at peak torque:**

   $$\text{SF} = \frac{\tau_{allow}}{\tau_{peak}} = \frac{180 \text{ MPa}}{48.9 \text{ MPa}} = 3.7$$ ✅

   The 25 mm shaft passes the strength check at $SF = 3.7$, above the required 3.0. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 3: Angle of twist check

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the angular deformation analysis**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Angle of twist under peak torque ($L$ = 200 mm = 0.2 m, $G = 80$ GPa = $80 \times 10^9$ Pa):**

   $$\varphi = \frac{T L}{G J} = \frac{150 \times 0.2}{80 \times 10^9 \times 3.835 \times 10^{-8}} = \frac{30}{3{,}068} = 9.78 \times 10^{-3} \text{ rad}$$ ✅

   Converting to degrees: $\varphi = 9.78 \times 10^{-3} \times (180/\pi) = 0.56°$ ✅

2. **Check against the precision requirement of $\pm 0.5°$:**

   $0.56° > 0.5°$: the shaft twists a little too far. A slightly larger diameter corrects this, and the strength is already enough. ✅

3. **Required diameter for $\varphi \leq 0.5° = 8.727 \times 10^{-3}$ rad.** Rearrange the twist formula for $J$, then the formula for $J$ for $d$:

   $$J_{req} = \frac{T L}{G \varphi} = \frac{150 \times 0.2}{80 \times 10^9 \times 8.727 \times 10^{-3}} = \frac{30}{6.982 \times 10^8} = 4.30 \times 10^{-8} \text{ m}^4$$ ✅

   $$d = \left(\frac{32 \, J_{req}}{\pi}\right)^{1/4} = \left(\frac{32 \times 4.30 \times 10^{-8}}{\pi}\right)^{1/4} = (4.38 \times 10^{-7})^{0.25} = 0.0257 \text{ m} = 25.7 \text{ mm}$$

   Rounding up to the next standard size, **26 mm**, meets both strength and stiffness requirements. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

:::note[Engineering Insight]
The 25 mm crankshaft is strong enough ($SF = 3.7$) and a little too flexible (0.56° against a limit of 0.5°). Stiffness decides this design, not strength. That is usual for precise indexing mechanisms. One more millimetre of diameter brings the twist to 0.48° and adds little mass.
:::

## Application 2: Sizing a Power-Transmitting Motor Shaft

<InArticleAd />


A small conveyor drive has a 5 kW induction motor that runs at 960 rpm. A steel shaft 400 mm long joins the motor to a gearbox. A 20 mm solid shaft is proposed. The nameplate gives power and speed, so the torque must be found first.

:::note[System Problem Statement]
- **Component:** solid steel motor output shaft, $d$ = 20 mm, $L$ = 400 mm
- **Motor power:** $P$ = 5 kW at $n$ = 960 rpm
- **Material:** steel, $G$ = 80 GPa, $\tau_{allow}$ = 60 MPa
- **Task:** compute the delivered torque, the peak shear stress, and the angle of twist; check the stress against the allowable

**Key Question:** Does the 20 mm shaft keep shear stress within the 60 MPa allowable, and how many degrees does it twist over its 400 mm length?
:::

Enter the motor shaft in the simulator and read the peak shear stress and the angle of twist over its length:

<div style={{ display: 'flex', justifyContent: 'center', width: '100%', margin: '0.5rem 0 1rem' }}>
  <LinkButton href="/product-development/shaft-torsion-simulator/" target="_blank" variant="primary">Open the Shaft Torsion Simulator</LinkButton>
</div>

### Step 1: Torque from power and speed

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the torque calculation**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Convert rotational speed to rad/s.** One turn is $2\pi$ rad and one minute is 60 s:

   $$\omega = \frac{2 \pi n}{60} = \frac{2 \pi \times 960}{60} = 100.5 \text{ rad/s}$$ ✅

2. **Compute the transmitted torque** (5 kW = 5,000 W):

   $$T = \frac{P}{\omega} = \frac{5{,}000 \text{ W}}{100.5 \text{ rad/s}} = 49.8 \text{ N·m}$$ ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 2: Polar second moment and peak shear stress

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the stress calculation**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Polar second moment for the 20 mm solid shaft** ($d$ = 0.020 m):

   $$J = \frac{\pi d^4}{32} = \frac{\pi (0.020)^4}{32} = \frac{\pi \times 1.600 \times 10^{-7}}{32} = 1.571 \times 10^{-8} \text{ m}^4$$ ✅

2. **Peak shear stress at the outer surface ($r$ = 10 mm = 0.010 m):**

   $$\tau = \frac{T r}{J} = \frac{49.8 \times 0.010}{1.571 \times 10^{-8}} = \frac{0.498}{1.571 \times 10^{-8}} = 31.7 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

3. **Safety factor:**

   $$\text{SF} = \frac{\tau_{allow}}{\tau} = \frac{60 \text{ MPa}}{31.7 \text{ MPa}} = 1.89$$ ✅

   The stress is within the allowable. A factor of 1.89 is acceptable for a steady, well-known load. A drive with shock loads or many starts and stops would need a larger diameter. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 3: Angle of twist

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the angle of twist calculation**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Apply the twist formula ($L$ = 400 mm = 0.4 m, $G = 80 \times 10^9$ Pa):**

   $$\varphi = \frac{T L}{G J} = \frac{49.8 \times 0.4}{80 \times 10^9 \times 1.571 \times 10^{-8}} = \frac{19.92}{1{,}257} = 0.01585 \text{ rad}$$ ✅

2. **Convert to degrees:**

   $$\varphi = 0.01585 \times \frac{180°}{\pi} = 0.91°$$ ✅

   Over 400 mm the shaft twists by a little less than one degree. A conveyor drive accepts this. A precise indexing shaft would need a larger diameter. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

:::note[Engineering Insight]
The 20 mm shaft is adequate for steady duty and has little margin for shock or fatigue. Shear stress falls with the cube of the diameter, so the safety factor rises with the cube: 22 mm gives about 2.5, and 25 mm gives about 3.7.
:::

## Application 3: Hollow Versus Solid Shaft for the Same Torque

<InArticleAd />


A drive shaft must transmit $T$ = 200 N·m with an allowable shear stress of 60 MPa. Two designs are compared: the smallest solid shaft, and a tube with a larger outer diameter.

```text
  Design A: solid, 26 mm (cut along the axis)
  ┌─────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │             metal all through           │
  └─────────────────────────────────────────┘

  Design B: hollow, 32 mm outside
  ┌─────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │ wall, 3 mm                              │
  ├─────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │             empty bore, 26 mm           │
  ├─────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │ wall, 3 mm                              │
  └─────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

The tube is sized for equal strength. Its outer diameter is set at a larger standard size, $d_o$ = 32 mm, and its bore is the largest that keeps the stress within the allowable:

- The stress limit needs $J \geq T r_o/\tau_{allow} = 200 \times 0.016/(60 \times 10^6) = 5.333 \times 10^{-8}$ m$^4$.
- That gives $d_i \leq \left(d_o^4 - 32J/\pi\right)^{1/4} = 26.7$ mm, so $d_i$ = 26 mm is used.

The two shafts are then compared by their cross-sectional area, which for one material is also their mass per metre.

:::note[System Problem Statement]
- **Torque:** $T$ = 200 N·m
- **Allowable shear stress:** $\tau_{allow}$ = 60 MPa
- **Design A:** solid shaft, diameter sized to meet the allowable
- **Design B:** hollow shaft, outer diameter $d_o$ = 32 mm, inner diameter $d_i$ = 26 mm
- **Task:** compute $J$, peak shear stress, and cross-sectional area for each; find the area saving

**Key Question:** How much lighter is the hollow shaft compared to the solid shaft for the same torque and the same allowable stress?
:::

### Step 1: Size the solid shaft

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the solid shaft sizing**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Minimum diameter from the allowable stress.** Rearranging $\tau = 16T/(\pi d^3)$ for $d$, with $\tau_{allow} = 60$ MPa = $60 \times 10^6$ Pa:

   $$d_{min} = \left(\frac{16 T}{\pi \, \tau_{allow}}\right)^{1/3} = \left(\frac{16 \times 200}{\pi \times 60 \times 10^6}\right)^{1/3} = \left(\frac{3{,}200}{1.885 \times 10^8}\right)^{1/3} = (1.698 \times 10^{-5})^{1/3} = 0.02570 \text{ m} = 25.7 \text{ mm}$$

   Round up to the standard size: **$d$ = 26 mm**. ✅

2. **Polar moment for the 26 mm solid shaft:**

   $$J_{solid} = \frac{\pi (0.026)^4}{32} = \frac{\pi \times 4.570 \times 10^{-7}}{32} = 4.486 \times 10^{-8} \text{ m}^4$$ ✅

3. **Shear stress check:**

   $$\tau_{solid} = \frac{T r}{J} = \frac{200 \times 0.013}{4.486 \times 10^{-8}} = \frac{2.60}{4.486 \times 10^{-8}} = 57.96 \text{ MPa} \approx 58.0 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

   This is a little below the 60 MPa allowable, so the 26 mm size is correct. ✅

4. **Cross-sectional area** (here $d$ is in mm, so the area is in mm$^2$):

   $$A_{solid} = \frac{\pi \, d^2}{4} = \frac{\pi \times (26)^2}{4} = 530.9 \text{ mm}^2$$ ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 2: Analyse the hollow shaft

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the hollow shaft analysis**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Polar second moment for the hollow shaft ($d_o$ = 32 mm = 0.032 m, $d_i$ = 26 mm = 0.026 m):**

   $$J_{hollow} = \frac{\pi (d_o^4 - d_i^4)}{32} = \frac{\pi \left[(0.032)^4 - (0.026)^4\right]}{32}$$

   Working out the fourth powers:
   - $(0.032)^4 = 1.049 \times 10^{-6}$ m$^4$ ✅
   - $(0.026)^4 = 4.570 \times 10^{-7}$ m$^4$ ✅
   - Difference: $1.049 \times 10^{-6} - 4.570 \times 10^{-7} = 5.916 \times 10^{-7}$ m$^4$ ✅

   $$J_{hollow} = \frac{\pi \times 5.916 \times 10^{-7}}{32} = 5.808 \times 10^{-8} \text{ m}^4$$ ✅

2. **Peak shear stress at the outer surface ($r_o$ = 16 mm = 0.016 m):**

   $$\tau_{hollow} = \frac{T \, r_o}{J_{hollow}} = \frac{200 \times 0.016}{5.808 \times 10^{-8}} = \frac{3.20}{5.808 \times 10^{-8}} = 55.1 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

   The peak stress of the hollow shaft is a little lower than that of the solid shaft (55.1 MPa against 58.0 MPa). Its outer radius is larger, which raises $Tr$, but its $J$ is larger by more. ✅

3. **Cross-sectional area:**

   $$A_{hollow} = \frac{\pi (d_o^2 - d_i^2)}{4} = \frac{\pi (32^2 - 26^2)}{4} = \frac{\pi \times 348}{4} = 273.3 \text{ mm}^2$$ ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 3: Compare and quantify the saving

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the comparison**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Area saving (equal to the saving in mass per metre for the same material):**

   $$\text{saving} = \frac{A_{solid} - A_{hollow}}{A_{solid}} \times 100 = \frac{530.9 - 273.3}{530.9} \times 100 = \frac{257.6}{530.9} \times 100 = 48.5\%$$ ✅

2. **Summary side by side:**

   | Quantity | Solid, $d$ = 26 mm | Hollow, $d_o/d_i$ = 32/26 mm |
   |---|:---:|:---:|
   | $J$ (m$^4$) | $4.486 \times 10^{-8}$ | $5.808 \times 10^{-8}$ |
   | Peak $\tau$ (MPa) | 58.0 | 55.1 |
   | Area (mm$^2$) | 530.9 | 273.3 |
   | Mass saving | reference | **48.5% lighter** |

</Steps>

</details>

:::note[Engineering Insight]
The material near the centre of a solid shaft adds almost nothing to $J$, because its distance $r$ from the axis is small, and it still adds its full weight. Here the tube has nearly half the mass and a peak stress a little below that of the solid shaft. The cost is a larger outer diameter (32 mm against 26 mm). Robot arms, aircraft linkages and fast spindles accept that cost to save weight. The comparison has a limit: a tube with a very thin wall can buckle (its wall folds) before the shear stress reaches the allowable.
:::

## Design Guidelines for Torsional Loading

<InArticleAd />


<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Convert power to torque first" icon="rocket">
  Every motor-driven shaft starts with $T = P/\omega$. An error in the torque passes directly into the stress and the twist.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Check strength and stiffness separately" icon="warning">
  A shaft can pass the shear stress check and still twist too far for a timing mechanism or a coupling. It must pass both checks.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Increase diameter before you shorten the shaft" icon="setting">
  A diameter 10% larger lowers the stress by 25% and the twist by 32% (stiffness up 46%). A length 10% shorter lowers the twist by only 10% and does not change the stress.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Consider hollow sections for weight" icon="puzzle">
  The centre of a shaft adds little strength or stiffness in torsion. A tube of somewhat larger outer diameter can carry the same torque with about half the area, as Application 3 shows.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

:::tip[Instrument it]
A strain gauge bridge bonded at 45 degrees to the shaft axis reads the strain from torsion, and the torque follows from it. Two encoders, one at each end of the shaft, read the real twist. An [ESP32 or STM32](/education/sensor-actuator-interfacing-stm32/) that reads both can [stream torque and twist data to siliconwit.io](https://siliconwit.io) and show when a shaft twists beyond its limit.
:::

## Summary and Next Steps

<InArticleAd />


### Key concepts

1. **Torsional shear stress** is $\tau = Tr/J$: zero at the centre and largest at the surface, for a solid or hollow circular shaft. It does not depend on the material.
2. **Polar second moment** is $J = \pi d^4/32$ for a solid shaft and $J = \pi(d_o^4 - d_i^4)/32$ for a hollow one.
3. **Angle of twist** is $\varphi = TL/(GJ)$. A shaft must pass the stress check and the twist check.
4. **Torque from a motor** is $T = P/\omega$, with $\omega = 2\pi n/60$.
5. **A hollow shaft** has no material where the stress would be low, so it carries the same torque with far less mass.

These formulas are for a plain round shaft. A step in diameter or a keyway raises the stress locally above these values.
