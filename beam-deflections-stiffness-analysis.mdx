A shelf full of books does not break. It sags, and the sag is the complaint. On a machine the same fault costs more: a circuit board that sags cracks its solder joints, an X-ray arm that droops blurs the image, and a machine rail that bends cuts the part to the wrong size. None of these parts is near breaking. They are strong enough, and they are not stiff enough. This lesson shows you how to calculate how far a beam bends under its load, what the supports and the span do to that number, and how to make a beam stiffer at the lowest cost. #BeamDeflection #Stiffness #PrecisionEngineering

## Engineering challenge: Deflection in precision systems

<InArticleAd />


### The universal deflection challenge

Strength and stiffness are two different properties. **Strength** is the load at which a part yields or breaks. **Stiffness** is how little the part moves under a load that is far below that. A fishing rod is strong and not stiff. The [lesson on bending stresses](/education/mechanics-of-materials/bending-stresses-simple-beams/) checks strength. This lesson checks stiffness, and for a precise machine it is usually stiffness that decides the size of a beam.

> **Engineering Question:** How far does a beam bend under its load, is that inside the limit for its job, and what is the cheapest way to make it stiffer?

## Fundamental Theory: Elastic beam deflections

<InArticleAd />


### The elastic curve equation

A loaded beam bends into a curve called the **elastic curve**. Elastic means that the beam returns to straight when the load is removed. The distance that a point of the beam moves from its unloaded position is its **deflection** $y$, measured at a position $x$ along the beam. This lesson counts $y$ positive upward, so a beam that sags has a negative $y$. The size of the largest deflection is written $\delta$ (delta), a positive number.

```text
 unloaded   ▲────────────────────────────────▲

                            P
                            ▼
 loaded     ▲────────╮              ╭────────▲
                     ╰──────────────╯
                       δ, the largest deflection
```

The drawing shows the sag much larger than it is. In the three machines of this lesson $\delta$ is less than half a millimetre.

Two things decide how sharply a beam curves at any point: how hard it is bent there, and how much it resists.

<Card title="Deriving the elastic curve equation" icon="document">

In words: curvature equals the bending moment divided by the flexural rigidity of the beam.

$$\frac{1}{\rho} = \frac{M}{EI}$$

Where:
- $\rho$ (rho) = radius of curvature: the radius of the circle that the bent beam follows at that point (m). A small radius is a sharp bend.
- $M(x)$ = bending moment at the position $x$ (N·m)
- $E$ = Young's modulus, the stiffness of the material (Pa)
- $I$ = second moment of area, the stiffness that the shape of the cross-section gives (m⁴)
- $EI$ = flexural rigidity, the stiffness of the beam in bending (N·m²)

For a beam that bends only a little, the curvature $1/\rho$ is very nearly $d^2y/dx^2$, the rate at which the slope of the beam changes along its length. This holds while the slope stays below about 10°, which is true of nearly every machine part. So:

$$\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = \frac{M(x)}{EI}$$

- Where the moment is large the beam curves sharply. Where the moment is zero the beam is straight.
- A larger $EI$ gives less curvature for the same moment.
- $E$ comes from the material and $I$ from the shape. The yield strength is not in the equation: a stronger steel is not a stiffer steel.

</Card>

### Double integration method

The elastic curve equation gives the curvature. Integrate it once and you have the slope of the beam. Integrate again and you have the deflection. Each integration leaves an unknown constant, and the supports supply the facts that fix the constants.

<Tabs>
  <TabItem label="Integration Steps">

    The example is a cantilever of length $L$, fixed at $x = 0$, with a downward load $P$ on its free tip.

    **1. Write the bending moment $M(x)$.** A cut at $x$ has the load $P$ at a distance $(L - x)$ to its right, and the beam hogs, so the moment is negative:
    $$M(x) = -P(L-x)$$

    **2. Put it into the elastic curve equation:**
    $$\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = \frac{-P(L-x)}{EI}$$

    **3. Integrate once for the slope.**

    <Card title="Slope equation" icon="document">
    $$\frac{dy}{dx} = \int \frac{M(x)}{EI} dx + C_1$$

    $dy/dx$ is the slope of the bent beam at $x$, and $C_1$ is the first unknown constant. For the cantilever:
    $$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{P}{EI}\left[-Lx + \frac{x^2}{2}\right] + C_1$$
    </Card>

    **4. Integrate again for the deflection.**

    <Card title="Deflection equation" icon="document">
    $$y = \int\int \frac{M(x)}{EI} dx \, dx + C_1 x + C_2$$

    $y$ is the deflection at $x$, and $C_2$ is the second unknown constant. For the cantilever:
    $$y = \frac{P}{EI}\left[-\frac{Lx^2}{2} + \frac{x^3}{6}\right] + C_1 x + C_2$$
    </Card>

    **5. Use the support to find $C_1$ and $C_2$.** A fixed end cannot rotate and cannot move. Its slope is zero, so $C_1 = 0$. Its deflection is zero, so $C_2 = 0$.

    **6. Read the deflection at the tip ($x = L$):**
    $$y(L) = \frac{P}{EI}\left[-\frac{L^3}{2} + \frac{L^3}{6}\right] = \frac{-PL^3}{3EI}$$

    The minus sign says that the tip moves down, the way the load pushes. The size of the deflection is $\delta = PL^3/(3EI)$.

  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="Boundary Conditions">

    A boundary condition is a fact about the beam at a support. Each one fixes one constant.

    | At this place | Deflection $y$ | Slope $dy/dx$ | Moment $M$ |
    |--------------|--------------|-------------|----------|
    | **Pin or roller** | 0 | free | 0 |
    | **Fixed (clamped) end** | 0 | 0 | not 0: the support holds a moment |
    | **Free end** | free | free | 0 |

    - **Simply supported** (a pin and a roller): the beam cannot move up or down at either support, but it is free to tilt there. Use $y(0) = 0$ and $y(L) = 0$.
    - **Cantilever** (fixed at one end, free at the other): the fixed end cannot move and cannot tilt. Use $y(0) = 0$ and $dy/dx = 0$ at $x = 0$. The deflection is largest at the free end.
    - **Fixed-fixed** (clamped at both ends): neither end can move or tilt. The clamps hold moments that work against the sag, so this beam is the stiffest of the three. Equilibrium alone cannot give those moments, so use the ready formulas of the next tab.

  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="Standard Cases">

    These are the results of the double integration method for the six cases met most often. $P$ is a point load (N), $w$ is a uniform load on every metre of the beam (N/m), and $L$ is the span (m).

    <Card title="Standard deflection formulas" icon="document">

    | Supports | Point load $P$ | Uniform load $w$ | Where the beam sags most |
    |---------------|------------|--------------|------|
    | Cantilever | $\delta = \frac{PL^3}{3EI}$ (load at the tip) | $\delta = \frac{wL^4}{8EI}$ | at the free end |
    | Simply supported | $\delta = \frac{PL^3}{48EI}$ (load at the centre) | $\delta = \frac{5wL^4}{384EI}$ | at the centre |
    | Fixed-fixed | $\delta = \frac{PL^3}{192EI}$ (load at the centre) | $\delta = \frac{wL^4}{384EI}$ | at the centre |

    Three things to read from the table:

    1. **Length counts most.** The deflection grows with $L^3$ for a point load and with $L^4$ for a uniform load. Double the span and the beam sags 8 or 16 times as far.
    2. **$EI$ is in every denominator.** Double $E$ or $I$ and the deflection halves. For a rectangle $I = bh^3/12$, so double the depth $h$ and the deflection falls to one eighth.
    3. **The supports change the number in front.** For the same point load, span and beam, a simply supported beam sags 16 times less than a cantilever ($48/3$), and a fixed-fixed beam 4 times less again ($192/48$). For a uniform load, fixed-fixed sags 5 times less than simply supported.

    Use SI units (N, m, Pa, m⁴) and the answer is in metres.

    </Card>

  </TabItem>
</Tabs>

### Superposition principle

A real beam often carries several loads at once, such as its own weight and a load hung on it. No table has every combination, and none is needed.

<Card title="Superposition for beam deflections" icon="document">

In words: the deflection under several loads is the sum of the deflections that each load causes alone.

$$\delta_{total} = \delta_1 + \delta_2 + \delta_3 + \ldots$$

Example: a simply supported beam with a centre load $P$ and a uniform load $w$ sags at its centre by

$$\delta_{total} = \frac{PL^3}{48EI} + \frac{5wL^4}{384EI}$$

Three rules:
- Add deflections at the same point of the beam.
- A load that acts upward gives a deflection of the opposite sign.
- The sum is valid only while the beam stays elastic (no yielding) and the deflections stay small.

</Card>

:::tip[Instrument it]
A calculated deflection is a prediction until you measure it on the real beam. A dial indicator or an LVDT (a sensor that turns a small movement into a voltage) reads the deflection directly. A foil strain gauge bonded to the surface, read through an HX711 or an instrumentation amplifier, gives the strain and so the stress at that point. An [ESP32 or STM32](/education/sensor-actuator-interfacing-stm32/) reads the gauge and [logs it](https://siliconwit.io), so you can confirm that the beam behaves as the theory says before you trust it under load.
:::

## Application 1: PCB sagging in rack (electronics)

<InArticleAd />


A printed circuit board (PCB) is screwed down at two opposite edges in an electronics rack. The weight of the components on it makes it sag between the screws, and a board that bends strains its solder joints.

:::note[System Problem Statement]
- **Loading:** uniformly distributed load (UDL), the weight of the components spread along the board
- **Support:** fixed-fixed (both ends screwed down)
- **Find:** the largest deflection, what it means for the solder joints, and ways to stiffen the board

**Key Question:** How much does the PCB deflect under component weight, and what design changes can reduce sagging to protect solder joints?
:::

- PCB dimensions: length $L = 200\ \text{mm}$, width $b = 100\ \text{mm}$, thickness $h = 1.6\ \text{mm}$
- Total component weight: $W = 12\ \text{N}$, spread evenly along the span
- Load on each metre: $w = W/L = 12\ \text{N} / 0.2\ \text{m} = 60\ \text{N/m}$
- Material: FR4, the glass and epoxy laminate of most circuit boards ($E = 20\ \text{GPa}$)
- Second moment of area: $I = bh^3/12 = (100 \times 1.6^3)/12 = 34.13\ \text{mm}^4 = 3.413 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{m}^4$
- Supports: fixed at both ends (screwed down at $x = 0$ and $x = 200\ \text{mm}$)

### Step 1: Calculate maximum midspan deflection

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal deflection calculations**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Choose the formula.** The board is a fixed-fixed beam with a uniform load, so from the table of standard cases:

   $$\delta_{max} = \frac{wL^4}{384EI}$$

   A simply supported board would sag 5 times as far, $5wL^4/(384EI)$. The screws at both ends are what make this board stiff.

2. **Convert every value to SI units:**

   - $L = 200\ \text{mm} = 0.2\ \text{m}$
   - $w = 60\ \text{N/m}$ (the load on each metre, not the total weight of 12 N)
   - $E = 20\ \text{GPa} = 20 \times 10^9\ \text{Pa}$
   - $I = 3.413 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{m}^4$ ✅

3. **Put the values into the formula:**

   $$\delta_{max} = \frac{60 \times (0.2)^4}{384 \times 20 \times 10^9 \times 3.413 \times 10^{-11}}$$

   $$\delta_{max} = \frac{60 \times 0.0016}{384 \times 20 \times 10^9 \times 3.413 \times 10^{-11}}$$

   $$\delta_{max} = \frac{0.096}{262.12} = 0.000366 \text{ m} = 0.366 \text{ mm}$$ ✅

4. **Write the deflection as a fraction of the span,** which is how limits are stated:

   $$\frac{\delta_{max}}{L} = \frac{0.366}{200} = 0.00183 = \frac{L}{546}$$ ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 2: Relate deflection to solder joint reliability

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal solder joint analysis**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Find the limits.** Deflection limits for circuit boards are stated as a fraction of the span $L$:

   - **Usual guideline (IPC-2221):** less than L/100 for boards with components
   - **Cautious limit:** L/150 for boards with sensitive parts, such as ball grid arrays (BGAs) and fine-pitch chips
   - **Calculated deflection:** L/546 ✅

2. **Compare:**

   - Deflection now: $0.366\ \text{mm} = L/546$
   - Usual limit: $L/100 = 200/100 = 2.0\ \text{mm}$
   - Cautious limit: $L/150 = 200/150 = 1.33\ \text{mm}$
   - **Result:** well inside both limits ✅

   The margin is one minus the deflection divided by the limit:
   - against L/100: $1 - 0.366/2.0$ = 82% margin ✅
   - against L/150: $1 - 0.366/1.33$ = 72% margin ✅

3. **How a bent board damages solder joints:**

   - A curved board stretches the solder between the board and the stiff body of each component.
   - Heating and cooling in service repeat that stretch many times.
   - Repeated stretch starts small cracks in the solder.
   - The cracks grow, the electrical resistance of the joint rises, and the joint fails ✅

4. **Where the risk is highest:**

   - At the centre of the board, where the bending is largest
   - Under large or heavy components
   - At fine-pitch chips, whose solder joints are small
   - At BGAs, whose solder balls are hidden under the part and cannot be inspected ✅

5. **Risk at the present deflection (0.366 mm):**

   - ✅ Excellent for: through-hole components, whose wire leads can flex
   - ✅ Acceptable for: standard surface-mount components (resistors, capacitors, small chips)
   - ✅ Good for: BGAs and fine-pitch components
   - Review it for medical or aerospace boards, which may ask for L/200 or better

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 3: Suggest stiffening design modifications

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal design improvement strategies**</summary>

The board already passes. These four changes show what each one buys if a stricter limit applies.

<Steps>

1. **Strategy 1: a thicker board**

   The deflection is proportional to $1/I$, and $I = bh^3/12$ grows with the cube of the thickness. With the standard 2.0 mm board:

   $$I_{new} = \frac{100 \times (2.0)^3}{12} = 66.67 \text{ mm}^4$$

   $$\delta_{new} = \delta_{old} \times \frac{I_{old}}{I_{new}} = 0.366 \times \frac{34.13}{66.67} = 0.187 \text{ mm}$$ ✅

   The deflection falls by 49%, to L/1070.

   - ✅ Simple, and 2.0 mm is a standard thickness
   - ✅ Gives extra margin for high-reliability boards
   - Cost: about 25% more material and 25% more weight

2. **Strategy 2: a support at the centre**

   A support at the centre makes two spans of 100 mm. The deflection goes with $L^4$, so half the span gives one sixteenth of the deflection:

   $$\delta_{new} = \frac{wL^4}{384EI} \text{ with } L = 0.1 \text{ m}$$

   $$\delta_{new} = \frac{60 \times (0.1)^4}{384 \times 20 \times 10^9 \times 3.413 \times 10^{-11}}$$

   $$\delta_{new} = \frac{0.006}{262.12} = 0.0000229 \text{ m} = 0.0229 \text{ mm}$$ ✅

   The deflection falls by 93.8%, to L/4,370 on each 100 mm span. The support can be one more standoff at the centre, or a metal rail bonded under the board.

   - ✅ The most effective of the four
   - ✅ No change to the board material or thickness
   - Cost: the rack must be redesigned, and assembly has one more step

3. **Strategy 3: stiffener strips**

   Bond an aluminum strip, 2 mm thick and 10 mm wide, along each edge of the board, parallel to the span. Aluminum has $E_{\text{al}} = 70\ \text{GPa}$. A simple estimate replaces each strip by a wider strip of FR4 with the same stiffness:

   - Ratio of the two moduli: $n = E_{\text{al}} / E_{\text{FR4}} = 70/20 = 3.5$
   - Equal width of FR4: $10 \times 3.5 = 35\ \text{mm}$ on each side
   - Effective width: $100 + 2(35) = 170\ \text{mm}$

   $$I_{composite} \approx \frac{170 \times 1.6^3}{12} = 58.0 \text{ mm}^4$$

   $$\delta_{new} = 0.366 \times \frac{34.13}{58.0} = 0.215 \text{ mm}$$ ✅

   The deflection falls by 41%, to L/930.

   - ✅ A moderate gain without a new design
   - ✅ Can be added to an existing board
   - Cost: a little more weight, and aluminum and FR4 expand by different amounts when they warm

4. **Strategy 4: move the heavy components**

   Place heavy components (transformers, heat sinks, connectors) near the screws and light ones (resistors, capacitors) near the centre. Less of the load then sits where the board sags most. The gain depends on the layout, and is about 20 to 30%.

   - ✅ No change to the material or the structure
   - ✅ Costs nothing
   - Limit: the circuit routing and the cooling decide where many parts must go

5. **Recommendation:**

   - Deflection now: 0.366 mm = L/546
   - **The fixed-fixed mounting already makes this board stiff enough** ✅
   - It meets L/100 with an 82% margin and L/150 with a 72% margin

   Keep the design. If a limit of L/200 or better is required, use the 2.0 mm board ($\delta = 0.187\ \text{mm}$) or the edge strips ($\delta = 0.215\ \text{mm}$).

</Steps>

</details>

## Application 2: Mobile C-arm fluoroscopy system (mechatronics/medical)

<InArticleAd />


A mobile C-arm is an X-ray machine used during surgery. Fluoroscopy means live X-ray video. A C-shaped arm reaches out from a column and holds the X-ray source at one end and the detector at the other. The weight of the two bends the arm, and if the arm droops, the source and the detector no longer line up and the image is blurred.

:::note[System Problem Statement]
- **Loading:** one load at the tip, the weight of the X-ray source and the detector
- **Support:** cantilever (fixed at the column, free at the detector end)
- **Find:** the deflection of the tip, what imaging needs, and a deflection limit that fits this machine

**Key Question:** Does the cantilever arm meet stiffness requirements for sub-millimeter imaging precision during surgical procedures, and what design improvements are needed?
:::

### Step 1: Calculate tip deflection

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal tip deflection calculations**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Choose the formula.** The arm is a cantilever with a point load on its free end, so from the table of standard cases:

   $$\delta_{tip} = \frac{PL^3}{3EI}$$

2. **Calculate the second moment of area of the round tube:**

   $$I = \frac{\pi}{64}(OD^4 - ID^4) = \frac{\pi}{64}(120^4 - 104^4) \text{ mm}^4$$

   $$I = \frac{\pi}{64}(207{,}360{,}000 - 116{,}985{,}856) = \frac{\pi \times 90{,}374{,}144}{64}$$

   $$I = 4{,}436{,}230 \text{ mm}^4 = 4.44 \times 10^6 \text{ mm}^4$$ ✅

3. **Convert every value to SI units:**

   - $P = 800\ \text{N}$
   - $L = 1.2\ \text{m}$
   - $E = 210\ \text{GPa} = 210 \times 10^9\ \text{Pa}$
   - $I = 4.44 \times 10^6\ \text{mm}^4 = 4.44 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{m}^4$ ✅

4. **Put the values into the formula:**

   $$\delta_{tip} = \frac{800 \times (1.2)^3}{3 \times 210 \times 10^9 \times 4.44 \times 10^{-6}}$$

   $$\delta_{tip} = \frac{800 \times 1.728}{3 \times 210 \times 10^9 \times 4.44 \times 10^{-6}}$$

   $$\delta_{tip} = \frac{1382.4}{2{,}797{,}200} = 0.000494 \text{ m} = 0.494 \text{ mm}$$ ✅

5. **Write the deflection as a fraction of the length:**

   $$\frac{\delta_{tip}}{L} = \frac{0.494}{1200} = 0.000412 = \frac{L}{2429}$$ ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 2: Comment on stiffness requirements for imaging precision

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal imaging precision analysis**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **What a C-arm needs:**

   - **Detail it must show:** 1.0 to 2.0 mm (bones, instruments, contrast dye)
   - **Alignment of the source and the detector:** within ±1.0 mm
   - **Detector pixel size:** 0.2 to 0.4 mm

   A C-arm makes flat, two-dimensional images, so it needs less precision than a CT scanner, which builds three-dimensional images ✅

2. **Why a straight cantilever is a fair model.** The arm is curved, but its droop at the detector is set mostly by the distance from the column to the detector and by the stiffness of the section. A straight beam of the same length and section gives nearly the same tip deflection ✅

3. **Add the other sources of position error.** The droop of the arm is one of four errors, and the four do not all peak at the same moment. The usual estimate of the total is the square root of the sum of their squares:

   - Deflection of the arm (static): 0.494 mm (calculated) ✅
   - Play in the bearings and the track: ±0.3 mm
   - Thermal expansion in a long procedure: ±0.2 mm
   - Vibration from the motors and the floor: ±0.15 mm
   - **Total error:** $\sqrt{0.494^2 + 0.3^2 + 0.2^2 + 0.15^2} = 0.63\ \text{mm}$

4. **Compare with the requirement:**

   - Required alignment of source and detector: ±1.0 mm
   - Calculated total error: 0.63 mm
   - **Margin:** $1 - 0.63/1.0$ = 37% below the requirement ✅

   The arm is also moved during a procedure, and a moving load can raise the deflection by a factor of 1.5 to 2:
   - Deflection with a factor of 1.5: $0.494 \times 1.5 = 0.74\ \text{mm}$
   - Total error with that deflection: about 0.84 mm, which leaves a margin of 16%

   What this means for use:
   - ✅ Acceptable for: bone surgery, blood vessel imaging, general fluoroscopy
   - Marginal for: work on blood vessels of the brain, which needs high precision
   - Not enough for: heart catheter laboratories, which need less than 0.3 mm of deflection

5. **Stiffness sizes this arm, not strength:**

   - The bending stress is about 13 MPa, far below a yield strength of 350 MPa or more.
   - The deflection decides: the source and the detector must stay within 1 mm.
   - **The design is limited by stiffness, not by strength** ✅

6. **Option 1 for a target of 0.25 mm: a larger tube**

   - The deflection is proportional to $1/I$, so: $I_{\text{needed}} = 0.494/0.25 \times 4.44 \times 10^{-6} = 8.77 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{m}^4$
   - A tube with $t=8\ \text{mm}$ reaches this at $\text{OD} \approx 149\ \text{mm}$
   - **Result:** $\delta$ falls to 0.25 mm ✅
   - **Cost:** 24% larger diameter, about 26% heavier

7. **Option 2: a thicker wall**

   - Keep $\text{OD} = 120\ \text{mm}$ and make the wall $t = 12\ \text{mm}$ ($\text{ID} = 96\ \text{mm}$)
   - $I = \pi(120^4-96^4)/64 = 6.01 \times 10^6\ \text{mm}^4$
   - **Result:** $\delta$ falls to $0.494 \times 4.44/6.01 = 0.37\ \text{mm}$ ✅
   - **Cost:** 45% heavier, less room inside for cables, and still above the target

8. **Option 3: a counterweight**

   - Balance 63% of the load, so the net tip load falls from 800 N to about 296 N
   - **Result:** $\delta$ falls to $0.494 \times 296/800 = 0.18\ \text{mm}$ ✅
   - **Cost:** a heavier machine and larger motor torques

9. **Option 4: carbon fiber composite**

   - Carbon fiber in epoxy: $E = 140\ \text{GPa}$ (67% of steel), density $\rho = 1600\ \text{kg/m}^3$ (20% of steel)
   - With $\text{OD} = 140\ \text{mm}$, $t = 10\ \text{mm}$: $I = 8.68 \times 10^6\ \text{mm}^4$
   - **Result:** $\delta$ falls to 0.38 mm, and the arm is 70% lighter ✅
   - **Cost:** a higher price, and still above the target

   For general fluoroscopy the present arm is adequate. For heart and brain work, the 149 mm tube or the counterweight reaches the 0.25 mm target.

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 3: Establish allowable deflection limits for medical imaging

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal allowable deflection analysis**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **The L/250 rule.** Building codes limit the deflection of a beam to a fraction of its span $L$:

   - **L/360:** floors and ceilings with brittle finishes, such as plaster
   - **L/250:** general beams and floor joists
   - **L/180:** roof beams with flexible finishes

   L/250 is a **general guideline** that keeps a sag from being seen or from damaging a finish ✅

2. **Apply L/250 to the C-arm:**

   $$\delta_{allowable} = \frac{L}{250} = \frac{1200\ \text{mm}}{250} = 4.8 \text{ mm}$$ ✅

3. **Compare:**

   - Calculated deflection: 0.494 mm
   - L/250 limit: 4.8 mm
   - **Ratio:** $0.494 / 4.8 = 0.103$ (10.3% of the limit)
   - **The arm passes the L/250 rule easily** ✅

4. **Is L/250 the right limit for a C-arm?**

   No. L/250 protects buildings from visible sag. A C-arm must keep an X-ray source lined up with a detector, and a droop of 4.8 mm would blur and distort the image.

   - **A C-arm needs its own limit, between 0.5 and 1.0 mm** ✅

5. **Limits that fit the three kinds of use:**

   **High-precision work on the heart and the brain:**
   - Target: L/4000 to L/6000 = 0.20 to 0.30 mm
   - Maximum: $L/2400 = 0.50\ \text{mm}$
   - This design: 0.494 mm, which is **inside the maximum by only 1% and misses the target**

   **Standard surgical fluoroscopy:**
   - Target: $L/2000 = 0.60\ \text{mm}$
   - Maximum: $L/1200 = 1.0\ \text{mm}$
   - This design: 0.494 mm, which is **acceptable (51% margin)** ✅

   **General bone and injury imaging:**
   - Target: $L/1200 = 1.0\ \text{mm}$
   - Maximum: $L/800 = 1.5\ \text{mm}$
   - This design: 0.494 mm, which is **excellent (67% margin)** ✅

6. **Summary:**

   | Use | Target | Maximum | Calculated | Result |
   |------------------|--------------|---------------|------------|--------|
   | L/250 structural rule | none | 4.8 mm | 0.494 mm | ✅ Pass (90% margin) |
   | High-precision heart and brain work | 0.25 mm | 0.50 mm | 0.494 mm | Fail (misses the target, 1% inside the maximum) |
   | Standard surgical | 0.60 mm | 1.0 mm | 0.494 mm | ✅ Good (51% margin) |
   | General bone imaging | 1.0 mm | 1.5 mm | 0.494 mm | ✅ Excellent (67% margin) |

   The arm is adequate for general surgical and bone imaging. It fails the requirement for high-precision work on the heart and the brain.

7. **What to do:**

   **For general surgical use:**
   - The present $\delta = 0.494\ \text{mm}$ meets the requirement ✅
   - Keep 20 to 30% of margin for moving loads
   - Check the deflection during the life of the machine, because worn bearings add to it

   **For high-precision use,** the target is less than 0.25 mm, about half of the present deflection:
   - **Option 1:** a tube with an OD of 149 mm ($t=8\ \text{mm}$) gives $\delta = 0.25\ \text{mm}$ ✅
   - **Option 2:** a wall of 12 mm gives $\delta = 0.37\ \text{mm}$, which is not enough
   - **Option 3:** a counterweight for 63% of the load gives $\delta = 0.18\ \text{mm}$ ✅

   A deflection limit comes from the job of the machine. An arm for bone surgery can droop 1.0 mm, and an arm for heart work must stay below 0.3 mm.

</Steps>

</details>

## Application 3: CNC machine gantry rail (mechatronics)

<InArticleAd />


A CNC (computer numerical control) router or laser cutter moves its tool on a gantry, a bridge that rides on a rail. The rail is held at its two ends. The weight of the gantry bends it, and every bit of sag moves the tool away from where the program expects it.

:::note[System Problem Statement]
- **Loading:** one point load at the centre, the gantry at midspan, which is the position that sags most
- **Support:** simply supported (a pin and a roller) at the two ends
- **Find:** the largest deflection, the same rail on other supports, and ways to stiffen it

**Key Question:** Does the simply supported rail meet precision requirements for CNC machining, and how do different support configurations affect positioning accuracy?
:::

  </TailwindWrapper>
</div>
**Given** (the picture labels the load P1; the text calls it $P$):
- Rail length (span): $L = 1.0\ \text{m}$
- Point load at the centre: $P = 500\ \text{N}$ (gantry: motors, linear guides and tool head)
- Cross-section: rectangular steel rail
  - Width: $b = 80\ \text{mm}$
  - Height: $h = 30\ \text{mm}$
  - Area: $A = 2400\ \text{mm}^2$
- Material: steel ($E = 210\ \text{GPa}$)

</Card>

### Step 1: Calculate maximum midspan deflection

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal maximum deflection calculations**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Choose the formula.** The rail is a simply supported beam with a point load at its centre, so from the table of standard cases:

   $$\delta_{max} = \frac{PL^3}{48EI}$$

   The deflection is largest at midspan ($x = L/2$), under the load ✅

2. **Calculate the second moment of area of the rectangle:**

   $$I = \frac{bh^3}{12} = \frac{80 \times (30)^3}{12} = \frac{80 \times 27{,}000}{12} = 180{,}000 \text{ mm}^4$$

   $$I = 1.80 \times 10^{-7} \text{ m}^4$$ ✅

3. **List every value in SI units:**

   - $P = 500\ \text{N}$
   - $L = 1.0\ \text{m}$
   - $E = 210\ \text{GPa} = 210 \times 10^9\ \text{Pa}$
   - $I = 1.80 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^4$ ✅

4. **Put the values into the formula:**

   $$\delta_{max} = \frac{500 \times (1.0)^3}{48 \times 210 \times 10^9 \times 1.80 \times 10^{-7}}$$

   $$\delta_{max} = \frac{500}{1{,}814{,}400} = 0.0002756 \text{ m} = 0.276 \text{ mm}$$ ✅

5. **Write the deflection as a fraction of the span:**

   $$\frac{\delta_{max}}{L} = \frac{0.276}{1000} = 0.000276 = \frac{L}{3623}$$ ✅

   A usual limit for CNC machines is L/1000, which is 1.0 mm for this rail. The rail is well inside it.

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 2: Compare with other support configurations

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal support configuration comparison analysis**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **The same rail and load, clamped at both ends (fixed-fixed).** From the table of standard cases:

   $$\delta_{fixed} = \frac{PL^3}{192EI}$$

   $$\delta_{fixed} = \frac{500 \times (1.0)^3}{192 \times 210 \times 10^9 \times 1.80 \times 10^{-7}}$$

   $$\delta_{fixed} = \frac{500}{7{,}257{,}600} = 0.0000689 \text{ m} = 0.069 \text{ mm}$$ ✅

2. **The same rail as a cantilever, fixed at one end with the load on the free tip:**

   $$\delta_{cantilever} = \frac{PL^3}{3EI}$$

   $$\delta_{cantilever} = \frac{500 \times (1.0)^3}{3 \times 210 \times 10^9 \times 1.80 \times 10^{-7}}$$

   $$\delta_{cantilever} = \frac{500}{113{,}400} = 0.00441 \text{ m} = 4.41 \text{ mm}$$ ✅

3. **Put the three side by side:**

   | Supports | Formula | Deflection | Against simply supported |
   |---------------|--------------------|-----------------------|--------------------|
   | **Fixed-fixed** | $PL^3/(192EI)$ | **0.069 mm** | **4.0 times stiffer** ✅ |
   | **Simply supported** | $PL^3/(48EI)$ | **0.276 mm** | **1.0** ✅ |
   | **Cantilever** | $PL^3/(3EI)$ | 4.41 mm | 16 times more flexible |

4. **Why the supports change the stiffness so much:**

   **Fixed-fixed beam:**
   - ✅ **No rotation** at the supports
   - ✅ **No movement** at the supports
   - ✅ **The clamps hold moments** that work against the sag
   - ✅ **The moment at midspan is smaller** because of them
   - Result: **4 times stiffer** than simply supported ✅

   **Simply supported beam (this design):**
   - ✅ No movement up or down at the supports
   - The ends are free to rotate, so nothing works against the sag
   - ✅ The roller end can slide, so the rail can grow when it warms
   - ✅ Easier to make and to assemble
   - Result: **the reference stiffness**, a good balance for a real machine ✅

   **Cantilever beam:**
   - ✅ No rotation or movement at the fixed end
   - The free end is not held at all, and the whole load bends the beam about the fixed end
   - Result: **16 times more flexible** than simply supported ✅

5. **Check the ratios against the formulas.** The three formulas differ only in the number in the denominator:

   **Simply supported against fixed-fixed:**
   - $\frac{PL^3}{48EI}$ against $\frac{PL^3}{192EI}$
   - **Ratio of the numbers: 192:48 = 4:1** ✅

   **Simply supported against cantilever:**
   - $\frac{PL^3}{48EI}$ against $\frac{PL^3}{3EI}$
   - **Ratio of the numbers: 48:3 = 16:1** ✅

6. **What this means for a CNC gantry:**

   Simply supported is the usual choice:
   - The roller end lets the rail expand with heat without stress.
   - Standard rail mounting blocks give this kind of support, and it is simple to assemble.
   - **It is 16 times stiffer than a cantilever, enough for most CNC work** ✅

   The other two:
   - **Fixed-fixed:** for the highest stiffness. It needs rigid clamps at both ends, and the design must allow for thermal stress.
   - **Cantilever:** only when the rail can be held at one end. It is 16 times more flexible.
   - **Simply supported:** the standard choice for most CNC machines ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 3: Discuss stiffness improvement strategies

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal stiffness enhancement strategies**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Strategy 1: a taller rail**

   The deflection is proportional to $1/I$, and $I = bh^3/12$ grows with the cube of the height. Raise the height from 30 mm to 40 mm:

   $$I_{new} = \frac{80 \times (40)^3}{12} = 426{,}667 \text{ mm}^4$$

   $$\delta_{new} = \delta_{old} \times \frac{I_{old}}{I_{new}} = 0.276 \times \frac{180{,}000}{426{,}667} = 0.116 \text{ mm}$$ ✅

   The deflection falls by 58%, from 0.276 mm to 0.116 mm.

   - ✅ **The largest gain** for each kilogram of added steel
   - ✅ Also raises the section modulus, so the rail is stronger in bending
   - Cost: 33% more weight (30 mm to 40 mm), and the guide blocks may need to be taller
   - ✅ **Recommended for high-precision CNC machining** ✅

2. **Strategy 2: an aluminum extrusion, to save weight**

   Aluminum 6061-T6 has $E = 70\ \text{GPa}$, one third of steel. A larger section, 100 mm × 40 mm, makes up for it:

   $$I_{al} = \frac{100 \times (40)^3}{12} = 533{,}333 \text{ mm}^4$$

   $$\delta_{al} = \frac{500 \times (1.0)^3}{48 \times 70 \times 10^9 \times 5.33 \times 10^{-7}} = 0.000279 \text{ m} = 0.279 \text{ mm}$$ ✅

   The deflection is almost the same (0.279 mm against 0.276 mm), and the rail is 43% lighter.

   - ✅ **43% less weight**, which matters for a part that moves
   - ✅ Less mass to speed up and slow down, so less motor torque
   - ✅ Easy to machine, and standard profiles exist
   - Cost: aluminum wears faster than steel, so the rail mounts may need hardened inserts
   - ✅ **Recommended for high-speed CNC routers** where weight matters ✅

3. **Strategy 3: clamp both ends (fixed-fixed)**

   Step 2 gave $\delta_{fixed} = 0.069$ mm. The deflection falls by 75%, from 0.276 mm to 0.069 mm.

   - ✅ **Four times the stiffness** with no new material
   - Cost: rigid clamps at both ends, careful alignment, and a problem with heat

   The heat problem is why CNC machines avoid this:
   - Motors, cutting and coolant warm and cool the machine all day.
   - Steel grows about 12 μm for each metre and each °C, so a 1 m rail grows 120 μm for a change of 10 °C.
   - A rail clamped at both ends cannot grow, so it pushes on the frame and distorts it.
   - **A simply supported rail with a roller end is free to grow** ✅

4. **Strategy 4: a support at the centre**

   A support at the centre makes two spans of 0.5 m. The deflection goes with $L^3$:

   $$\delta_{half} = \frac{P \times (0.5)^3}{48EI} = 0.276 \times (0.5)^3 = 0.276 \times 0.125 = 0.034 \text{ mm}$$ ✅

   The deflection falls by 88%, from 0.276 mm to 0.034 mm.

   - ✅ **The largest gain** with no change to the rail
   - ✅ No change to the material or the cross-section
   - Cost: a post or beam under the centre, which blocks the work area. For most CNC machines this is not practical.

5. **Recommendation:**

   **For general CNC routing and laser cutting:**
   - The present $\delta = 0.276\ \text{mm}$ meets the usual L/1000 limit (it reaches L/3623) ✅
   - Make sure the supports are rigid: correct bolt torque and flat mounting faces

   **For high-precision machining (target less than 0.15 mm):**
   - **Best:** raise the rail height to 40 mm, which gives $\delta = 0.116\ \text{mm}$ ✅
   - **For high speed:** the 100×40 mm aluminum extrusion, with $\delta = 0.279\ \text{mm}$ and 43% less weight
   - Avoid fixed-fixed (heat) and a centre support (it blocks the work area)

6. **Which rail for which job:**

   | Job | Target δ | Rail | Reason |
   |-------------|----------|---------------------|-----------|
   | **General CNC routing** | less than 1.0 mm (L/1000) | Present design (80×30 mm steel) ✅ | Stiff enough, low cost |
   | **High-precision milling** | less than 0.15 mm | 80×40 mm steel ✅ | 2.4 times stiffer, small added cost |
   | **High-speed routing** | less than 0.3 mm | Aluminum extrusion 100×40 mm ✅ | Same stiffness, 43% lighter |
   | **Ultra-precision** | less than 0.1 mm | 80×40 mm with guides on both sides | Needs a new design |

7. **Why simply supported is the standard:**

   - ✅ The rail can expand with heat
   - ✅ Easy to assemble
   - ✅ Stiff enough: 16 times stiffer than a cantilever
   - ✅ Fits standard linear rail mounts

   More stiffness is worth its cost for hard materials (high cutting forces), fine surface finish and tolerances of ±0.01 mm or better.

</Steps>

</details>

:::tip[Instrument it]
On a machine like this CNC gantry, deflection is not checked once: it sets the tolerance you can hold on every cut. Mount a dial gauge or, better, a displacement probe that does not touch the rail (capacitive or eddy-current) and watch the rail deflect under a real cutting load. An [ESP32 or STM32](/education/sensor-actuator-interfacing-stm32/) reads the probe and [streams](/education/iot-systems/) it to a [live trend](https://siliconwit.io), so you can compare the sag with the feed rate and the depth of cut and choose the settings that hold your tolerance.
:::

## Summary and next steps

<InArticleAd />


- A beam can be strong enough and still not stiff enough. Stiffness is checked with the deflection $\delta$, against a limit that comes from the job of the machine.
- The deflection is the load multiplied by $L^3$ (point load) or $L^4$ (uniform load), divided by $EI$ and by a number that the supports set.
- For the same point load and beam: cantilever 3, simply supported 48, fixed-fixed 192. A cantilever sags 64 times as far as a fixed-fixed beam.
- The cheapest stiffness is a shorter span, then a deeper section ($I$ grows with $h^3$). A stronger material does not help: only $E$ counts.

The three applications gave: a PCB at 0.366 mm (L/546), a C-arm at 0.494 mm (1% inside its 0.50 mm limit for heart work), and a CNC rail at 0.276 mm (L/3623).
