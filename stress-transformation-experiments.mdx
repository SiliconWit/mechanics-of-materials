A shaft is checked for the stress along its axis, passes, and then cracks on a plane at forty-five degrees that nobody calculated. The stress at a point depends on the plane you look at: a small square element drawn one way shows only tension, and the same element turned by forty-five degrees can show only shear. The part fails on the worst plane, not on the plane of your drawing. Mohr's circle shows the stress on every plane through the point. In these six experiments you build the circle, turn the element, find the planes that matter and let two failure criteria judge the part. #MohrsCircle #StressTransformation #FailureAnalysis

:::tip[What you need]
The [Mohr's Circle Simulator](/product-development/mohrs-circle-simulator/), and Python 3 with NumPy and Matplotlib for the analysis scripts (`pip install numpy matplotlib`).

The simulator sets each stress in steps of 5 MPa and the element angle $\theta$ from 0 to 180 degrees in steps of 1 degree, and shows stresses to 0.1 MPa. A script value and a simulator value can differ in the last digit.
:::

## Reference

All stresses are in megapascals (MPa) and all angles in degrees.

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Plane stress** | Two normal stresses $\sigma_x$, $\sigma_y$ and one shear stress $\tau_{xy}$, with no stress out of the plane. It is true on any free surface |
| **Stress transformation** | The stresses $(\sigma_{x'}, \sigma_{y'}, \tau_{x'y'})$ on an element turned by the angle $\theta$ from the reference axes |
| **Principal stresses** | The largest and smallest normal stresses $\sigma_1$, $\sigma_2$, on the planes where the shear stress is zero |
| **Principal angle ($\theta_p$)** | The rotation from the x-axis to the plane that carries $\sigma_1$ |
| **Maximum in-plane shear** | $\tau_{\max}$, the radius of the circle, on planes forty-five degrees from the principal planes |
| **Mohr's circle** | A circle with centre $(\sigma_{\text{avg}}, 0)$ and radius $R$. Each point is the pair $(\sigma, \tau)$ on one plane through the point |
| **von Mises / Tresca** | Two yield criteria. Each gives one equivalent stress to compare with the yield strength |

**Shear sign convention.** In this lesson and in the simulator, $\tau_{xy}$ is positive when the shear stress on the positive x-face points in the positive y direction, and the element angle $\theta$ is positive counterclockwise. The circle is drawn with $\tau$ positive upward, and the rotated x-face is plotted as the point $(\sigma_{x'}, \tau_{x'y'})$. At $\theta = 0$ the x-face is therefore the point $(\sigma_x, +\tau_{xy})$ and the y-face is the point $(\sigma_y, -\tau_{xy})$. With this convention, turning the element counterclockwise by $\theta$ moves the point clockwise by $2\theta$ around the circle. Some textbooks draw $\tau$ positive downward. Their circle is the mirror image of this one, and the principal stresses and $\tau_{\max}$ are the same.

### How every experiment runs

Each experiment is one short sitting: set the stress state in the simulator, predict by hand, read the simulator, record the values in a CSV file, run the Python script, and compare the three.

## Experiment 1: Build Mohr's circle from a stress state

<InArticleAd />


A stress report gives three numbers for a point: $\sigma_x$, $\sigma_y$ and $\tau_{xy}$. None of them is the largest stress at the point. To find it, turn the three numbers into a circle and read where it crosses the horizontal axis.

:::note[Objective]
For a general plane-stress state, compute the circle centre and radius, the principal stresses $\sigma_1$ and $\sigma_2$, and the principal angle $\theta_p$, and confirm them against the simulator's diagram and readouts.
:::

<Steps>
1. **Configure the simulator**
   Open the simulator and select the General plane stress preset ($\sigma_x = 80$, $\sigma_y = -20$, $\tau_{xy} = 40\ \text{MPa}$). Leave the element angle $\theta$ at 0.

2. **Predict**
   Before reading the panel, compute by hand. The centre is the average of the two normal stresses, $\sigma_{\text{avg}} = (\sigma_x + \sigma_y)/2$. The radius is the distance from the centre to the x-face point, $R = \sqrt{((\sigma_x - \sigma_y)/2)^2 + \tau_{xy}^2}$. Then $\sigma_{1,2} = \sigma_{\text{avg}} \pm R$, and $\theta_p$ follows from $\tan(2\theta_p) = 2\tau_{xy}/(\sigma_x - \sigma_y)$.

3. **Read the circle**
   On the diagram, confirm the circle centre sits at $\sigma_{\text{avg}}$ on the horizontal axis and that the circle crosses the axis at your $\sigma_1$ and $\sigma_2$. Read $\sigma_1$, $\sigma_2$, and $\theta_p$ from the panel.

4. **Change the state**
   Switch to Custom and try two more states of your own. Each time, predict first, then check.

5. **Save data**
   Create `data/exp1_circle.csv` with columns: sx, sy, txy, sigma_avg, R, sigma_1, sigma_2, theta_p_deg.
</Steps>

### Python analysis

```python title="experiment_1_circle.py"
# Stress Transformation Experiment 1: build Mohr's circle from a state
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import os

os.makedirs('plots', exist_ok=True)

def mohr(sx, sy, txy):
    avg = (sx + sy) / 2.0
    R = np.hypot((sx - sy) / 2.0, txy)
    s1, s2 = avg + R, avg - R
    theta_p = 0.5 * np.degrees(np.arctan2(2 * txy, sx - sy))
    return avg, R, s1, s2, theta_p

sx, sy, txy = 80.0, -20.0, 40.0
avg, R, s1, s2, theta_p = mohr(sx, sy, txy)
print(f"centre sigma_avg = {avg:.2f} MPa, radius R = {R:.2f} MPa")
print(f"sigma_1 = {s1:.2f} MPa, sigma_2 = {s2:.2f} MPa, theta_p = {theta_p:.2f} deg")

# Draw the circle and mark the two reference faces (x-face and y-face).
phi = np.linspace(0, 2 * np.pi, 400)
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 7))
ax.plot(avg + R * np.cos(phi), R * np.sin(phi), color='#2A9D8F', lw=2)
ax.plot([sx, sy], [txy, -txy], 'o-', color='#E76F51', lw=1.5, label='x-face / y-face (diameter)')
ax.plot([s1, s2], [0, 0], 's', color='#264653', label='principal stresses')
ax.axhline(0, color='gray', lw=0.8); ax.axvline(0, color='gray', lw=0.8)
ax.set_aspect('equal'); ax.grid(True, alpha=0.3)
ax.set_xlabel('normal stress sigma (MPa)'); ax.set_ylabel('shear stress tau (MPa)')
ax.set_title("Mohr's circle for the stress state"); ax.legend(fontsize=8)
plt.tight_layout(); plt.savefig('plots/exp1_circle.png', dpi=150, bbox_inches='tight'); plt.show()
```

### Expected results

For $(80, -20, 40)$: $\sigma_{\text{avg}} = 30.0\ \text{MPa}$, $R = 64.0\ \text{MPa}$, $\sigma_1 = 94.0\ \text{MPa}$, $\sigma_2 = -34.0\ \text{MPa}$, $\theta_p = 19.33$ deg (the simulator shows 19.3). The x-face point $(80, +40)$ and the y-face point $(-20, -40)$ sit at opposite ends of a diameter, and the circle crosses the axis exactly at $\sigma_1$ and $\sigma_2$.

As a check, $\sigma_1 + \sigma_2$ must equal $\sigma_x + \sigma_y$: $94.0 + (-34.0) = 60 = 80 + (-20)$.

### Design question

If you doubled only $\tau_{xy}$, would $\sigma_1$ grow faster or slower than the increase in $\tau_{xy}$? Use the $R$ expression to argue why a state already dominated by shear is less sensitive to extra shear than a state dominated by normal stress.

## Experiment 2: Stress on an inclined plane

<InArticleAd />


A weld seam or a glued joint lies at its own angle in the part, and the stress on that plane decides if it holds. This experiment turns the element through a quarter turn and confirms that the face stress never leaves the circle.

The two transformation equations give the normal and the shear stress on the face turned by $\theta$. Both swing with twice the angle:

$$\sigma_{x'} = \sigma_{\text{avg}} + \frac{\sigma_x - \sigma_y}{2}\cos 2\theta + \tau_{xy}\sin 2\theta$$

$$\tau_{x'y'} = -\frac{\sigma_x - \sigma_y}{2}\sin 2\theta + \tau_{xy}\cos 2\theta$$

:::note[Objective]
Rotate the stress element and verify that the pair $(\sigma_{x'}, \tau_{x'y'})$ for every angle $\theta$ lies on the Mohr circle, and that $\sigma_{x'}(\theta)$ and $\tau_{x'y'}(\theta)$ follow the two equations above.
:::

<Steps>
1. **Configure the simulator**
   Keep the General plane stress preset. Open the Normal stress vs angle and Shear stress vs angle charts.

2. **Predict**
   The normal stress should peak at $\theta = \theta_p$ and the shear should cross zero there. The shear should peak forty-five degrees later. Predict the angle at which $\sigma_{x'}$ equals $\sigma_{\text{avg}}$.

3. **Sweep the angle**
   Drag $\theta$ from 0 to 90 degrees in steps of 15. At each step record $\sigma_{x'}$ and $\tau_{x'y'}$ from the readout.

4. **Check the circle**
   For each recorded pair, the distance from $(\sigma_{\text{avg}}, 0)$ should equal $R$. Confirm a few by hand.

5. **Save data**
   Create `data/exp2_incline.csv` with columns: theta_deg, sigma_xprime, tau_xprime.
</Steps>

### Python analysis

```python title="experiment_2_incline.py"
# Stress Transformation Experiment 2: stress on an inclined plane
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import os

os.makedirs('plots', exist_ok=True)

sx, sy, txy = 80.0, -20.0, 40.0
avg = (sx + sy) / 2.0
d = (sx - sy) / 2.0
R = np.hypot(d, txy)

theta = np.linspace(0, 180, 361)
t = np.radians(theta)
sxp = avg + d * np.cos(2 * t) + txy * np.sin(2 * t)
txp = -d * np.sin(2 * t) + txy * np.cos(2 * t)

# Every point must lie on the circle: distance from (avg, 0) equals R.
dist = np.hypot(sxp - avg, txp)
print(f"R = {R:.3f} MPa; max deviation of points from the circle = {np.max(np.abs(dist - R)):.2e} MPa")

fig, ax = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(13, 5))
ax[0].plot(theta, sxp, color='#2A9D8F', lw=2, label="sigma_x'")
ax[0].plot(theta, txp, color='#E76F51', lw=2, label="tau_x'y'")
ax[0].axhline(avg, color='gray', ls='--', lw=1, label='sigma_avg')
ax[0].set_xlabel('element angle theta (deg)'); ax[0].set_ylabel('stress (MPa)')
ax[0].grid(True, alpha=0.3); ax[0].legend(fontsize=8); ax[0].set_title('Face stresses vs rotation')

ax[1].plot(avg + R * np.cos(np.linspace(0, 2*np.pi, 400)),
           R * np.sin(np.linspace(0, 2*np.pi, 400)), color='#999999', lw=1)
ax[1].scatter(sxp[::20], txp[::20], c=theta[::20], cmap='viridis', s=30)
ax[1].set_aspect('equal'); ax[1].grid(True, alpha=0.3)
ax[1].set_xlabel('sigma (MPa)'); ax[1].set_ylabel('tau (MPa)'); ax[1].set_title('The points ride the circle')
plt.tight_layout(); plt.savefig('plots/exp2_incline.png', dpi=150, bbox_inches='tight'); plt.show()
```

### Expected results

The script prints a largest distance between the points and the circle of about $10^{-14}\ \text{MPa}$, which is zero within the rounding of the computer. $\sigma_{x'}(\theta)$ is a sine curve centred on $\sigma_{\text{avg}} = 30\ \text{MPa}$ with amplitude $R = 64\ \text{MPa}$. It peaks at $\theta_p = 19.33$ deg, exactly where $\tau_{x'y'}$ crosses zero.

Your table must read 70.0 and $-50.0$ MPa at 45 degrees, and $-20.0$ and $-40.0$ MPa at 90 degrees, where the x-face has taken the place of the y-face.

### Design question

A rosette is bonded with its reference axis 30 degrees off the principal direction. For the General plane stress preset, by how many megapascals does the measured $\sigma_{x'}$ fall below the true $\sigma_1$, and why does a small misalignment in angle cause only a second-order error in the peak normal stress?

## Experiment 3: Maximum shear and its plane

<InArticleAd />


Ductile metals such as mild steel and aluminum yield by shear, so for them the plane of maximum shear is the plane where failure starts, and it is never the plane of maximum normal stress. This experiment finds $\tau_{\max}$, the plane it acts on, and the normal stress that acts on the same plane.

:::note[Objective]
Locate the maximum in-plane shear stress and the angle $\theta_s$ at which it acts, and show that the normal stress on the maximum-shear planes equals $\sigma_{\text{avg}}$, not zero.
:::

<Steps>
1. **Configure the simulator**
   Keep the General plane stress preset and read $\theta_p$. The plane of maximum shear is at $\theta_s = \theta_p - 45 = -25.67$ degrees. The slider starts at 0, so add 180 degrees, which gives the same plane, and set the angle to 154 degrees.

2. **Predict**
   Predict that the shear readout reaches its largest magnitude here, equal to $R$, and that the normal stress on this plane equals $\sigma_{\text{avg}}$ rather than zero.

3. **Confirm**
   Read $\tau_{x'y'}$ and $\sigma_{x'}$ at 154 degrees, then at 155 degrees. Compare $\tau_{\max}$ with $R$ from Experiment 1.

4. **Contrast**
   Note that at the principal planes the shear is zero but the normal stress is extreme, while at $\theta_s$ the shear is extreme but the normal stress is the average. The two critical orientations are forty-five degrees apart.

5. **Save data**
   Create `data/exp3_maxshear.csv` with columns: theta_s_deg, tau_max, sigma_on_maxshear_plane.
</Steps>

```python title="experiment_3_maxshear.py"
# Stress Transformation Experiment 3: maximum shear and its plane
import numpy as np

sx, sy, txy = 80.0, -20.0, 40.0
avg = (sx + sy) / 2.0
d = (sx - sy) / 2.0
R = np.hypot(d, txy)
theta_p = 0.5 * np.degrees(np.arctan2(2 * txy, sx - sy))
theta_s = theta_p - 45.0

t = np.radians(theta_s)
sxp = avg + d * np.cos(2 * t) + txy * np.sin(2 * t)
txp = -d * np.sin(2 * t) + txy * np.cos(2 * t)

print(f"theta_p = {theta_p:.2f} deg, theta_s = {theta_s:.2f} deg")
print(f"tau_max (= R) = {R:.2f} MPa, measured shear at theta_s = {txp:.2f} MPa")
print(f"normal stress on the max-shear plane = {sxp:.2f} MPa (should equal sigma_avg = {avg:.2f})")
```

### Expected results

$\theta_p = 19.33$ deg, $\theta_s = -25.67$ deg. The shear magnitude at $\theta_s$ equals $R = 64.0\ \text{MPa}$, and the normal stress on that plane is $30.0\ \text{MPa}$, exactly $\sigma_{\text{avg}}$. This is why a maximum-shear (Tresca) check uses $\tau_{\max} = R$ and why the average stress, not zero, accompanies the worst shear.

In the simulator the shear reads 64.0 MPa at 154 and at 155 degrees, and the normal stress 29.3 and 31.5 MPa: the exact angle lies between them. As a check, $\tau_{\max}$ must equal half of $\sigma_1 - \sigma_2$: $(94.0 + 34.0)/2 = 64.0$ MPa.

### Design question

For a shaft in pure torsion the normal stresses $\sigma_x$ and $\sigma_y$ are both zero. Where do the maximum-shear planes lie relative to the shaft axis, and how does that explain a ductile shaft failing on a flat transverse face?

## Experiment 4: Pure shear and the forty-five-degree failure

<InArticleAd />


Twist a stick of chalk until it breaks, and it breaks on a spiral surface (a helix) at forty-five degrees. A steel shaft twisted until it breaks shears off flat. Both are in pure shear. The difference is the stress that each material is weak against.

:::note[Objective]
Set a pure-shear state and show that its principal stresses are equal and opposite at forty-five degrees, then connect that to why brittle materials in torsion fail on a forty-five-degree helix while ductile ones shear transversely.
:::

<Steps>
1. **Configure the simulator**
   Select the Pure shear preset ($\sigma_x = 0$, $\sigma_y = 0$, $\tau_{xy} = 80\ \text{MPa}$).

2. **Predict**
   With $\sigma_x = \sigma_y = 0$ the circle is centred at the origin. Predict $\sigma_1 = +\tau$, $\sigma_2 = -\tau$ and $\theta_p = 45$ degrees.

3. **Read the principal state**
   Confirm $\sigma_1 = +80$, $\sigma_2 = -80$, and that the principal planes sit at 45 degrees. Note that the maximum tensile stress and maximum shear are equal in magnitude here.

4. **Reason about failure**
   A brittle material fails when $\sigma_1$ reaches its tensile limit, so it cracks on the plane normal to $\sigma_1$: the forty-five-degree helix. A ductile material yields when $\tau_{\max}$ reaches its shear limit, so it fails on the transverse plane of maximum shear.

5. **Save data**
   Create `data/exp4_pureshear.csv` with columns: txy, sigma_1, sigma_2, theta_p_deg.
</Steps>

### Expected results

$\sigma_1 = +80\ \text{MPa}$, $\sigma_2 = -80\ \text{MPa}$, $\theta_p = 45$ deg. The circle is centred on the origin with radius $80\ \text{MPa}$, so the largest tensile, compressive, and shear stresses are all $80\ \text{MPa}$. The orientation of $\sigma_1$ at 45 degrees is the helix on which brittle torsion specimens crack.

Cast iron is roughly four times stronger in compression than in tension. Using the principal stresses of pure shear, predict which forty-five-degree surface (the one normal to $\sigma_1$ or the one normal to $\sigma_2$) a cast-iron shaft cracks on, and sketch the helix direction.

## Experiment 5: Combined bending and torsion on a shaft surface

<InArticleAd />


The force on a pulley bends its shaft, and the torque it transmits twists the shaft. The surface carries a normal stress from the bending and a shear stress from the torque at the same point, and neither alone is the stress that the shaft must survive. This experiment builds that state from the shaft loads and finds the principal stresses that govern the design.

:::note[Objective]
Compute the surface stress state of a solid circular shaft under a bending moment $M$ and torque $T$, build its Mohr's circle, and find the principal stresses and maximum shear that a failure check will use.
:::

<Steps>
1. **Compute the state**
   For a solid shaft of diameter $d$, the bending stress at the surface is $\sigma_x = 32M/(\pi d^3)$ and the shear stress from the torque is $\tau_{xy} = 16T/(\pi d^3)$, with $\sigma_y = 0$. Work these out for $M = 200\ \text{N·m}$, $T = 350\ \text{N·m}$, $d = 25\ \text{mm}$. With N·mm and mm the result is in MPa.

2. **Configure the simulator**
   Select the Bending + torsion preset, then switch to Custom and set $\sigma_y = 0$. The sliders move in steps of 5 MPa, so set the nearest values to your results: $\sigma_x = 130$ and $\tau_{xy} = 115\ \text{MPa}$.

3. **Read the principal state**
   Record $\sigma_1$, $\sigma_2$, $\theta_p$, and $\tau_{\max}$ from the panel.

4. **Check the sign**
   $\sigma_2$ should be negative even though there is no applied compression: the shear pulls one principal stress below zero. Confirm the sign.

5. **Save data**
   Create `data/exp5_shaft.csv` with columns: M_Nm, T_Nm, d_mm, sigma_x, tau_xy, sigma_1, sigma_2, tau_max.
</Steps>

### Python analysis

```python title="experiment_5_shaft.py"
# Stress Transformation Experiment 5: combined bending and torsion on a shaft
import numpy as np

M = 200.0      # bending moment, N*m
T = 350.0      # torque, N*m
d = 25.0e-3    # shaft diameter, m

sx = 32 * M / (np.pi * d**3) / 1e6   # surface bending stress, MPa
txy = 16 * T / (np.pi * d**3) / 1e6  # torsional shear, MPa
sy = 0.0

avg = (sx + sy) / 2.0
R = np.hypot((sx - sy) / 2.0, txy)
s1, s2 = avg + R, avg - R
tau_max = R
theta_p = 0.5 * np.degrees(np.arctan2(2 * txy, sx - sy))

print(f"surface bending stress sigma_x = {sx:.1f} MPa")
print(f"torsional shear tau_xy = {txy:.1f} MPa")
print(f"sigma_1 = {s1:.1f} MPa, sigma_2 = {s2:.1f} MPa")
print(f"tau_max = {tau_max:.1f} MPa, theta_p = {theta_p:.1f} deg")

# Equivalent (combined) bending and torque, a shorthand many shaft codes use:
M_eq = 0.5 * (M + np.hypot(M, T))   # equivalent bending moment
T_eq = np.hypot(M, T)               # equivalent torque
print(f"equivalent bending moment M_eq = {M_eq:.1f} N*m, equivalent torque T_eq = {T_eq:.1f} N*m")
```

### Expected results

From the script: $\sigma_x = 130.4\ \text{MPa}$, $\tau_{xy} = 114.1\ \text{MPa}$, $\sigma_1 = 196.6\ \text{MPa}$, $\sigma_2 = -66.2\ \text{MPa}$, $\tau_{\max} = 131.4\ \text{MPa}$ and $\theta_p = 30.1$ deg. With the slider values 130 and 115 MPa the simulator shows 197.1, $-67.1$ and 132.1 MPa and 30.3 deg. The script also prints an equivalent bending moment of 301.6 N·m and an equivalent torque of 403.1 N·m, a shorthand that shaft design codes use.

Torsion brings one principal stress into compression although nothing pushes on the shaft, and $\sigma_1$ is 51% higher than the bending stress. A check of the bending stress alone would miss both. As a check, $\sigma_1 + \sigma_2$ must equal $\sigma_x$: $196.6 - 66.2 = 130.4$ MPa.

### Design question

## Experiment 6: von Mises versus Tresca and the safety factor

<InArticleAd />


Two engineers analyze the same shaft and report two different safety factors, and neither is wrong: one used von Mises and the other Tresca. This experiment measures the gap between the two, so that you know when the choice decides if a part passes.

Both criteria give one equivalent stress, and the safety factor is the yield strength divided by it. Here Tresca takes the full diameter of the circle, $\sigma_1 - \sigma_2$, and von Mises takes $\sqrt{\sigma_1^2 - \sigma_1\sigma_2 + \sigma_2^2}$.

:::note[Objective]
For a sweep of stress states, compute the von Mises and Tresca equivalent stresses and their safety factors against a fixed yield strength, and quantify how much more conservative Tresca is across the range.
:::

2. **Predict**
   Predict that Tresca always reports the lower safety factor.

3. **Sweep the shear**
   Hold $\sigma_x$ at $140\ \text{MPa}$ and $\sigma_y$ at 0, and step $\tau_{xy}$ from 0 to $120\ \text{MPa}$. At each step record both safety factors.

4. **Quantify the gap**

### Python analysis

```python title="experiment_6_criteria.py"
# Stress Transformation Experiment 6: von Mises vs Tresca
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import os

os.makedirs('plots', exist_ok=True)

def principals(sx, sy, txy):
    avg = (sx + sy) / 2.0
    R = np.hypot((sx - sy) / 2.0, txy)
    return avg + R, avg - R

def von_mises(s1, s2):           # plane stress, third principal is zero
    return np.sqrt(s1**2 - s1 * s2 + s2**2)

def tresca(s1, s2):              # max(|s1-s2|, |s1|, |s2|) with s3 = 0
    return np.maximum.reduce([np.abs(s1 - s2), np.abs(s1), np.abs(s2)])

sx, sy, Y = 140.0, 0.0, 250.0
txy = np.linspace(0, 120, 25)
s1, s2 = principals(sx, sy, txy)
vm, tr = von_mises(s1, s2), tresca(s1, s2)
sf_vm, sf_tr = Y / vm, Y / tr
gap = 100 * (tr - vm) / vm

for q, q_vm, q_tr in zip(txy[::8], sf_vm[::8], sf_tr[::8]):
    print(f"txy = {q:5.0f} MPa: SF_vm = {q_vm:.2f}, SF_tresca = {q_tr:.2f}")
print(f"max conservatism of Tresca over von Mises in this sweep = {gap.max():.1f}%")

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 5))
ax.plot(txy, sf_vm, color='#2A9D8F', lw=2, label='von Mises')
ax.plot(txy, sf_tr, color='#E76F51', lw=2, label='Tresca')
ax.axhline(1.0, color='gray', ls='--', lw=1, label='yield (SF = 1)')
ax.set_xlabel('shear stress tau_xy (MPa)'); ax.set_ylabel('safety factor')
ax.grid(True, alpha=0.3); ax.legend(); ax.set_title('Tresca is always the more conservative of the two')
plt.tight_layout(); plt.savefig('plots/exp6_criteria.png', dpi=150, bbox_inches='tight'); plt.show()
```

### Expected results

For the preset in step 1, von Mises gives 185.2 MPa (safety factor 1.35) and Tresca gives 198.0 MPa (1.26).

Tresca always reports the lower (more conservative) safety factor. The gap is zero at $\tau_{xy} = 0$ and widens as the shear grows, to about 11 percent at $\tau_{xy} = 120\ \text{MPa}$. The largest possible gap, at pure shear, is about 15.5 percent: von Mises puts yield in shear at $1/\sqrt{3}$ of the yield strength and Tresca at $1/2$.

At $\tau_{xy} = 120\ \text{MPa}$ the von Mises stress is 250.6 MPa, just above yield (safety factor 1.00), and Tresca gives 0.90. At $\tau_{xy} = 80\ \text{MPa}$ the factors are 1.27 and 1.18: a design that needs 1.25 passes by one criterion and fails by the other.
