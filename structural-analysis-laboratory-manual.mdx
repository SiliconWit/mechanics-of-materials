A stress picture from a finite element model always looks convincing. The finite element method (FEM) cuts a part into many small elements and lets a computer solve them together. A support that is fixed the wrong way, or a load that points the wrong way, still gives a smooth colour picture, and the stress in it can be wrong by a factor of two. The safe habit is to solve a simple version of the part by hand first, then make a Python script and the FEM model repeat it, and explain every difference. These three labs build that habit on a robotic arm, a 3D printer rail and a drone arm. #StructuralAnalysis #EngineeringLabs #FEMAnalysis

### The engineering worry

The forearm of a material handling robot carries the gripper and payload at its tip and is bolted to the elbow joint at the other end. A beam held at one end only is a cantilever. If the arm yields at the elbow (it bends and stays bent), the robot loses its accuracy permanently. A thicker arm is not a free answer, because every gram needs more motor torque.

### Problem statement

<Card title="Robotic arm data" icon="document">

| Quantity | Symbol | Value |
|---|---|---|
| Beam length | $L$ | 500 mm |
| Cross-section, a flat rectangle | $b \times h$ | 60 mm wide × 20 mm tall |
| Second moment of area, $bh^3/12$ | $I$ | $4.0 \times 10^4\ \text{mm}^4$ |
| Distance to the surface, $h/2$ | $c$ | 10 mm |
| Load at the free end: gripper and payload | $P$ | 500 N, downward |
| Material | | Aluminum alloy 6061-T6 |
| Yield strength: the stress at which the metal stays bent | $\sigma_{\text{yield}}$ | 275 MPa |
| Young's modulus: the stiffness of the material | $E$ | 69 GPa |
| Support | | Cantilever: fixed at $x = 0$, free at $x = 500\ \text{mm}$ |

</Card>

**Critical question:** What are the largest shear force and the largest bending moment in this arm, and where are they?

**You record:** the two reactions, the shear force, the bending moment at three points, the largest bending stress and the safety factor.

### Part 1: Analytical solution

Solve the arm by hand first. Signs follow the course: the shear force $V$ is positive when the forces to the left of a cut push up, and the bending moment $M$ is positive when it stretches the bottom of the beam.

<Steps>

1. **Draw the free body diagram**, a sketch of the beam alone with every force on it: the reaction force $R_y$ and the reaction moment $M_A$ at the fixed end, and the load $P = 500\ \text{N}$ at the free end. ✅

2. **Vertical reaction.** The beam does not move, so the upward forces equal the downward forces:

   $$\sum F_y = 0: \quad R_y - P = 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad R_y = P = 500\ \text{N (upward)}$$ ✅

3. **Reaction moment.** The beam does not turn, so the wall balances the moment of the load, force times distance:

   $$M_A = P \times L = 500\ \text{N} \times 0.5\ \text{m} = 250\ \text{N·m (counterclockwise)}$$ ✅

4. **Shear force.** Cut the beam anywhere. The only vertical force to the left of the cut is the wall reaction, which pushes up:

   $$V(x) = R_y = +500\ \text{N} \quad \text{for } 0 \leq x < 500\ \text{mm}$$

   At the tip the load brings the shear force back to zero. ✅

5. **Bending moment.** At a cut at $x$, the load acts at a distance $L - x$ to the right and stretches the top of the beam, so the moment is negative:

   $$M(x) = -P \times (L - x) = -500\ \text{N} \times (0.5\ \text{m} - x)$$

   - Fixed end, $x = 0$: $M = -500 \times 0.5 = -250\ \text{N·m}$
   - Middle, $x = 250\ \text{mm}$: $M = -500 \times 0.25 = -125\ \text{N·m}$
   - Free end, $x = 500\ \text{mm}$: $M = 0\ \text{N·m}$ ✅

6. **Largest bending stress.** By the flexure formula, the stress at the surface is the moment times the distance $c$ to the surface, divided by $I$. The largest moment, 250 N·m, is 250,000 N·mm:

   $$\sigma_{\max} = \frac{M_{\max} \times c}{I} = \frac{250{,}000\ \text{N·mm} \times 10\ \text{mm}}{4.0 \times 10^4\ \text{mm}^4} = 62.5\ \text{MPa}$$ ✅

7. **Safety factor**, the yield strength divided by the largest stress:

   $$SF = \frac{\sigma_{\text{yield}}}{\sigma_{\max}} = \frac{275\ \text{MPa}}{62.5\ \text{MPa}} = 4.4$$ ✅

</Steps>

Factors of 3 to 5 are usual in robotics, because the arm also meets loads from acceleration and impact that this static calculation leaves out.

:::tip[Is your answer reasonable?]
- The wall carries the whole load, so $R_y = P$.
- The moment is zero at the free end and largest at the wall.
- Aluminum machine parts work at tens of MPa. A result of 0.0625 MPa or 62,500 MPa means mixed units: m with mm, or cm⁴ with mm⁴.

The Python script must reproduce your values.
:::

### Part 2: Python analysis

The script repeats the hand solution and draws the two diagrams.

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# ===== PROBLEM PARAMETERS =====
# Geometry
L = 500  # Beam length in mm
b = 60   # Width in mm
h = 20   # Height in mm

# Loading
P = 500  # Point load in N

# Material
sigma_yield = 275  # Yield strength in MPa

# Section properties
I = (b * h**3) / 12  # Moment of inertia in mm^4
c = h / 2            # Distance to outer fiber in mm

print(f'Moment of Inertia: {I:.2e} mm^4')
print(f'Distance to outer fiber: {c:.1f} mm')

# ===== ANALYSIS =====
# Create position array (0 to L)
x = np.linspace(0, L, 100)

# Shear force (constant for cantilever with end load)
V = np.ones_like(x) * P  # Positive: the wall pushes the left part up

# Bending moment (linear variation)
M = -P * (L - x)  # Negative indicates tension on top fiber

# Find maximum values
V_max = np.max(np.abs(V))
M_max = np.max(np.abs(M))
M_max_location = x[np.argmax(np.abs(M))]

# Calculate maximum bending stress
sigma_max = (M_max * c) / I  # in N/mm^2 = MPa

# Safety factor
SF = sigma_yield / sigma_max

# ===== PRINT RESULTS =====
print('\n===== ANALYSIS RESULTS =====')
print(f'Maximum Shear Force: {V_max:.1f} N')
print(f'Maximum Bending Moment: {M_max:.1f} N·mm = {M_max/1000:.2f} N·m')
print(f'Location of M_max: {M_max_location:.1f} mm from fixed end')
print(f'Maximum Bending Stress: {sigma_max:.2f} MPa')
print(f'Safety Factor: {SF:.2f}')

# ===== CREATE PLOTS =====
fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(2, 1, figsize=(10, 8))

# Shear Force Diagram
ax1.plot(x, V, 'b-', linewidth=2, label='Shear Force')
ax1.axhline(y=0, color='k', linestyle='--', linewidth=0.5)
ax1.fill_between(x, 0, V, alpha=0.3, color='blue')
ax1.set_xlabel('Position along beam (mm)', fontsize=12)
ax1.set_ylabel('Shear Force (N)', fontsize=12)
ax1.set_title('Shear Force Diagram - Robotic Arm Cantilever', fontsize=14, fontweight='bold')
ax1.grid(True, alpha=0.3)
ax1.legend()

# Bending Moment Diagram
ax2.plot(x, M/1000, 'r-', linewidth=2, label='Bending Moment')
ax2.axhline(y=0, color='k', linestyle='--', linewidth=0.5)
ax2.fill_between(x, 0, M/1000, alpha=0.3, color='red')
ax2.set_xlabel('Position along beam (mm)', fontsize=12)
ax2.set_ylabel('Bending Moment (N·m)', fontsize=12)
ax2.set_title('Bending Moment Diagram - Robotic Arm Cantilever', fontsize=14, fontweight='bold')
ax2.grid(True, alpha=0.3)
ax2.legend()

plt.tight_layout()
plt.savefig('SFD_BMD_RoboticArm.png', dpi=300, bbox_inches='tight')
plt.show()

print('\nPlots saved as SFD_BMD_RoboticArm.png')
```

### The engineering worry

The print head of a desktop 3D printer moves along a horizontal rail, the gantry. Its weight is a point load that changes position all the time. If the rail bends too much, the layers of the print do not line up, and if the stress is too high, the rail fails after many cycles. The printer needs a positioning accuracy of $\pm 0.05\ \text{mm}$ and a light rail, so that the head can move fast.

### Problem statement

<Card title="3D printer gantry data" icon="document">

| Quantity | Symbol | Value |
|---|---|---|
| Rail length | $L$ | 800 mm |
| Cross-section, a round tube | | Outer diameter (OD) 30 mm, inner diameter (ID) 24 mm |
| Second moment of area, $\pi(\text{OD}^4 - \text{ID}^4)/64$ | $I$ | $2.35 \times 10^4\ \text{mm}^4$ |
| Distance to the surface, the outer radius | $c$ | 15 mm |
| Print head weight | $P$ | 200 N, anywhere from 0 to 800 mm |
| Material | | Aluminum alloy 6061-T6 |
| Young's modulus | $E$ | 70 GPa |
| Poisson's ratio: sideways shrinking divided by lengthwise stretching | $\nu$ | 0.33 |
| Yield strength | $\sigma_{\text{yield}}$ | 275 MPa |
| Supports | | Simply supported: a pin at $x = 0$, a roller at $x = 800\ \text{mm}$. The roller lets the rail expand when it warms |

</Card>

**Critical questions:**
1. Where is the print head when the bending stress is highest?
2. How large is the stress at that position?
3. Does the FEM model confirm the hand solution?

**You record:** the worst position, the largest moment, stress and safety factor by hand and from Python, then the FEM stress and the number of elements for three mesh sizes.

### Part 1: Analytical solution

#### Theory: moving load on a simply supported beam

Put the load $P$ at a distance $a$ from the left support. The support nearer to the load carries more of it:

$$R_A = \frac{P(L-a)}{L} \qquad R_B = \frac{Pa}{L}$$

The bending moment is largest under the load, where it is the left reaction times $a$:

$$M(a) = \frac{Pa(L-a)}{L}$$

It is zero with the load over a support and grows toward the middle. Its slope is zero at the largest value:

$$\frac{dM}{da} = \frac{P(L-2a)}{L} = 0$$

So the bending moment is largest with the load at midspan, $a = L/2 = 400\ \text{mm}$.

<Steps>

1. **Largest moment.** At midspan the formula above becomes $PL/4$:

   $$M_{\max} = \frac{PL}{4} = \frac{200\ \text{N} \times 800\ \text{mm}}{4} = 40{,}000\ \text{N·mm} = 40\ \text{N·m}$$ ✅

2. **Largest bending stress**, by the flexure formula: the moment times the distance to the surface, divided by $I$:

   $$\sigma_{\max} = \frac{M_{\max} \times c}{I} = \frac{40{,}000\ \text{N·mm} \times 15\ \text{mm}}{2.35 \times 10^4\ \text{mm}^4} = 25.6\ \text{MPa}$$ ✅

3. **Safety factor**, the yield strength divided by the largest stress:

   $$SF = \frac{\sigma_{\text{yield}}}{\sigma_{\max}} = \frac{275\ \text{MPa}}{25.6\ \text{MPa}} = 10.8$$ ✅

4. **Read the result.** A factor of 10.8 is high for a load that stands still. The print head accelerates and stops, the rail vibrates, and the load repeats millions of times. These effects can multiply the stress by 2 to 5, and the high static factor is the margin for them. ✅

</Steps>

:::tip[Is your answer reasonable?]
- With the head over a support, the moment under it is zero.
- If your FEM stress is near 12.8 MPa, half of the hand value, the ends of your model cannot rotate. Built-in ends give a midspan moment of $PL/8$, not $PL/4$.
:::

### Part 2: Python parametric analysis

The script moves the load along the rail in 200 steps, calculates the stress at each position and plots the result.

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# ===== PROBLEM PARAMETERS =====
# Geometry
L = 800  # Rail length in mm
OD = 30  # Outer diameter in mm
t = 3  # Wall thickness in mm
ID = OD - 2*t  # Inner diameter in mm

# Loading
P = 200  # Print head weight in N

# Material
sigma_yield = 275  # Yield strength in MPa
E = 70000  # Young's modulus in MPa

# Section properties (hollow circular)
I = (np.pi / 64) * (OD**4 - ID**4)  # Moment of inertia in mm^4
c = OD / 2  # Distance to outer fiber in mm

print(f'Moment of Inertia: {I:.2e} mm^4')
print(f'Distance to outer fiber: {c:.1f} mm')

# ===== PARAMETRIC ANALYSIS =====
# Analyze load at multiple positions
a_positions = np.linspace(0, L, 201)  # Load positions (odd count, so midspan is one of them)

# Calculate moment at each position
M_at_load = P * a_positions * (L - a_positions) / L

# Calculate stress at each position
sigma = (M_at_load * c) / I

# Find maximum values
a_critical = a_positions[np.argmax(sigma)]
M_max = np.max(M_at_load)
sigma_max = np.max(sigma)
SF = sigma_yield / sigma_max

# ===== PRINT RESULTS =====
print('\n===== ANALYSIS RESULTS =====')
print(f'Critical load position: {a_critical:.1f} mm from left support')
print(f'Maximum bending moment: {M_max:.1f} N·mm = {M_max/1000:.2f} N·m')
print(f'Maximum bending stress: {sigma_max:.3f} MPa')
print(f'Safety factor: {SF:.1f}')

# ===== CREATE PLOTS =====

### The engineering worry

Each arm of a quadcopter drone holds a motor and a propeller at its tip. The thrust bends the arm up, air drag bends it sideways, and the spinning propeller twists it. A carbon fiber tube is strong along its fibers and weak in shear, so a stress that looks small can be the one that breaks the arm. An arm that fails in flight destroys the drone, and every gram that is not needed shortens the flight. The target is a safety factor of 3.0 with the lightest tube.

### Part 1: Combined stress analysis

Find the stress from each load alone, then add them at the worst point.

<Steps>

1. **Moment from the thrust**, largest at the fixed end: force times length.

   $$M_y = P \times L = 30\ \text{N} \times 200\ \text{mm} = 6{,}000\ \text{N·mm}$$ ✅

2. **Bending stress from the thrust**, from the flexure formula $\sigma = Mc/I$. It is tension on the bottom of the tube and compression on the top:

   $$\sigma_x = \frac{M_y \cdot c}{I} = \frac{6{,}000\ \text{N·mm} \times 8\ \text{mm}}{2{,}199\ \text{mm}^4} = 21.8\ \text{MPa}$$ ✅

3. **Moment from the drag**, which bends the arm sideways:

   $$M_z = F_{\text{drag}} \times L = 5\ \text{N} \times 200\ \text{mm} = 1{,}000\ \text{N·mm}$$ ✅

4. **Bending stress from the drag**, largest at the two sides of the tube:

   $$\sigma_x = \frac{M_z \cdot c}{I} = \frac{1{,}000\ \text{N·mm} \times 8\ \text{mm}}{2{,}199\ \text{mm}^4} = 3.6\ \text{MPa}$$ ✅

5. **Shear stress from the torque**, largest at the outer radius $r$: the torque (1,000 N·mm) times the radius, divided by $J$:

   $$\tau_{xy} = \frac{T \cdot r}{J} = \frac{1{,}000\ \text{N·mm} \times 8\ \text{mm}}{4{,}398\ \text{mm}^4} = 1.82\ \text{MPa}$$ ✅

6. **Resultant moment.** The critical point is at the fixed end, on the outer surface. A circular tube has the same $I$ about every axis through its centre, so the two bending moments add as vectors into one resultant moment:

   $$M_R = \sqrt{M_y^2 + M_z^2} = \sqrt{(6{,}000)^2 + (1{,}000)^2}\ \text{N·mm} = 6{,}083\ \text{N·mm}$$ ✅

7. **Where the critical point is.** The largest bending stress is at the surface point farthest from the neutral axis of $M_R$. That point is not at 45°. Measured from the vertical axis of the section, it is turned toward the drag side by:

   $$\phi = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{M_z}{M_y}\right) = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{1{,}000}{6{,}000}\right) = 9.5°$$ ✅

8. **Stress state at the critical point.** The bending stress there is:

   $$\sigma_x = \frac{M_R \cdot c}{I} = \frac{6{,}083 \times 8}{2{,}199} = \sqrt{(21.8)^2 + (3.6)^2} = 22.1 \text{ MPa (tensile)}$$

   $$\tau_{xy} = 1.82 \text{ MPa (shear from torsion)}$$

   The point on the opposite side of the tube has the same shear stress and $\sigma_x = -22.1\ \text{MPa}$ (compressive). ✅

9. **Largest principal stress.** On one plane through the point the shear stress is zero and the normal stress is as large as it can be. This is the principal stress $\sigma_1$:

   $$\sigma_1 = \frac{\sigma_x}{2} + \sqrt{\left(\frac{\sigma_x}{2}\right)^2 + \tau_{xy}^2}$$

   $$\sigma_1 = \frac{22.1}{2} + \sqrt{\left(\frac{22.1}{2}\right)^2 + (1.82)^2} = 22.3 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

10. **Smallest principal stress.** The same formula with a minus sign gives the normal stress on the plane at 90° to the first:

    $$\sigma_2 = \frac{\sigma_x}{2} - \sqrt{\left(\frac{\sigma_x}{2}\right)^2 + \tau_{xy}^2}$$

    $$\sigma_2 = \frac{22.1}{2} - \sqrt{\left(\frac{22.1}{2}\right)^2 + (1.82)^2} = -0.15 \text{ MPa}$$

    Nothing presses on the surface of the tube, so the third principal stress is $\sigma_3 = 0$ (plane stress). ✅

11. **Von Mises stress**, one equivalent stress from the three principal stresses. It was made for ductile metals, and for a composite it is only a first check:

    $$\sigma_{VM} = \sqrt{\frac{(\sigma_1-\sigma_2)^2 + (\sigma_2-\sigma_3)^2 + (\sigma_3-\sigma_1)^2}{2}}$$

    $$\sigma_{VM} = \sqrt{\frac{(22.3-(-0.15))^2 + (-0.15-0)^2 + (0-22.3)^2}{2}} = 22.4 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

12. **Safety factor by von Mises.** The lower of the two normal strengths, 500 MPa, is used in place of a yield strength:

    $$SF = \frac{\sigma_{\text{yield}}}{\sigma_{VM}} = \frac{500}{22.35} = 22.4$$ ✅

13. **Maximum stress criterion, tension.** This criterion suits a brittle composite better: compare each largest stress with its own strength. In tension, at the critical point:

    $$SF_{\text{tension}} = \frac{\sigma_t}{\sigma_1} = \frac{600}{22.3} = 26.9$$ ✅

14. **Maximum stress criterion, compression**, at the opposite point. There the stress state is the mirror image, so the largest compressive principal stress is also 22.3 MPa:

    $$SF_{\text{compression}} = \frac{\sigma_c}{|\sigma_{\min}|} = \frac{500}{22.3} = 22.4$$ ✅

15. **Largest shear stress.** The maximum in-plane shear stress is the radius of Mohr's circle:

    $$\tau_{\max} = \sqrt{\left(\frac{\sigma_x}{2}\right)^2 + \tau_{xy}^2} = \sqrt{\left(\frac{22.1}{2}\right)^2 + (1.82)^2} = 11.2 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

16. **Maximum stress criterion, shear.**

    $$SF_{\text{shear}} = \frac{\tau_{\text{ultimate}}}{\tau_{\max}} = \frac{70}{11.2} = 6.2$$

    **Governing safety factor: 6.2 (shear failure)** ✅

    This shear check uses the 70 MPa strength on every plane, which is the safe assumption when the fiber layup is not known. If all fibers run along the arm, the 70 MPa strength belongs to the plane along the fibers, where the shear stress is only $\tau_{xy} = 1.82\ \text{MPa}$ ($70/1.82 = 38$). A real composite design checks each stress in the fiber directions of each layer.

</Steps>

**Engineering assessment:** The governing safety factor is 6.2, and the expected failure mode is shear, not tension. The low shear strength of the composite controls the design, even though the normal stress is far below the tensile strength. The factor is about twice the 3.0 target, so the arm is heavier than it needs to be. Some margin is useful for loads in maneuvers (up to 3 times the static load), defects in the composite, fatigue from vibration and impact in a crash. A safety factor of 3 to 4 would suit this arm better.

:::tip[Is your answer reasonable?]
- For a round tube, $J$ is exactly twice $I$.
- The torque is small here, so $\sigma_1$ is only a little above $\sigma_x$ (22.3 against 22.1 MPa), and $\tau_{\max}$ is close to half of $\sigma_x$ (11.2 against 11.1 MPa).
- The two principal stresses add up to $\sigma_x$: $22.3 + (-0.15) = 22.1$ MPa.

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# Geometry
L = 200  # Arm length in mm
OD = 16  # Outer diameter in mm
ID = 12  # Inner diameter in mm
r_outer = OD / 2
r_inner = ID / 2

P_vertical = 30  # N
F_horizontal = 5  # N
T_torque = 1000  # N·mm

# Material (carbon fiber)
sigma_tensile = 600  # MPa
sigma_compressive = 500  # MPa
tau_ultimate = 70  # MPa

# Section properties

I = (np.pi / 64) * (OD**4 - ID**4)  # Moment of inertia

print(f'Polar Moment of Inertia: {J:.1f} mm^4')
print(f'Moment of Inertia: {I:.1f} mm^4')

# ===== STRESS ANALYSIS =====
# Bending moments
M_vertical = P_vertical * L
M_horizontal = F_horizontal * L

# Largest bending stress from each load on its own
sigma_vertical = (M_vertical * r_outer) / I
sigma_horizontal = (M_horizontal * r_outer) / I

# Resultant moment on the circular section and the angle of the critical point,
# measured from the vertical axis of the section toward the drag side
M_resultant = np.sqrt(M_vertical**2 + M_horizontal**2)
phi_critical = np.degrees(np.arctan2(M_horizontal, M_vertical))

sigma_x = (M_resultant * r_outer) / I

tau_xy = (T_torque * r_outer) / J

print(f'Bending stress (vertical load): {sigma_vertical:.2f} MPa')
print(f'Bending stress (horizontal load): {sigma_horizontal:.2f} MPa')

print(f'Critical point angle from vertical axis: {phi_critical:.1f} degrees')
print(f'Torsional shear stress: {tau_xy:.2f} MPa')

# ===== PRINCIPAL STRESSES =====

sigma_avg = sigma_x / 2

sigma_1 = sigma_avg + R
sigma_2 = sigma_avg - R
sigma_3 = 0  # plane stress

print('\n===== PRINCIPAL STRESSES =====')
print(f'σ₁ (maximum principal): {sigma_1:.2f} MPa')
print(f'σ₂ (minimum principal): {sigma_2:.2f} MPa')
print(f'σ₃ (out-of-plane): {sigma_3:.2f} MPa')

sigma_vm = np.sqrt(((sigma_1-sigma_2)**2 + (sigma_2-sigma_3)**2 + (sigma_3-sigma_1)**2) / 2)

# Tresca (maximum shear stress)
tau_max = (sigma_1 - sigma_2) / 2

# Safety factors

SF_max_stress_tensile = sigma_tensile / sigma_1
# The opposite point of the tube has the mirror-image state, so its largest
# compressive principal stress has the same size as sigma_1
SF_max_stress_compressive = sigma_compressive / sigma_1
SF_max_stress_shear = tau_ultimate / tau_max

safety_factors = {
    'Von Mises criterion': SF_von_mises,

ax.text(sigma_2, -2, f'σ₂ = {sigma_2:.1f} MPa', fontsize=11, ha='center')

# Plot original stress state
ax.plot([sigma_x, 0], [tau_xy, -tau_xy], 'gs', markersize=8, label='Original State')

# Draw line connecting original state points

F_horizontal = 5  # N
T_torque = 1000  # N·mm
sigma_tensile = 600  # MPa
sigma_compressive = 500  # MPa
tau_ultimate = 70  # MPa

# Design constraints
SF_target = 3.0  # Target safety factor
L = 200  # Length fixed

# Vary tube dimensions
OD_range = np.linspace(10, 24, 29)  # Outer diameter range, 0.5 mm steps
thickness = 2  # Fix wall thickness at 2mm

# Material density
rho = 1.6e-6  # kg/mm^3 (carbon fiber)


def analyze_tube(OD, ID):
    """Return (governing safety factor, mass in grams) for one tube."""
    r_outer = OD / 2
    r_inner = ID / 2

    # Section properties
    J = (np.pi / 32) * (OD**4 - ID**4)
    I = (np.pi / 64) * (OD**4 - ID**4)

    # Stress analysis (same loading)
    M_resultant = np.sqrt((P_vertical * L)**2 + (F_horizontal * L)**2)
    sigma_x = (M_resultant * r_outer) / I
    tau_xy = (T_torque * r_outer) / J

    # Principal stress and maximum shear stress
    R = np.sqrt((sigma_x/2)**2 + tau_xy**2)
    sigma_1 = sigma_x/2 + R
    tau_max = R

    # Governing safety factor: the lowest of tension, compression and shear
    SF = min(sigma_tensile / sigma_1,
             sigma_compressive / sigma_1,
             tau_ultimate / tau_max)

    # Mass calculation
    volume = np.pi * (r_outer**2 - r_inner**2) * L
    mass = volume * rho * 1000  # Convert to grams
    return SF, mass


results = [analyze_tube(OD, OD - 2*thickness) for OD in OD_range]
safety_factors = np.array([r[0] for r in results])
masses = np.array([r[1] for r in results])

# Original design (OD 16 mm, ID 12 mm)
original_SF, original_mass = analyze_tube(16, 12)

# Find optimal design: the lightest tube that still meets the target
feasible = np.where(safety_factors >= SF_target)[0]
optimal_idx = feasible[np.argmin(masses[feasible])]
optimal_OD = OD_range[optimal_idx]
optimal_mass = masses[optimal_idx]
optimal_SF = safety_factors[optimal_idx]

# Plotting
fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(14, 5))

# Mass vs Outer Diameter
ax1.plot(OD_range, masses, 'b-', linewidth=2)
ax1.plot(optimal_OD, optimal_mass, 'ro', markersize=12, label=f'Optimal: OD={optimal_OD:.1f}mm, m={optimal_mass:.1f}g')
ax1.set_xlabel('Outer Diameter (mm)', fontsize=12)
ax1.set_ylabel('Mass (grams)', fontsize=12)
ax1.set_title('Arm Mass vs Tube Diameter', fontsize=14, fontweight='bold')
ax1.grid(True, alpha=0.3)
ax1.legend()

# Safety Factor vs Outer Diameter
ax2.plot(OD_range, safety_factors, 'r-', linewidth=2)
ax2.axhline(y=SF_target, color='g', linestyle='--', linewidth=2, label=f'Target SF = {SF_target}')
ax2.plot(optimal_OD, optimal_SF, 'ro', markersize=12, label=f'Optimal: SF={optimal_SF:.2f}')
ax2.set_xlabel('Outer Diameter (mm)', fontsize=12)
ax2.set_ylabel('Safety Factor', fontsize=12)
ax2.set_title('Safety Factor vs Tube Diameter', fontsize=14, fontweight='bold')
ax2.grid(True, alpha=0.3)
ax2.legend()

plt.tight_layout()
plt.savefig('Design_Optimization_Study.png', dpi=300, bbox_inches='tight')
plt.show()

print('\n===== OPTIMIZATION RESULTS =====')
print(f'Optimal outer diameter: {optimal_OD:.1f} mm')
print(f'Optimal mass per arm: {optimal_mass:.1f} grams')
print(f'Safety factor achieved: {optimal_SF:.2f}')
print(f'\nOriginal design: OD=16mm, mass={original_mass:.1f}g, SF={original_SF:.2f}')
print(f'Mass savings: {original_mass - optimal_mass:.1f}g ({(original_mass - optimal_mass)/original_mass*100:.1f}%)')
print(f'Total savings for 4 arms: {4*(original_mass - optimal_mass):.1f}g')
```

**Expected results:**
- Optimal outer diameter: 12.0 mm (with the 2 mm wall), safety factor 3.09
- Optimal mass per arm: 20.1 g, against 28.1 g for the original design
- Mass savings: 8.0 g per arm (28.6%), 32.2 g for 4 arms

### Appendix F: Troubleshooting guide

**Python errors:**

<Tabs>
  <TabItem label="Import errors">

  `ModuleNotFoundError: No module named 'numpy'` means that a library is missing. Install it:
  ```bash
  pip install numpy
  ```

  With Anaconda:
  ```bash
  conda install numpy
  ```

  </TabItem>

  <TabItem label="Runtime errors">

  `ZeroDivisionError` or `invalid value encountered`: the script divides by zero. Check that no input value is zero. `np.seterr(divide='warn')` shows where it happens.

  </TabItem>

  <TabItem label="Plotting issues">

  If the plot window is empty or does not open, check that `plt.show()` is called, or set the backend with `matplotlib.use('TkAgg')`. `plt.savefig()` always writes the file.

  </TabItem>
</Tabs>

**FreeCAD FEM errors:**

<Tabs>
  <TabItem label="Meshing failures">

  "Meshing failed", or a mesh of very poor quality: remove fillets, chamfers and small holes, increase the maximum element size, or change between Gmsh and Netgen.

  </TabItem>

  <TabItem label="Solver errors">

  CalculiX fails or gives no results:
  - The supports must stop every rigid movement of the part
  - The material values must be physical: positive $E$, and $\nu$ between 0 and 0.5

  </TabItem>

  <TabItem label="Unrealistic results">

  The FEM stress differs from the hand value by more than 50%:
  - Check the size and direction of each load. Sign errors are common
  - Check the units: N, mm and MPa, or N, m and Pa, never a mixture
  - Check that the material values are those of the hand solution
  - Run a mesh convergence study

  </TabItem>
</Tabs>
