A pin is checked for bending and passes. It is checked for torsion and passes. It still yields, because the largest stress at a point is often on a plane that nobody checked: the stress you find depends on the direction in which you cut the material. This lesson shows how to find the largest and smallest stresses at a point (the principal stresses), how Mohr's circle shows every direction in one picture, and how a failure theory turns the result into one number to compare with the yield strength. You use it on the pin of a universal joint in a robot drive. #PrincipalStress #MohrsCircle #FailureAnalysis

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

1. **Construct** Mohr's circle for any 2D stress state in mechatronic components
2. **Determine** principal stresses and maximum shear stresses graphically and analytically
3. **Apply** appropriate failure theories for different materials and loading conditions
4. **Decide** whether a loaded part will yield, and with what safety factor

## Real-World System Problem: Universal joint in robotic drive system

<InArticleAd />


A universal joint lets a drive shaft turn through an angle. Each shaft ends in a fork, and a cross-shaped pin joins the two forks. The pin carries three loads at once: the torque of the drive twists it, the angle of the joint bends it, and the bearings press on its surface.

```text
           bearing presses on the surface: 50 MPa
                     ▼  ▼  ▼  ▼  ▼
         ┌──────────────────────────────┐
         │   pin, 25 mm in diameter     │  ↻ torque 500 N·m
         └──────────────────────────────┘
         bending moment 200 N·m bends the pin
```

> **Engineering Question:** What is the largest stress at the surface of the pin, on which plane does it act, and will the pin yield?

## Fundamental Theory: Principal stress analysis

<InArticleAd />


### The stress at a point depends on the cut

Glue two pieces of wood end to end and pull them. If the joint is cut square, the glue is only pulled. If the joint is cut at a slant, the same pull also tries to slide one piece along the other, so the glue carries tension and shear together. The load did not change, only the direction of the cut.

A glued joint is one real cut. A solid part must survive on every cut that can be imagined.

To describe the stress at a point, imagine a very small square of material around it, with faces along x and y. Three numbers act on its faces:

- $\sigma_x$ = normal stress on the x face, in MPa (tension is positive, compression is negative)
- $\sigma_y$ = normal stress on the y face (MPa)
- $\tau_{xy}$ = shear stress on the faces (MPa)

### Principal stress theory

Turn the square and the three numbers change. At one angle the shear stress becomes zero. The two normal stresses at that angle are the principal stresses: $\sigma_1$ is the largest normal stress at the point and $\sigma_2$ is the smallest. No cut carries more tension than $\sigma_1$.

<Tabs>
  <TabItem label="Principal stress equations">

<Card title="Principal stress equations" icon="document">
The principal stresses are the average of the two normal stresses, plus and minus one square root:

$$\sigma_{1,2} = \frac{\sigma_x + \sigma_y}{2} \pm \sqrt{\left(\frac{\sigma_x - \sigma_y}{2}\right)^2 + \tau_{xy}^2}$$

The angle $\theta_p$ (theta, in degrees) from the x face to the plane of a principal stress is given by:

$$\tan(2\theta_p) = \frac{2\tau_{xy}}{\sigma_x - \sigma_y}$$
</Card>

  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="Maximum shear stress">

<Card title="Maximum shear stress" icon="document">
The largest shear stress in the plane is half the difference of the principal stresses:

$$\tau_{\max} = \frac{\sigma_1 - \sigma_2}{2}$$

It acts on planes at 45° to the principal planes. On those planes the normal stress is the average, $\sigma_{\text{avg}} = (\sigma_x + \sigma_y)/2$.
</Card>

  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="Third principal stress">

A free surface carries no stress at right angles to itself, so a third principal stress, equal to zero, exists there. Put the three in order, $\sigma_1 \geq \sigma_2 \geq \sigma_3$. The largest shear stress on any plane is then:

$$\tau_{\text{abs},\max} = \frac{\sigma_1 - \sigma_3}{2}$$

  </TabItem>
</Tabs>

### Mohr's circle construction

Plot each cut as one point: its normal stress across and its shear stress up. As the cut turns, the point moves on a circle. This is Mohr's circle, and two numbers define it.

<Card title="Mohr's circle parameters" icon="document">
The centre $C$ is on the horizontal axis, at the average normal stress:

$$C = \frac{\sigma_x + \sigma_y}{2}$$

The radius $R$ is the square root in the principal stress equation:

$$R = \sqrt{\left(\frac{\sigma_x - \sigma_y}{2}\right)^2 + \tau_{xy}^2}$$
</Card>

- The circle crosses the horizontal axis at $\sigma_1 = C + R$ and $\sigma_2 = C - R$.
- Its top and bottom give the largest shear stress, $\tau_{\max} = R$.
- When the cut turns through an angle $\theta$, its point moves through $2\theta$ on the circle.

### Failure theories

A tension test gives one number for a metal, the yield strength $\sigma_{yield}$: the stress at which it stops springing back. A real part has two or three principal stresses. A failure theory turns them into one number to compare with $\sigma_{yield}$.

- **Maximum normal stress theory.** Compare $\sigma_1$ alone with the strength. It suits brittle materials such as cast iron, which crack under tension. It misses yielding that shear causes, so do not use it for ductile metals (metals that stretch before they break).
- **Maximum shear stress theory (Tresca).** A ductile metal yields when $\tau_{\max}$ reaches $\sigma_{yield}/2$, the shear stress at yield in a tension test. This is the cautious choice.
- **Von Mises theory.** A ductile metal yields when the equivalent stress $\sigma_{\text{eq}}$ reaches $\sigma_{yield}$. Tests agree best with this theory.

$$\sigma_{\text{eq}} = \sqrt{\sigma_1^2 - \sigma_1\sigma_2 + \sigma_2^2}$$

Equal pressure from all sides squeezes a metal but does not make it yield. Unequal stresses change its shape, and that causes yielding. The von Mises stress measures this difference, so it is one number for how close the point is to yielding. The safety factor (SF) is $\sigma_{yield}/\sigma_{\text{eq}}$.

## Application: Universal joint pin analysis

<InArticleAd />


:::note[Problem Statement]
- **Pin:** solid round steel pin, diameter $d = 25$ mm
- **Material:** steel 4340, $\sigma_{yield} = 470$ MPa
- **Torque:** $T = 500$ N·m
- **Bending moment:** $M = 200$ N·m, from the angle of the joint
- **Bearing pressure:** 50 MPa on the surface of the pin
- **Required safety factor:** 4

**Key Question:** Can the 25 mm pin carry 500 N·m of torque and 200 N·m of bending with a safety factor of 4, and which failure theory gives the lowest safety factor?
:::

### Step 1: Calculate individual stress components

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal individual stress component calculations**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Second moment of area** of the round pin, for the bending stress:

   $$I = \frac{\pi d^4}{64} = \frac{\pi (25 \text{ mm})^4}{64} = 19{,}175 \text{ mm}^4$$ ✅

2. **Polar second moment of area**, for the torsional stress. For a round section it is twice $I$:

   $$J = \frac{\pi d^4}{32} = \frac{\pi (25 \text{ mm})^4}{32} = 38{,}350 \text{ mm}^4$$ ✅

3. **Bending stress at the surface**, with 200 N·m written as $200 \times 10^3$ N·mm and $c = d/2 = 12.5$ mm. Take the x axis along the pin:

   $$\sigma_x = \frac{Mc}{I} = \frac{200 \times 10^3 \text{ N·mm} \times 12.5 \text{ mm}}{19{,}175 \text{ mm}^4} = 130.4 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

4. **Torsional shear stress at the surface**, with $r = 12.5$ mm:

   $$\tau_{xy} = \frac{Tr}{J} = \frac{500 \times 10^3 \text{ N·mm} \times 12.5 \text{ mm}}{38{,}350 \text{ mm}^4} = 163.0 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

5. **Bearing stress.** The bearing presses on the surface, which is a compression across the pin, so it is negative:

   $$\sigma_y = -50 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 2: Principal stress analysis using Mohr's circle

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal Mohr's circle and principal stress calculations**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Centre of the circle**, the average normal stress:

   $$\sigma_{\text{avg}} = \frac{\sigma_x + \sigma_y}{2} = \frac{130.4 + (-50)}{2} = 40.2 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

2. **Radius of the circle**, with $\sigma_x - \sigma_y = 130.4 - (-50) = 180.4$ MPa:

   $$R = \sqrt{\left(\frac{\sigma_x - \sigma_y}{2}\right)^2 + \tau_{xy}^2} = \sqrt{\left(\frac{180.4}{2}\right)^2 + 163.0^2} = 186.3 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

3. **Principal stresses**, the centre plus and minus the radius. The largest tension at the point is 74% above the bending stress of 130.4 MPa:

   $$\sigma_1 = 40.2 + 186.3 = 226.5 \text{ MPa} \qquad \sigma_2 = 40.2 - 186.3 = -146.1 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

4. **Direction of $\sigma_1$**, measured from the x face:

   $$\tan(2\theta_p) = \frac{2 \times 163.0}{180.4} = 1.807 \qquad \theta_p = 30.5°$$ ✅

5. **Largest shear stress**, equal to the radius. The third principal stress, zero, lies between $\sigma_1$ and $\sigma_2$, so no plane carries more:

   $$\tau_{\max} = \frac{\sigma_1 - \sigma_2}{2} = \frac{226.5 - (-146.1)}{2} = 186.3 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 3: Apply failure theories and design assessment

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal failure theory analysis**</summary>

With a required safety factor of 4, the allowed stress is $470/4 = 117.5$ MPa.

<Steps>

1. **Maximum normal stress theory.** $\sigma_1 = 226.5$ MPa is above 117.5 MPa, so the check fails:

   $$\text{SF} = \frac{470}{226.5} = 2.08$$ ✅

2. **Maximum shear stress theory (Tresca).** The allowed shear stress is $117.5/2 = 58.75$ MPa, and $\tau_{\max} = 186.3$ MPa is above it, so the check fails:

   $$\text{SF} = \frac{470}{2 \times 186.3} = 1.26$$ ✅

3. **Von Mises equivalent stress:**

   $$\sigma_{\text{eq}} = \sqrt{\sigma_1^2 - \sigma_1\sigma_2 + \sigma_2^2} = \sqrt{226.5^2 - 226.5(-146.1) + (-146.1)^2} = 325.2 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

4. **Von Mises safety factor.** 325.2 MPa is above 117.5 MPa, so the check fails:

   $$\text{SF} = \frac{470}{325.2} = 1.45$$ ✅

5. **Design assessment.** Every safety factor is above 1, so the pin does not yield, but none reaches the required 4. For this ductile steel, report the von Mises value, 1.45, with Tresca, 1.26, as the cautious limit. The 2.08 looks better only because that theory ignores shear. The pin needs a larger diameter. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

## Engineering problems

<InArticleAd />


### Problem 1: Pressure vessel joint analysis

A joint in the wall of a spherical pressure vessel carries the stress from the internal pressure and an extra stress from heating.

**Given:**
- Spherical pressure vessel: inner radius $R = 500$ mm, wall thickness $t = 20$ mm
- Internal pressure: $p = 2.5$ MPa
- Extra thermal stress: $\sigma_{\text{thermal}} = 50$ MPa, in one direction along the wall
- Material: steel, $\sigma_{yield} = 250$ MPa
- Required safety factor: 3.0

**Find:** the principal stresses, and the safety factor by the von Mises theory.

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal solution**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Stress from the pressure.** In a thin sphere it is the same in every direction along the wall. Through the thickness the stress is close to zero:

   $$\sigma = \frac{pR}{2t} = \frac{2.5 \text{ MPa} \times 500 \text{ mm}}{2 \times 20 \text{ mm}} = 31.25 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

2. **Principal stresses.** Add the thermal stress in its own direction: $31.25 + 50 = 81.25$ MPa. No shear stress acts on these faces, so the values are already principal stresses: $\sigma_1 = 81.25$ MPa, $\sigma_2 = 31.25$ MPa and $\sigma_3 = 0$ (through the thickness). ✅

3. **Von Mises stress.** With three principal stresses, the general form uses the three differences:

   $$\sigma_{\text{eq}} = \frac{\sqrt{(\sigma_1-\sigma_2)^2 + (\sigma_2-\sigma_3)^2 + (\sigma_3-\sigma_1)^2}}{\sqrt{2}}$$

   $$\sigma_{\text{eq}} = \frac{\sqrt{(81.25-31.25)^2 + (31.25-0)^2 + (0-81.25)^2}}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{\sqrt{2500 + 976.6 + 6601.6}}{\sqrt{2}} = 71.0 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

4. **Safety factor.** It is above the required 3.0, so the design is adequate:

   $$\text{SF} = \frac{\sigma_{yield}}{\sigma_{\text{eq}}} = \frac{250 \text{ MPa}}{71.0 \text{ MPa}} = 3.52$$ ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Problem 2: Shaft with keyway under combined loading

A motor shaft has a keyway, the slot for the key that locks a gear to the shaft. Its sharp corners multiply the stress near them by the stress concentration factor $K_t$.

**Given:**
- Solid round shaft: diameter $d = 50$ mm
- Bending moment: $M = 800$ N·m
- Torque: $T = 1200$ N·m
- Material: steel 4140, $\sigma_{yield} = 415$ MPa
- Stress concentration factor for the keyway: $K_t = 2.5$

**Find:** the principal stresses at the keyway, and the safety factor.

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal solution**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Nominal bending stress** at the surface, as if there were no keyway, with $c = 25$ mm:

   $$\sigma = \frac{Mc}{I} = \frac{800 \times 10^3 \times 25}{\pi \times 50^4/64} = 65.2\ \text{MPa}$$ ✅

2. **Nominal torsional shear stress** at the surface, with $r = 25$ mm:

   $$\tau = \frac{Tr}{J} = \frac{1200 \times 10^3 \times 25}{\pi \times 50^4/32} = 48.9\ \text{MPa}$$ ✅

3. **Apply the stress concentration** to both stresses:

   $$\sigma_k = K_t \times \sigma = 2.5 \times 65.2 = 163.0\ \text{MPa} \qquad \tau_k = K_t \times \tau = 2.5 \times 48.9 = 122.2\ \text{MPa}$$ ✅

4. **Centre and radius of Mohr's circle.** There is no second normal stress, so the centre is half of $\sigma_k$:

   $$\sigma_{\text{avg}} = \frac{163.0}{2} = 81.5\ \text{MPa} \qquad R = \sqrt{81.5^2 + 122.2^2} = 146.9\ \text{MPa}$$ ✅

5. **Principal stresses**, the centre plus and minus the radius. The radius, 146.9 MPa, is also the largest shear stress:

   $$\sigma_1 = 81.5 + 146.9 = 228.4\ \text{MPa} \qquad \sigma_2 = 81.5 - 146.9 = -65.4\ \text{MPa}$$ ✅

6. **Von Mises stress:**

   $$\sigma_{\text{eq}} = \sqrt{\sigma_1^2 - \sigma_1\sigma_2 + \sigma_2^2} = \sqrt{228.4^2 - 228.4 \times (-65.4) + (-65.4)^2} = 267.2 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

7. **Safety factor.** The shaft does not yield, but the margin is small:

   $$\text{SF} = \frac{415}{267.2} = 1.55$$ ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Problem 3: Bolted joint under eccentric loading

A bolt is pulled by a load that does not act along its centre line (an eccentric load), so the bolt is also bent.

**Given:**
- Bolt material: steel grade 8.8, $\sigma_{yield} = 640$ MPa
- Bolt size: M12 (nominal diameter 12 mm)
- Tensile load: $F = 8000$ N
- Bending moment on the bolt: $M = 96$ N·m, from the eccentric load
- Stress concentration factor at the thread: $K_t = 3.0$
- Required safety factor: 4.0

**Find:** the principal stresses in the bolt, and whether the design is adequate.

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal solution**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Section of the bolt at the thread.** Standard values for M12 are a tensile stress area $A_s = 84.3$ mm² and a minor diameter $d = 10.106$ mm. The section modulus $S$ is $I/c$, which for a round section is:

   $$S = \frac{\pi d^3}{32} = \frac{\pi (10.106 \text{ mm})^3}{32} = 101.3\ \text{mm}^3$$ ✅

2. **Direct tensile stress**, force over area:

   $$\sigma_t = \frac{F}{A_s} = \frac{8000 \text{ N}}{84.3 \text{ mm}^2} = 94.9 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

3. **Bending stress**, moment over section modulus:

   $$\sigma_b = \frac{M}{S} = \frac{96 \times 10^3 \text{ N·mm}}{101.3 \text{ mm}^3} = 947.4 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

4. **Total stress.** Both are normal stresses along the bolt, so on the tension side they add:

   $$\sigma_{\text{total}} = \sigma_t + \sigma_b = 94.9 + 947.4 = 1042.3 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

5. **Apply the stress concentration.** One stress acts, along the bolt, with no shear, so this value is $\sigma_1$, the other principal stresses are zero, and $\sigma_{\text{eq}} = \sigma_1$:

   $$\sigma_{\max} = K_t \times \sigma_{\text{total}} = 3.0 \times 1042.3 = 3126.9 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

6. **Safety factor.** It is below 1, so the bolt yields at the thread. Bending gives ten times the stress of the direct pull, so the joint must be changed until the load acts along the centre line of the bolt:

   $$\text{SF} = \frac{\sigma_{yield}}{\sigma_{\text{eq}}} = \frac{640}{3126.9} = 0.20$$ ✅

</Steps>

</details>

## Course Completion Summary

<InArticleAd />


### Key concepts

1. **The stress at a point depends on the direction of the cut.** $\sigma_x$, $\sigma_y$ and $\tau_{xy}$ describe one pair of faces only.
2. **The principal stresses** are the largest and smallest normal stresses at the point, on planes with no shear stress.
3. **Mohr's circle** shows every cut as one point: centre at the average normal stress, radius equal to the largest shear stress.
4. **A failure theory** gives one number to compare with the yield strength. For ductile metals use von Mises.
