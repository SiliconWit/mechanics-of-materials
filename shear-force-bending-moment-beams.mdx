A robot arm that carries its load at the tip cracks at the base, where it is bolted down, and never at the tip. A plank across a stream breaks near the middle, far from both banks. Each point along a beam carries a different internal force, and the beam fails where that force is largest. This lesson shows you how to find it: cut the beam in your mind, ask what the cut face must carry, and plot the answer along the beam as two diagrams, the shear force and the bending moment. #BeamAnalysis #StructuralMechanics #EngineeringDesign

## Fundamental Theory: Shear force and bending moment

<InArticleAd />


### Basic beam concepts

A support holds a beam by stopping some of its movements. For each movement it stops, the support must push back with a force or a moment, called a reaction. So the type of support tells you how many unknown reactions the beam has.

<div style={{ display: 'flex', justifyContent: 'center', width: '100%' }}>
  <TailwindWrapper>
    ![Animation of three beams, each pushed up at its free end by a force P. The beam on a pinned support turns about the pin. The beam on a roller support turns and also moves sideways. The beam in a fixed support does not turn and does not move](https://cdn.siliconwit.com/education/solid-mechanics/support-comparison.gif)
  </TailwindWrapper>
</div>

1. **Pinned support (hinge)**, like a door hinge
   - ✅ Allows rotation at the connection
   - Stops movement up, down and sideways
   - **Reactions:** a vertical force and a horizontal force (2 unknowns: $R_x$, $R_y$)

2. **Roller support**, like a beam end resting on a wheel
   - ✅ Allows rotation at the connection
   - ✅ Allows movement in one direction, usually along the beam, so the beam can grow when it warms
   - **Reactions:** one force, at right angles to the surface it rolls on (1 unknown: $R_y$)

3. **Fixed support (built-in or clamped)**, like a shelf bracket screwed to a wall
   - Stops rotation and stops movement in every direction
   - **Reactions:** a vertical force, a horizontal force, and a moment (3 unknowns: $R_x$, $R_y$, $M$)

A beam on one pin and one roller is called simply supported. A beam with one fixed end and one free end is a cantilever. For both, the two equilibrium rules give every reaction: the forces add up to zero, and the moments about any point add up to zero.

### Shear force and bending moment at a cut

Cut the beam in your mind at a distance $x$ from its left end and keep only the left part. The left part must still be in balance, so the cut face must supply whatever the loads and reactions on that part do not balance among themselves. It supplies two things:

- **Shear force $V$**, in newtons (N): the force across the beam at the cut. It stops the left part from sliding up or down past the right part.
- **Bending moment $M$**, in newton metres (N·m): the turning effect at the cut. It stops the left part from rotating. A moment is a force multiplied by its distance from the cut.

```text
          400 N
            │
            ▼                 cut at x
 ┌───────────────────────────┐┆
 │     left part of beam     │┆  the cut face carries
 └───────────────────────────┘┆  V (a force) and M (a moment)
 ▲
 │ 280 N from the support
 │◄──────────── x ──────────►│
```

Take a 2,000 mm beam on two supports with one 400 N box on it, 600 mm from the left support. The left support pushes up with 280 N (the live figure further down shows why). Cut the beam at $x = 1{,}000$ mm. On the left part the reaction is 1.0 m from the cut and the box is 0.4 m from it:

$$V = 280 - 400 = -120\ \text{N}$$

$$M = 280 \times 1.0 - 400 \times 0.4 = 120\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}$$

Repeat this for every $x$, plot the two results along the beam, and you have the shear force diagram and the bending moment diagram.

### Sign conventions

Every worked step below keeps to one convention, with $x$ measured from the left end.

- **$V$ is positive** when the forces on the left part add up to an upward push. An upward force to the left of the cut adds to $V$, and a downward load is subtracted.
- **$M$ is positive** when it bends the beam into a sag: the bottom is stretched (tension) and the top is squeezed (compression). An upward force to the left of the cut gives a positive moment. A **negative** moment bends the beam into a hog, with the top stretched, as in a cantilever with a load on its tip.
- **A distributed load $w$** is a load spread along the beam, in newtons per metre (N/m), such as the weight of the beam itself. It is a positive number when it acts downward.

```text
  Positive M: the beam sags      Negative M: the beam hogs

  ╲     top squeezed     ╱           top stretched
   ╲                    ╱         ╭──────────────────╮
    ╰──────────────────╯         ╱                    ╲
      bottom stretched          ╱   bottom squeezed    ╲
   ▲                    ▲                  ▲
   support        support               support
```

### Common beam equations

Two rules link the load, the shear force and the bending moment. In words: as you move along the beam, the shear force falls at the rate of the load you pass, and the bending moment changes at a rate equal to the shear force.

<Card title="Load, shear and moment" icon="document">
$$\frac{dV}{dx} = -w(x)$$

$$\frac{dM}{dx} = V(x)$$

Where:
- $x$ = position along the beam, from the left end (m)
- $w(x)$ = distributed load at $x$, positive downward (N/m)
- $V(x)$ = shear force at $x$ (N)
- $M(x)$ = bending moment at $x$ (N·m)

$dV/dx$ is the slope of the shear diagram and $dM/dx$ is the slope of the moment diagram.
</Card>

Think of the moment as the height of a path and the shear as its slope. While the shear is positive the path climbs, and while it is negative the path descends. Where the shear passes through zero the path is at a top or a bottom, so the moment is largest there. The comparison stops at a point load: there the slope changes suddenly, and the path has a corner.

The cards below give $V(x)$ and $M(x)$ for the three beams of this lesson. $L$ is the length of the beam and $P$ is a point load in newtons.

<Card title="Cantilever beam (fixed at one end, free at the other)" icon="document">
<div style={{ display: 'flex', justifyContent: 'center', width: '100%', marginBottom: '1rem' }}>
  ![A cantilever beam of length L, fixed in a wall at the left. A distributed load w presses down along the whole beam and a point load P presses down at the free tip. The wall pushes up with the reaction Ry. The position x is measured from the wall](https://cdn.siliconwit.com/education/solid-mechanics/cantilever_beam.svg)
</div>

The beam carries a distributed load $w$ and a point load $P$ at the tip. Measure $x$ from the fixed support. The support pushes up with $R_y = wL + P$ and holds a moment $M_{base} = wL^2/2 + PL$.

Shear force:

$$V(x) = R_y - w \cdot x = (wL + P) - w \cdot x$$

Bending moment:

$$M(x) = -M_{base} + R_y \cdot x - w \cdot \frac{x^2}{2}$$

The shear is positive and the moment is negative (hogging). Both are largest at the fixed support, and the moment is zero at the free end.
</Card>

<Card title="Simply supported beam (pin and roller)" icon="document">
<div style={{ display: 'flex', justifyContent: 'center', width: '100%', marginBottom: '1rem' }}>
  ![A beam of length L on a pinned support at the left end, with reaction RA, and a roller support at the right end, with reaction RB. One point load P presses down at the centre](https://cdn.siliconwit.com/education/solid-mechanics/simply_supported_beam.svg)
</div>

The beam carries one point load $P$ at its centre, so each support carries $P/2$.

Shear force:

$$V(x) = \frac{P}{2} \quad \text{constant from } 0 \text{ to } L/2$$

Bending moment:

$$M(x) = \frac{P}{2} \cdot x \quad \text{for } 0 \le x \le L/2$$

The moment is zero at both supports, because a pin or a roller cannot resist rotation. It is largest at the centre, $PL/4$, where the shear changes sign to $-P/2$.
</Card>

<Card title="Overhanging beam" icon="document">
<div style={{ display: 'flex', justifyContent: 'center', width: '100%', marginBottom: '1rem' }}>
  ![A beam on a pinned support, reaction RA, and a roller support, reaction RB, with a distributed load w along its whole length. In this picture the beam reaches past both supports](https://cdn.siliconwit.com/education/solid-mechanics/overhanging_beam.svg)
</div>

The picture shows an overhang at both ends. The formulas below are for the simpler beam of Application 3: support A is at the left end ($x = 0$), support B is at $x = L_{span}$, and the beam reaches past B only. The load $w$ covers the whole length.

Shear force:
- Between the supports: $V(x) = R_A - w \cdot x$
- On the overhang: $V(x) = R_A - w \cdot x + R_B$

Bending moment:
- Between the supports: $M(x) = R_A \cdot x - w \cdot \frac{x^2}{2}$
- On the overhang: $M(x) = R_A \cdot x - w \cdot \frac{x^2}{2} + R_B(x - L_{span})$

The moment is positive (sagging) in part of the span and negative (hogging) over support B, where it is usually largest.
</Card>

### Quick sketching techniques

The two rules $dV/dx = -w$ and $dM/dx = V$ hold for every beam. With them you can sketch both diagrams before you calculate anything, and catch a wrong calculation afterwards.

#### Rule 1: each diagram is one step more curved than the one above it

<div style={{ display: 'flex', justifyContent: 'center', width: '100%' }}>
  <TailwindWrapper>
    ![Three columns, each with a load, the shape of its shear diagram and the shape of its moment diagram. A point load gives a level shear line and a straight sloping moment line. A uniformly distributed load gives a straight sloping shear line and a curved moment line. A triangular load gives a curved shear line and a more strongly curved moment line](https://cdn.siliconwit.com/education/solid-mechanics/sketching_integration_pattern.svg)
  </TailwindWrapper>
</div>

- Between **point loads** the shear is constant, so the moment is a straight sloping line.
- Under a **uniformly distributed load (UDL)**, with the same $w$ all along, the shear is a straight sloping line, so the moment is a parabola.

#### Rule 2: the shear is the slope of the moment diagram

Where the shear is far from zero, the moment diagram is steep. Where the shear is near zero, it is nearly flat. Where the shear is positive the moment rises to the right, and where it is negative the moment falls.

<div style={{ display: 'flex', justifyContent: 'center', width: '100%' }}>
  <TailwindWrapper>
    ![Two pairs of sketches. In each pair a sloping shear line is drawn above a curved moment line. The ends of the shear line are labelled Fast where the shear is far from zero and Slow where it is near zero, and the moment curve is steep at the Fast end and nearly flat at the Slow end. A small car sits on each moment curve](https://cdn.siliconwit.com/education/solid-mechanics/sketching_slope_direction_guide.svg)
  </TailwindWrapper>
</div>

This picture draws positive moment downward, the opposite of every other plot in this lesson, and both of its shear lines are below zero. Use it for one thing only: the moment curve is steep at the end marked Fast and nearly flat at the end marked Slow.

#### Rule 3: checks at the ends, the supports and the loads

1. **Free end:** $M = 0$. The shear equals the point load on the tip, or zero if there is none.
2. **Pin or roller at the end of a beam:** $M = 0$, because the support cannot resist rotation.
3. **Fixed support:** $V$ equals the reaction force and $M$ equals the reaction moment.
4. **Point load $P$:** the shear jumps by $P$. The moment does not jump, but its diagram has a corner there.
5. **Shear passes through zero:** the moment has a local largest or smallest value there.

To sketch a beam: calculate the reactions, draw the shear from left to right (up at each upward force, down at each load), then draw the moment with a slope equal to the shear, and check it against Rule 3.

### Step 1: Calculate reaction forces

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal reaction force calculations**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Sum of vertical forces (↑ positive).** The support reaction $R_y$ must balance the two downward loads:
   
   $$\sum F_y = 0: R_y - 75(2.5) - 1200 = 0$$
   
   - $R_y$ = upward reaction (positive)
   - $75\ \text{N/m} \times 2.5\ \text{m} = 187.5\ \text{N}$ = total distributed load (downward, negative) ✅
   - $1200\ \text{N}$ = point load (downward, negative) ✅
   
   $$R_y = 187.5 + 1200 = 1387.5 \text{ N (upward)}$$ ✅

2. **Sum of moments about the base (counterclockwise positive).** The whole distributed load acts as if it sat at the middle of the arm, 1.25 m from the base. The payload is 2.5 m from the base:
   
   $$\sum M_{base} = 0: M_{base} - 75(2.5)(1.25) - 1200(2.5) = 0$$
   
   - $M_{base}$ = reaction moment at the base (counterclockwise, positive)
   - $187.5\ \text{N} \times 1.25\ \text{m} = 234.375\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}$ = moment of the distributed load (clockwise, negative) ✅
   - $1200\ \text{N} \times 2.5\ \text{m} = 3000\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}$ = moment of the point load (clockwise, negative) ✅
   
   $$M_{base} = 234.375 + 3000 = 3234.375 \text{ N·m (counterclockwise)}$$ ✅

3. **Check the balance:**
   
   Forces: ↑1387.5 N = ↓(187.5 + 1200) N ✅
   Moments: 3234.375 N·m = 3234.375 N·m ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 2: Construct shear force diagram

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal shear force analysis**</summary>

Cut the arm at a distance $x$ from the base and keep the left part. On it act the reaction $R_y$, upward, and the weight of $x$ metres of arm, $75x$, downward.

For $0 \le x \le 2.5\ \text{m}$:

$$V(x) = R_y - wx = 1387.5 - 75x \quad \text{(N, with } x \text{ in m)}$$

Values at the two ends:
- At $x = 0$: $V(0) = +1387.5\ \text{N}$ (positive: the support reaction pushes the left part up) ✅
- At $x = 2.5\ \text{m}$: $V(2.5) = 1387.5 - 75(2.5) = +1200\ \text{N}$ ✅

At the tip the payload makes the shear jump: <br/>
Just before the tip: $V = +1200\ \text{N}$ <br/>
Just after the tip: $V = 1200 - 1200 = 0\ \text{N}$ ✅

### Step 3: Construct bending moment diagram

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal bending moment analysis**</summary>

Take moments about the cut for the same left part. Three things act on it: the base moment, which hogs the arm (negative); the reaction $R_y$ at a distance $x$ (positive); and the weight $75x$ at a distance $x/2$ (negative).

For $0 \le x \le 2.5\ \text{m}$:

$$M(x) = -3234.375 + 1387.5x - 75x \cdot \frac{x}{2} = -3234.375 + 1387.5x - 37.5x^2 \quad \text{(N·m)}$$

Check with the slope rule: $dM/dx = 1387.5 - 75x = V(x)$ ✅

Values at the two ends:
- At $x = 0$: $M(0) = -3234.375\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}$ (negative = hogging moment at the fixed end, tension on the top face) ✅
- At $x = 2.5\ \text{m}$: $M(2.5) = -3234.375 + 1387.5(2.5) - 37.5(2.5)^2 = -3234.375 + 3468.75 - 234.375 = 0\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}$ ✅

  </TailwindWrapper>

**Check at the free end:**
Nothing applies a moment at the free end ($x = 2.5\ \text{m}$), so the moment there must be zero. The equation gives $M(2.5) = 0$ ✅

### Step 4: Summary of critical values

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal summary of maximum shear and moment**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Largest shear force:**

   Maximum $|V| = 1387.5\ \text{N}$ at $x = 0$ (fixed support) ✅

2. **Largest bending moment.** Every load on a cantilever has its longest lever arm about the fixed support, so the moment is largest there:

   - At $x = 0$ (support): $|M(0)| = 3234.375\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}$ ✅
   - At $x = 2.5\ \text{m}$ (tip): $M(2.5) = 0\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}$ ✅

   **Maximum |M|** = 3234.375 N·m at $x = 0$ (fixed support) ✅

3. **What the diagrams say:**

   - The bending moment follows a parabola. The payload supplies 3000 of the 3234 N·m at the base, so the curve looks almost straight.
   - The fixed support is the critical section: it carries the largest shear and the largest moment, and it is the first place to fail if the arm is overloaded.

</Steps>

- Cross-section: hollow rectangular steel 60×40×4 mm ($I = 3.45 \times 10^5\ \text{mm}^4$ with the 60 mm side vertical, $c = 30\ \text{mm}$)

### Step 1: Calculate reaction forces

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal reaction force calculations**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Sum of vertical forces (↑ positive).** The two reactions together carry the five boxes:

   $$\sum F_y = 0: R_A + R_B - (5 \times 400) = 0$$

   $$R_A + R_B = 2000 \text{ N}$$ ✅

2. **Sum of moments about A (counterclockwise positive).** Each box is multiplied by its distance from A, in N and mm:

   $$\sum M_A = 0: R_B(2000) - 400(200 + 600 + 1000 + 1400 + 1800) = 0$$

   $$R_B(2000) = 400(5000) = 2{,}000{,}000\ \text{N}\cdot\text{mm}$$

   $$R_B = \frac{2{,}000{,}000}{2000} = 1000 \text{ N}$$ ✅

3. **Calculate $R_A$:**

   $$R_A = 2000 - 1000 = 1000 \text{ N}$$ ✅

4. **Check by symmetry:**

   The loads are placed symmetrically about the centre ($x = 1000\ \text{mm}$), so the reactions are equal: $R_A = R_B = 1000$ N ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 2: Construct shear force diagram

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal shear force analysis**</summary>

Start at the left support with $V = R_A$ and move to the right. Between boxes nothing changes. At each box the shear drops by 400 N.

**For $0 \le x < 200\ \text{mm}$:**
$$V(x) = R_A = 1000 \text{ N}$$ ✅

**At $x = 200\ \text{mm}$ (first load):**
Jump down by 400 N: $V = 1000 - 400 = 600\ \text{N}$ ✅

**For $200 < x < 600\ \text{mm}$:**
$$V(x) = 600 \text{ N}$$ ✅

**At $x = 600\ \text{mm}$ (second load):**
Jump down by 400 N: $V = 600 - 400 = 200\ \text{N}$ ✅

**For $600 < x < 1000\ \text{mm}$:**
$$V(x) = 200 \text{ N}$$ ✅

**At $x = 1000\ \text{mm}$ (center load):**
Jump down by 400 N: $V = 200 - 400 = -200\ \text{N}$ ✅

**For $1000 < x < 1400\ \text{mm}$:**
$$V(x) = -200 \text{ N}$$ ✅

**At $x = 1400\ \text{mm}$ (fourth load):**
Jump down by 400 N: $V = -200 - 400 = -600\ \text{N}$ ✅

**For $1400 < x < 1800\ \text{mm}$:**
$$V(x) = -600 \text{ N}$$ ✅

**At $x = 1800\ \text{mm}$ (fifth load):**
Jump down by 400 N: $V = -600 - 400 = -1000\ \text{N}$ ✅

**For $1800 < x \le 2000\ \text{mm}$:**
$$V(x) = -1000 \text{ N}$$ ✅

**Maximum shear force:** $|V_{max}| = 1000\ \text{N}$ at both supports ✅

### Step 3: Construct bending moment diagram

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal bending moment analysis**</summary>

Cut the beam at $x$ and take moments about the cut for the left part. The reaction $R_A$ is at a distance $x$. Each box to the left of the cut is at a distance ($x$ minus its position). Here $x$ is in mm and $M$ is in N·mm.

**For $0 \le x \le 200\ \text{mm}$:**
$$M(x) = R_A \cdot x = 1000x$$

**For $200 \le x \le 600\ \text{mm}$:**
$$M(x) = 1000x - 400(x - 200) = 600x + 80{,}000$$

**For $600 \le x \le 1000\ \text{mm}$:**
$$M(x) = 1000x - 400(x - 200) - 400(x - 600) = 200x + 320{,}000$$

**For $1000 \le x \le 1400\ \text{mm}$:**
$$M(x) = 1000x - 400(x - 200) - 400(x - 600) - 400(x - 1000) = -200x + 720{,}000$$

**For $1400 \le x \le 1800\ \text{mm}$:**
$$
\begin{aligned}
M(x) &= 1000x - 400(x - 200) - 400(x - 600) - 400(x - 1000) - 400(x - 1400) \\
     &= -600x + 1{,}280{,}000
\end{aligned}
$$

**For $1800 \le x \le 2000\ \text{mm}$:**
$$
\begin{aligned}
M(x) &= 1000x - 400(x - 200) - 400(x - 600) - 400(x - 1000) - 400(x - 1400) - 400(x - 1800) \\
     &= -1000x + 2{,}000{,}000
\end{aligned}
$$

**Maximum bending moment:** $M_{max} = 200(1000) + 320{,}000 = 520{,}000\ \text{N}\cdot\text{mm} = 520\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}$ at $x = 1000\ \text{mm}$ (center) ✅

### Step 4: Critical section analysis

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal critical analysis and design recommendations**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Where the moment is largest:**

   **Center ($x = 1000\ \text{mm}$):** $M = 520\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}$ ✅
   **Under the neighboring boxes ($x = 600, 1400\ \text{mm}$):** $M = 600(600) + 80{,}000 = 440{,}000\ \text{N}\cdot\text{mm} = 440\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}$ ✅

   **Critical location:** the center of the beam (midspan), under the middle box, where the shear changes sign from $+200\ \text{N}$ to $-200\ \text{N}$ ✅

2. **Why midspan is critical:**

   - The moment keeps rising while the shear is positive, so it grows from each box to the next up to $x = 1000\ \text{mm}$.
   - With point loads only, the largest moment is always under a load: the one where the shear changes sign.

   **Design recommendation:** size the beam for the moment under the middle box, where the shear changes sign ✅

3. **What follows from this:**

   - The beam also sags most at the center.
   - If the moment is too large for the section, add a support between the two ends.

</Steps>

</details>

## Application 3: Solar tracker arm (electronics/mechatronics)

<InArticleAd />


A solar tracker arm turns a row of panels to follow the sun. The arm rests on two supports and reaches 0.5 m past the second one. It carries the weight of the panels and the wind along its whole length, and the weight of a clamp mechanism at its tip.

:::note[System Problem Statement]
- **Loading:** a uniformly distributed load (panel weight and wind) and one point load at the tip (the clamp)
- **Support:** simply supported beam with an overhang
- **Find:** the reactions, the two diagrams, and the largest sagging and hogging moments

**Key Question:** What are the maximum positive and negative bending moments, and which section is most critical?
:::

**Given** (the picture labels the distributed load W and marks the total length as L; the text calls the load $w$ and the tip load $P$):
- Total beam length: $L_{total} = 3.0\ \text{m}$ (2.5 m span + 0.5 m overhang)
- Support A (pinned): at $x = 0$
- Support B (roller): at $x = 2.5\ \text{m}$
- Uniformly distributed load: $w = 300\ \text{N/m}$ across the entire length (panel weight + wind)
- Point load at the tip: $P = 600\ \text{N}$ at $x = 3.0\ \text{m}$ (clamp mechanism)
- Cross-section: structural steel I-beam ($I = 6.8 \times 10^6\ \text{mm}^4$, $c = 40\ \text{mm}$)
- Material: structural steel ($\sigma_{\text{yield}} = 250\ \text{MPa}$)
</Card>

### Step 1: Calculate reaction forces

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal reaction force calculations**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Sum of vertical forces (↑ positive):**

   $$\sum F_y = 0: R_A + R_B - w \cdot L_{total} - P = 0$$

   $$R_A + R_B = 300 \times 3.0 + 600 = 900 + 600 = 1500 \text{ N}$$ ✅

2. **Sum of moments about A (counterclockwise positive).** The whole distributed load, 900 N, acts at the middle of the beam, 1.5 m from A. The clamp is 3.0 m from A:

   $$\sum M_A = 0: R_B(2.5) - w \cdot L_{total} \cdot \frac{L_{total}}{2} - P(3.0) = 0$$

   $$R_B(2.5) = 300 \times 3.0 \times 1.5 + 600 \times 3.0$$

   $$R_B(2.5) = 1350 + 1800 = 3150\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}$$

   $$R_B = \frac{3150}{2.5} = 1260 \text{ N}$$ ✅

3. **Calculate $R_A$:**

   $$R_A = 1500 - 1260 = 240 \text{ N}$$ ✅

4. **Check with moments about B.** The loads to the right of B turn the beam the opposite way to the loads on the span, so their terms have the opposite sign:

   $$\sum M_B = 0: R_A(2.5) - w(2.5)(1.25) + w(0.5)(0.25) + P(0.5) = 0$$

   - Moment of $R_A$: $+240(2.5) = +600\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}$
   - Moment of the distributed load on the span (0 to 2.5 m): $-300(2.5)(1.25) = -937.5\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}$
   - Moment of the distributed load on the overhang (2.5 to 3.0 m): $+300(0.5)(0.25) = +37.5\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}$
   - Moment of $P$ at the tip: $+600(0.5) = +300\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}$

   $$\sum M_B = 600 - 937.5 + 37.5 + 300 = 0$$ ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 2: Construct shear force diagram

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal shear force analysis**</summary>

Move from the left support to the right. The distributed load takes 300 N from the shear for each metre. At support B the reaction $R_B$ lifts the shear by 1260 N.

**For $0 \le x \le 2.5\ \text{m}$ (between the supports):**
$$V(x) = R_A - wx = 240 - 300x$$

**For $2.5 < x \le 3.0\ \text{m}$ (on the overhang):**
$$V(x) = R_A - wx + R_B = 240 - 300x + 1260 = 1500 - 300x$$

**Values at the points that matter:**
- At $x = 0$: $V(0) = 240\ \text{N}$ ✅
- At $x = 0.8\ \text{m}$: $V(0.8) = 240 - 300(0.8) = 0\ \text{N}$ ✅ (zero shear point)
- Just before support B ($x = 2.5^-$): $V(2.5^-) = 240 - 300(2.5) = -510\ \text{N}$ ✅
- Just after support B ($x = 2.5^+$): $V(2.5^+) = -510 + 1260 = 750\ \text{N}$ ✅
- At tip before point load ($x = 3.0^-$): $V(3.0^-) = 1500 - 300(3.0) = 600\ \text{N}$ ✅
- At tip after point load ($x = 3.0^+$): $V(3.0^+) = 600 - 600 = 0\ \text{N}$ ✅

**Maximum shear forces:**
- **Positive:** $V_{max} = 750\ \text{N}$ just after support B ✅
- **Negative:** $V_{min} = -510\ \text{N}$ just before support B ✅

### Step 3: Construct bending moment diagram

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal bending moment analysis**</summary>

The moment is found here by integrating the shear, which is the rule $dM/dx = V$ used backward. Each integration leaves a constant, $C_1$ or $C_2$, which is fixed by a place where the moment is already known.

**For $0 \le x \le 2.5\ \text{m}$:**
$$M(x) = \int V(x)dx = \int(240 - 300x)dx = 240x - 150x^2 + C_1$$

The moment is zero at the pin, $M(0) = 0$, so $C_1 = 0$:
$$M(x) = 240x - 150x^2$$

**For $2.5 < x \le 3.0\ \text{m}$:**
$$M(x) = \int(1500 - 300x)dx = 1500x - 150x^2 + C_2$$

The moment does not jump at support B, so both formulas must give the same value at $x = 2.5\ \text{m}$: $M(2.5) = 240(2.5) - 150(2.5)^2 = -337.5\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}$
$$C_2 = -337.5 - 1500(2.5) + 150(2.5)^2 = -337.5 - 3750 + 937.5 = -3150$$
$$M(x) = 1500x - 150x^2 - 3150$$

**Moment values at the points that matter:**
- At $x = 0$: $M(0) = 0\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}$ ✅
- At $x = 0.8\ \text{m}$ (zero shear): $M(0.8) = 240(0.8) - 150(0.8)^2 = 192 - 96 = 96\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}$ ✅ **← Max positive**
- At $x = 2.5\ \text{m}$ (support B): $M(2.5) = 240(2.5) - 150(2.5)^2 = 600 - 937.5 = -337.5\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}$ ✅ **← Max negative**
- At $x = 3.0\ \text{m}$ (tip): $M(3.0) = 1500(3.0) - 150(3.0)^2 - 3150 = 4500 - 1350 - 3150 = 0\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}$ ✅

**Maximum moments:**
- **Positive:** $M_{max} = +96\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}$ at $x = 0.8\ \text{m}$ ✅
- **Negative:** $M_{min} = -337.5\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}$ at support B ($x = 2.5\ \text{m}$) ✅

### Step 4: Critical section analysis

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal critical bending stress assessment**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Compare the two largest moments:**

   **Positive moment:** $M_{max} = +96\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}$ at $x = 0.8\ \text{m}$ ✅
   **Negative moment:** $|M_{min}| = 337.5\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}$ at support B ✅

   **Critical location:** support B, because the moment there is larger in size ✅

2. **Why support B is the critical section:**

   - The hogging moment at B (337.5 N·m) is 3.5 times the sagging moment in the span (96 N·m).
   - The shear is also largest at B.
   - So support B sizes the beam, and the span does not.

   At support B the moment is negative, so the arm hogs over the support: the top face is in tension and the bottom face is in compression.

3. **What a designer does next:** strengthen the arm near support B, and check fatigue, because wind and tracking motion load the arm again and again.

</Steps>

</details>

:::tip[Instrument it]
You can read the bending moment from a beam in service. Bond a strain gauge to the top or bottom face where the moment is largest. The surface strain, multiplied by Young's modulus and by the section modulus of the beam, gives the bending moment at that section, and a few gauges along the span give the whole moment diagram. Read them with a quarter-bridge into an HX711 or an instrumentation amplifier and an [ESP32 or STM32](/education/sensor-actuator-interfacing-stm32/), and you can [watch the live moment on siliconwit.io](https://siliconwit.io) and catch an overload before the beam yields.
:::

## Summary and next steps

<InArticleAd />


- Cut the beam, keep the left part, and ask what the cut face must carry: a shear force $V$ and a bending moment $M$.
- $dV/dx = -w(x)$: the shear falls at the rate of the load. It jumps at a point load.
- $dM/dx = V(x)$: the shear is the slope of the moment diagram. The moment is largest where the shear passes through zero, or at a fixed support.
- Largest moment: at the fixed support of a cantilever, under the load where the shear changes sign on a simply supported beam, and often at the support next to an overhang.

**Coming next:** the largest bending moment is the input to the flexure formula $\sigma = My/I$, which gives the bending stress and tells you if the beam can carry its load. That is the subject of [bending stresses in simple beams](/education/mechanics-of-materials/bending-stresses-simple-beams/).
