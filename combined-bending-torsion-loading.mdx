A shaft is checked for bending and passes. It is checked for twisting and passes. In service it still yields, because both loads act on the same metal at the same moment and nobody combined them. The two stresses cannot simply be added, because bending pulls on the metal and twisting slides it. This lesson shows how to find both stresses at the worst point of a shaft and combine them into one number, the von Mises stress, which you compare with the strength of the material. You use it on a drone arm, a wind turbine shaft and a robot gripper finger. #VonMisesStress #CombinedLoading #FailureTheory

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

1. Analyze simultaneous bending and torsional stresses in shaft components
2. Calculate equivalent stresses using von Mises and maximum shear stress theories
3. Apply combined loading analysis to a drone arm, a turbine shaft and a gripper finger
4. Judge a design from its safety factor, and say which load governs it

## Engineering challenge: combined loading in mechanical systems

<InArticleAd />


Few machine parts carry one load only. A drone arm is bent by rotor thrust and twisted by its motor; a turbine shaft is twisted by the power it carries and bent by the rotor weight; a gripper finger is bent by the grip force and twisted by friction.

```text
 fixed end                       force F bends the shaft
   ║                                    │
   ║  A (top surface, fixed end)        ▼
   ║──●─────────────────────────────────┐
   ║              shaft                 │ ↻ torque T
   ║────────────────────────────────────┘   twists it
   ║◄──────────── length L ────────────►│
```

At point A the stress from bending and the stress from twisting are both at their largest, and they act on the same small piece of metal.

> **Engineering Question:** A shaft is bent and twisted at the same time. How do we find the two stresses at its worst point, combine them into one number, and compare that number with the strength of the material?

## Fundamental Theory: Combined stress analysis

<InArticleAd />


The method has three parts: find the stress from each load alone, put both stresses on the same small piece of metal, then combine them into one number.

### Stress from each load

Bending stretches one side of a shaft and squeezes the other. The stress is zero on the neutral axis (the line across the middle of the section that is neither stretched nor squeezed) and largest at the surface.

<Card title="Bending stress" icon="document">

$$\sigma = \frac{Mc}{I}$$

- $\sigma$ (sigma) = normal stress from bending, in MPa (1 MPa = 1 N/mm²)
- $M$ = bending moment at the section (N·mm)
- $c$ = distance from the neutral axis to the surface (mm)
- $I$ = second moment of area of the section (mm⁴), which measures how far the metal sits from the neutral axis

</Card>

Torsion (twisting) slides each cross-section over the next one, which is a shear stress. It is zero at the centre of a round shaft and largest at the surface.

<Card title="Torsional shear stress" icon="document">

$$\tau = \frac{Tr}{J}$$

- $\tau$ (tau) = shear stress from torsion (MPa)
- $T$ = torque (N·mm)
- $r$ = distance from the centre of the shaft (mm)
- $J$ = polar second moment of area (mm⁴). For a round section, solid or hollow, $J = 2I$

</Card>

Each stress is calculated as if the other load were absent, and both then act together: the principle of superposition, which holds while the material is elastic (it returns to its shape when unloaded) and the deflections are small.

Two other loads can be present. A force $P$ along the shaft adds $P/A$ to the normal stress, where $A$ is the cross-section area (mm²). A force across the shaft gives a shear stress of its own, but it is zero at the top and bottom of the section, where the bending stress is largest, so a shaft check usually leaves it out.

### The critical point

```text
               A   top: bending pulls hardest
            ───●───
          ╱         ╲
         │           │
   B ────●─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─●──── neutral axis: no bending stress
         │           │
          ╲         ╱
            ───●───
               C   bottom: bending squeezes hardest

   Torsion gives the same shear at every point of the surface.
```

The check is made at A or C, the surface points farthest from the neutral axis. There one piece of metal carries the largest $\sigma$ and the largest $\tau$ together. At B it carries the shear stress alone.

### Combining the two stresses

A normal stress and a shear stress load the metal in different ways, so $\sigma + \tau$ has no meaning. They combine as two walks at right angles do: 30 m east then 40 m north leave you 50 m from the start, more than either walk and less than their sum. The comparison breaks in one respect: shear brings a metal to yield sooner than its size suggests, so it is counted three times under the square root.

<Tabs>
  <TabItem label="Von Mises">

The von Mises stress is the single pulling stress that brings a ductile metal (one that stretches before it breaks, such as steel or aluminum) as close to yielding as the real combination does.

$$\sigma_{von\ Mises} = \sqrt{\sigma^2 + 3\tau^2}$$

The safety factor ($SF$) is the yield strength $\sigma_{yield}$ divided by this number. It has no unit.

$$SF = \frac{\sigma_{yield}}{\sigma_{von\ Mises}}$$

Tests on ductile metals agree best with this theory, so it is the usual choice in machine design.

  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="Tresca">

The Tresca theory, also called the maximum shear stress theory, says that a ductile metal yields when the largest shear stress on any plane reaches the value it has at yield in a tension test. For bending with torsion:

$$\sigma_{Tresca} = \sqrt{\sigma^2 + 4\tau^2}$$

Shear is counted four times in place of three, so Tresca is never below von Mises, and it is at most 15% above. Use it as a cautious second check.

  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="Maximum normal stress">

Brittle materials, such as cast iron and ceramics, crack when the largest pulling stress on any plane reaches the ultimate strength of the material. That largest pulling stress is the first principal stress:

$$\sigma_1 = \frac{\sigma}{2} + \sqrt{\left(\frac{\sigma}{2}\right)^2 + \tau^2}$$

Do not use this theory for ductile metals. It misses yielding that shear causes.

  </TabItem>
</Tabs>

These formulas are for one normal stress and one shear stress, which is the case at the surface of a shaft. [Principal Stresses and Failure Analysis](/education/mechanics-of-materials/principal-stresses-failure-analysis/) covers the general case and shows where the 3 and the 4 come from.

How large should the safety factor be? For a steady, well-known load, 1.5 to 3. For loads that repeat or shock, 3 to 6, because a part can crack from fatigue (damage from millions of load cycles) at a stress far below yield.

:::tip[Sizing a solid round shaft]
For a solid shaft of diameter $d$, put $\sigma = 32M/(\pi d^3)$ and $\tau = 16T/(\pi d^3)$ into the von Mises formula and solve for the diameter:

$$d = \sqrt[3]{\frac{32 \cdot SF}{\pi \, \sigma_{yield}} \sqrt{M^2 + 0.75\,T^2}}$$
:::

## Application 1: Drone arm design (mechatronics)

<InArticleAd />


A quadcopter carries each motor and propeller on a hollow carbon fiber arm, bent by the rotor thrust and twisted about its own axis by the motor reaction.

:::note[System Problem Statement]
- **Support:** fixed at the drone body, free at the motor end (a cantilever)
- **Section:** hollow round tube, outside diameter $D = 20$ mm, inside diameter $d = 16$ mm
- **Length:** $L = 300$ mm from the body to the motor
- **Loads:** thrust force $F = 50$ N across the arm at its free end, and motor torque $T = 3$ N·m about the axis of the arm
- **Material:** carbon fiber composite, limit stress $\sigma_{yield} = 400$ MPa
- **Target:** a safety factor of 3 to 5, usual for a drone arm with vibration and landing shocks

**Key Question:** Can the arm carry the thrust and the motor torque together with a safety factor of at least 3?
:::

```text
 drone body                         F = 50 N
   ║                                    │
   ║  A                                 ▼
   ║──●─────────────────────────────────┐
   ║  hollow tube, D = 20 mm, d = 16 mm │ ↻ T = 3 N·m
   ║────────────────────────────────────┘
   ║◄─────────── L = 300 mm ───────────►│
```

Carbon fiber does not yield as a metal does, and its strength depends on the direction of the fibers. Here the 400 MPa limit is used as if it were a yield strength, which is good for a first estimate only.

After Step 2, enter the bending sigma and the torsional tau into the Mohr's circle simulator to read the principal stresses and the safety factor:

### Step 1: Calculate section properties

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal section property calculations**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Second moment of area** of the hollow tube, for the bending stress. It is the value for the outside circle minus the value for the hole:

   $$I = \frac{\pi(D^4 - d^4)}{64} = \frac{\pi(20^4 - 16^4)\ \text{mm}^4}{64} = \frac{\pi \times 94{,}464}{64} = 4{,}637 \text{ mm}^4$$ ✅

2. **Polar second moment of area**, for the torsional stress. For a round section it is twice $I$:

   $$J = 2I = 2 \times 4{,}637 = 9{,}274 \text{ mm}^4$$ ✅

3. **Distance to the surface.** Both stresses are largest at the outside radius: $c = r = D/2 = 10$ mm. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 2: Calculate internal forces and individual stresses

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal stress component calculations**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Bending moment at the fixed end.** The force acts at the free end, so its moment is largest at the drone body: force times length.

   $$M = F \times L = 50 \text{ N} \times 300 \text{ mm} = 15{,}000 \text{ N·mm} \ (15 \text{ N·m})$$ ✅

2. **Bending stress at the surface**, from $\sigma = Mc/I$. It is tension on the top of the tube and compression on the bottom:

   $$\sigma = \frac{15{,}000 \text{ N·mm} \times 10 \text{ mm}}{4{,}637 \text{ mm}^4} = 32.3 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

3. **Torsional shear stress at the surface**, from $\tau = Tr/J$, with 3 N·m written as 3,000 N·mm:

   $$\tau = \frac{3{,}000 \text{ N·mm} \times 10 \text{ mm}}{9{,}274 \text{ mm}^4} = 3.23 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

4. **Critical point.** Point A, on the tension side at the fixed end, carries $\sigma = 32.3$ MPa and $\tau = 3.23$ MPa together. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 3: Apply von Mises failure theory

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal von Mises equivalent stress calculation**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Stress state at point A:** $\sigma = 32.3$ MPa and $\tau = 3.23$ MPa. No other stress acts on the surface of the tube. ✅

2. **Von Mises stress**, the two stresses combined with shear counted three times:

   $$\sigma_{von\ Mises} = \sqrt{\sigma^2 + 3\tau^2} = \sqrt{(32.3)^2 + 3(3.23)^2}\ \text{MPa}$$

   $$\sigma_{von\ Mises} = \sqrt{1{,}043.29 + 31.30} = \sqrt{1{,}074.59} = 32.8 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

3. **Read the result.** The arm is as close to its limit as a bar pulled with a plain stress of 32.8 MPa. The torque raised the number by only 0.5 MPa above the bending stress. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 4: Safety factor assessment and design evaluation

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal safety factor analysis**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Safety factor**, the limit stress over the von Mises stress:

   $$SF = \frac{\sigma_{yield}}{\sigma_{von\ Mises}} = \frac{400 \text{ MPa}}{32.8 \text{ MPa}} = 12.2$$ ✅

2. **Compare with the target.** The target was 3 to 5. At 12.2 the arm uses $32.8/400 = 8.2\%$ of the limit stress, so it is safe and heavier than it must be. ✅

3. **Share of bending.** Under the square root, bending gives $\sigma^2$ and torsion gives $3\tau^2$:

   $$\frac{\sigma^2}{\sigma^2 + 3\tau^2} = \frac{1{,}043.29}{1{,}074.59} = 97.1\%$$ ✅

4. **Share of torsion.** Bending governs this arm, and a change to the motor torque would hardly move the result:

   $$\frac{3\tau^2}{\sigma^2 + 3\tau^2} = \frac{31.30}{1{,}074.59} = 2.9\%$$ ✅

5. **Design decision.** A wall of 1.5 mm in place of 2 mm raises the stress by about 24% and still leaves a safety factor near 9.9. Keep the thicker wall if crash loads, vibration and fatigue matter more than weight: this safety factor covers a steady load only. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

## Application 2: Wind turbine main shaft (mechanical)

<InArticleAd />


The main shaft of a wind turbine carries the rotor's power to the gearbox and holds the rotor's weight, which hangs beyond the front bearing: it is twisted by the power and bent by the weight.

<div style={{ display: 'flex', justifyContent: 'center', width: '100%' }}>
  <TailwindWrapper>
	![Cutaway drawing of the top of a wind turbine: the blades and the rotor hub at the front, then the low speed shaft, the brake, the gear box, the high speed shaft and the generator, all on top of the tower. The low speed shaft is the main shaft of this application. The drawing does not show its two bearings](https://cdn.siliconwit.com/education/solid-mechanics/wind-turbine-transmits-power.jpg)
  </TailwindWrapper>
</div>

:::note[System Problem Statement]
- **Support:** bearing A (a pin support) at the rear, and bearing B (a roller support) 1.5 m in front of it. The shaft continues 0.5 m beyond B to the rotor: an overhang
- **Section:** hollow steel shaft, outside diameter $D = 100$ mm, inside diameter $d = 80$ mm
- **Loads:** rotor weight $P_1 = 1200$ N at the tip; weight of the shaft itself, $W = 300$ N for each metre along the whole length $L = 2.0$ m; torque $T = 5000$ N·m along the whole shaft
- **Material:** AISI 1045 steel, $\sigma_{yield} = 310$ MPa
- **Target:** a safety factor of 2.5 to 4, usual for a rotating shaft whose loads repeat for 20 years

**Key Question:** Can the shaft carry the torque and the weight on the overhang together without yielding?
:::

<div style={{ display: 'flex', justifyContent: 'center', width: '100%' }}>
  <TailwindWrapper>
	![Beam diagram of the shaft: support A, a pin, at the left end; support B, a roller, about three quarters of the way along; a distributed load W over the whole length; a point load P1 at the right tip; and the total length L marked below](https://cdn.siliconwit.com/education/solid-mechanics/solar-tracker-arm-loading-diagram.png)
  </TailwindWrapper>
</div>

The diagram has no dimensions: from A to B is 1.5 m, from B to $P_1$ is 0.5 m, and $L = 2.0$ m.

### Step 1: Calculate support reactions and determine critical section

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal support reactions and critical section analysis**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Reaction at B.** Take moments about A, so that the unknown reaction at A drops out. The weight of the shaft, $W \times L = 300 \times 2.0 = 600$ N, acts at the middle of the shaft, 1.0 m from A:

   $$R_B \times AB = P_1 \times L + (W \times L) \times \frac{L}{2}$$

   $$R_B = \frac{1200 \text{ N} \times 2.0 \text{ m} + 600 \text{ N} \times 1.0 \text{ m}}{1.5 \text{ m}} = \frac{3000 \text{ N·m}}{1.5 \text{ m}} = 2000 \text{ N (upward)}$$ ✅

2. **Reaction at A.** The upward forces equal the downward forces. The minus sign in the result means that bearing A pushes down with 200 N: the weight on the overhang tries to lift the rear end of the shaft.

   $$R_A = P_1 + W \times L - R_B = 1200 + 600 - 2000 = -200 \text{ N}$$ ✅

3. **Critical section.** The loads beyond B have their longest lever arm at B, so the bending moment is largest there. ✅

4. **Bending moment at B.** Look at the overhang alone. The rotor weight acts 0.5 m from B. The weight of the overhang itself, $300 \times 0.5 = 150$ N, acts at its middle, 0.25 m from B:

   $$M_B = 1200 \text{ N} \times 0.5 \text{ m} + 150 \text{ N} \times 0.25 \text{ m} = 600 + 37.5 = 637.5 \text{ N·m}$$

   This moment bends the shaft downward over the bearing, so the top surface at B is in tension. For the stress, use $637{,}500$ N·mm. ✅

5. **Torque.** It is the same at every section: $T = 5000$ N·m $= 5{,}000{,}000$ N·mm. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 2: Calculate section properties

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal section property calculations**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Second moment of area** of the hollow shaft:

   $$I = \frac{\pi(D^4 - d^4)}{64} = \frac{\pi(100^4 - 80^4)\ \text{mm}^4}{64} = \frac{\pi \times 59{,}040{,}000}{64} = 2.898 \times 10^6 \text{ mm}^4$$ ✅

2. **Polar second moment of area**, twice $I$ for a round section:

   $$J = 2I = 5.796 \times 10^6 \text{ mm}^4$$ ✅

3. **Distance to the surface:** $c = r = D/2 = 50$ mm. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 3: Calculate individual stress components

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal stress calculations at critical section**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Bending stress at the surface**, at B:

   $$\sigma = \frac{Mc}{I} = \frac{637{,}500 \text{ N·mm} \times 50 \text{ mm}}{2.898 \times 10^6 \text{ mm}^4} = 11.0 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

2. **Torsional shear stress at the surface:**

   $$\tau = \frac{Tr}{J} = \frac{5{,}000{,}000 \text{ N·mm} \times 50 \text{ mm}}{5.796 \times 10^6 \text{ mm}^4} = 43.1 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

3. **Critical point.** The top surface at B carries $\sigma = 11.0$ MPa of tension and $\tau = 43.1$ MPa of shear. Here the shear stress is about four times the bending stress, the opposite of the drone arm. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 4: Apply von Mises failure theory

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal von Mises equivalent stress calculation**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Von Mises stress** at the top surface at B:

   $$\sigma_{von\ Mises} = \sqrt{\sigma^2 + 3\tau^2} = \sqrt{(11.0)^2 + 3(43.1)^2}\ \text{MPa}$$

   $$\sigma_{von\ Mises} = \sqrt{121.0 + 5{,}572.83} = \sqrt{5{,}693.83} = 75.5 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

2. **Compare with torsion alone.** With no bending the result would be $\sqrt{3} \times 43.1 = 74.7$ MPa. The weight on the overhang adds less than 1 MPa. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 5: Safety factor assessment and design evaluation

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal safety factor analysis**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Safety factor**, the yield strength over the von Mises stress:

   $$SF = \frac{\sigma_{yield}}{\sigma_{von\ Mises}} = \frac{310 \text{ MPa}}{75.5 \text{ MPa}} = 4.11$$ ✅

2. **Compare with the target.** The target was 2.5 to 4, so 4.11 passes. The shaft uses $75.5/310 = 24.4\%$ of the yield strength. ✅

3. **Share of each load** under the square root:

   $$\text{torsion: } \frac{3 \times 43.1^2}{5{,}693.83} = 97.9\% \qquad \text{bending: } \frac{11.0^2}{5{,}693.83} = 2.1\%$$ ✅

4. **Design decision.** Torque governs, so a stronger shaft needs a larger diameter. Moving the rotor closer to bearing B lowers the bending moment and changes the result very little. This safety factor covers yielding only. The shaft turns, so its bending stress reverses on every turn, and a fatigue check is still needed. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

## Application 3: Robotic gripper finger (mechatronics)

<InArticleAd />


A robot gripper holds a round part between two aluminum fingers: the grip force bends each finger and friction on the part twists it. The finger is a flat bar, and a rectangle does not carry torsion as a round shaft does: its corners carry no shear stress, and the largest is at the middle of the long sides.

:::note[System Problem Statement]
- **Support:** fixed at the palm of the gripper, free at the tip (a cantilever)
- **Section:** rectangle, short side $b = 8$ mm, long side $h = 12$ mm
- **Length:** $L = 60$ mm from the palm to the tip
- **Loads:** grip force $F = 20$ N across the finger at its tip, which bends it across the 12 mm depth; friction torque $T = 0.5$ N·m $= 500$ N·mm about the axis of the finger
- **Material:** aluminum 6061-T6, $\sigma_{yield} = 275$ MPa
- **Target:** a safety factor of 3 to 5, usual for a gripper that closes many thousands of times

**Key Question:** Can the finger carry the grip force and the friction torque together without yielding?
:::

```text
        top face: bending stress largest
              ┌─────●─────┐  ▲
              │           │  │
              │           │  │
 torsion ───► ● ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ● ◄│── neutral axis
 shear        │           │  │   h = 12 mm
 largest      │           │  │
              └─────●─────┘  ▼
              ◄─ b = 8 mm ─►
        corners: no torsion shear
```

The drawing shows the cross-section of the finger at the palm.

### Step 1: Calculate rectangular section properties

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal section property calculations**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Second moment of area** for bending. The finger bends across its 12 mm depth, so $h$ is cubed:

   $$I = \frac{bh^3}{12} = \frac{8 \text{ mm} \times (12 \text{ mm})^3}{12} = \frac{8 \times 1{,}728}{12} = 1{,}152 \text{ mm}^4$$ ✅

2. **Distance to the surface:** $c = h/2 = 6$ mm. ✅

3. **Torsion coefficients.** The formula $\tau = Tr/J$ is for round sections only. For a rectangle, tables give two coefficients that depend on the ratio of the sides:

   | $h/b$ | 1.0 | 1.2 | 1.5 | 2.0 | 3.0 |
   |-------|-----|-----|-----|-----|-----|
   | $\alpha$ (alpha, for stress) | 0.208 | 0.219 | 0.231 | 0.246 | 0.267 |
   | $\beta$ (beta, for twist) | 0.141 | 0.166 | 0.196 | 0.229 | 0.263 |

   Here $h/b = 12/8 = 1.5$, so $\alpha = 0.231$ and $\beta = 0.196$. ✅

4. **Torsional constant** $K$, which takes the place of $J$ when you need the angle of twist ($b$ is the short side). The stress does not need $K$: it uses $\alpha$ in Step 3.

   $$K = \beta\, h\, b^3 = 0.196 \times 12 \times 8^3 = 0.196 \times 12 \times 512 = 1{,}204 \text{ mm}^4$$ ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 2: Calculate bending moment and bending stress

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal bending stress calculations**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Bending moment at the fixed end**, force times length:

   $$M = F \times L = 20 \text{ N} \times 60 \text{ mm} = 1{,}200 \text{ N·mm}$$ ✅

2. **Bending stress** at the top and bottom faces (the 8 mm faces), at the fixed end:

   $$\sigma = \frac{Mc}{I} = \frac{1{,}200 \text{ N·mm} \times 6 \text{ mm}}{1{,}152 \text{ mm}^4} = 6.25 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 3: Calculate torsional shear stress (rectangular section)

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal torsional stress calculations for rectangular section**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Largest shear stress**, at the middle of each long side. For a rectangle the torque is divided by $\alpha b^2 h$, with $b$ the short side:

   $$\tau_{max} = \frac{T}{\alpha\, b^2 h} = \frac{500 \text{ N·mm}}{0.231 \times (8 \text{ mm})^2 \times 12 \text{ mm}} = \frac{500}{177.4} = 2.82 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

2. **Shear stress at the middle of each short side.** It is lower. For $h/b = 1.5$ it is about 0.86 of the largest value:

   $$\tau_{short} = 0.86 \times 2.82 = 2.42 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 4: Identify critical stress location

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal critical location analysis**</summary>

The largest bending stress and the largest shear stress are at different points, so check both.

<Steps>

1. **Middle of the top or bottom face.** The bending stress is largest here, with the lower shear stress:

   $$\sigma_{von\ Mises} = \sqrt{6.25^2 + 3(2.42)^2} = 7.53 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

2. **Middle of a side face.** This point is on the neutral axis, so it has no bending stress, with the largest shear stress:

   $$\sigma_{von\ Mises} = \sqrt{3} \times 2.82 = 4.88 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

3. **Values for design.** The top and bottom faces govern. The usual cautious shortcut takes both largest values as if they were at one point: $\sigma = 6.25$ MPa with $\tau = 2.82$ MPa. The result is about 5% above the true 7.53 MPa. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 5: Apply von Mises failure theory

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal von Mises equivalent stress calculation**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Von Mises stress** with the two largest values:

   $$\sigma_{von\ Mises} = \sqrt{(6.25)^2 + 3(2.82)^2} = \sqrt{39.06 + 23.86} = \sqrt{62.92} = 7.93 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

2. **Share of each load** under the square root. Neither load governs alone here, so both must be in the check:

   $$\text{bending: } \frac{39.06}{62.92} = 62.1\% \qquad \text{torsion: } \frac{23.86}{62.92} = 37.9\%$$ ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 6: Safety factor assessment and design evaluation

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal safety factor analysis**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Safety factor**, the yield strength over the von Mises stress:

   $$SF = \frac{\sigma_{yield}}{\sigma_{von\ Mises}} = \frac{275 \text{ MPa}}{7.93 \text{ MPa}} = 34.7$$ ✅

2. **Compare with the target.** The target was 3 to 5. The finger uses $7.93/275 = 2.9\%$ of the yield strength, far less than the strength check allows. ✅

3. **A smaller section.** With $b = 5$ mm and $h = 8$ mm ($\alpha \approx 0.234$ for $h/b = 1.6$), the same steps give $\sigma = 22.5$ MPa, $\tau = 10.7$ MPa and $\sigma_{von\ Mises} = 29.1$ MPa, so $SF = 9.4$. ✅

4. **Design decision.** Stress rises in step with the load, so both loads could be $34.7/5.0 = 6.9$ times as large before $SF$ falls to 5. But other limits usually size a finger: how far it bends, wear, an accidental knock, and fatigue from many grip cycles. Check those before you remove metal. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

:::tip[Instrument it]
No single strain gauge can read a combined stress, but a rosette can: three gauges on one patch, at 0°, 45° and 90°. Bond it to the shaft surface at the critical section. Its three strains give, through Hooke's law, the normal stress and the shear stress that this lesson combines. Read the three channels with an [ESP32 or STM32](/education/sensor-actuator-interfacing-stm32/) and you can [trend the von Mises stress on siliconwit.io](https://siliconwit.io) and set an alarm well below the yield strength.
:::

## Summary and Next Steps

<InArticleAd />


### Key concepts

1. **Bending** gives a normal stress $\sigma = Mc/I$ and **torsion** gives a shear stress $\tau = Tr/J$. Both are largest at the surface, and each is found as if the other load were absent.
2. **The von Mises stress** $\sqrt{\sigma^2 + 3\tau^2}$ combines them into one number for a ductile metal. The safety factor is the yield strength divided by it.
3. **One load usually governs.** Bending gave 97% of the result in the drone arm, and torsion gave 98% in the turbine shaft. Find which one before you change a design.
4. **A rectangle in torsion** needs coefficients from a table, and its largest shear stress is at the middle of the long sides.
