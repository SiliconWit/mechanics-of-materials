## Chapter 2 assignments: structural behavior in motion and force transfer

A machine part can pass a stress check and still be useless: an arm that bends too far, a spindle that runs out of true, a shaft that cracks after one day of work. Each of these five assignments gives you one machine and asks which requirement decides its design. #StructuralAnalysis #BeamDesign #FailureAnalysis

## Assignment 1: Industrial robotic manipulator structural analysis

<InArticleAd />


### System description

A robot with six joints (6-DOF) lifts car parts on an assembly line without rest. Its main arm, held at one end, is a cantilever; if it bends too far, the tool at its tip (the end-effector) misses its target. The robot must place a 150 kg payload to within $\pm 0.1\ \text{mm}$.

**Given:**
- Main arm: cantilever, length 3.2 m, mass 85 kg/m
- At its tip: payload 150 kg, end-effector 25 kg, secondary arm (1.8 m long) 120 kg
- Dynamic amplification factor 1.5, torque from the wrist joint 2500 N·m
- Steel: $\sigma_{\text{yield}} = 350\ \text{MPa}$, $E = 200\ \text{GPa}$
- Cross-section: hollow rectangle, 200 mm tall × 150 mm wide, wall 8 mm

:::tip[Is your answer reasonable?]
Expect a moment of about 20 kN·m at the fixed end, a bending stress near 67 MPa and a tip deflection of several millimetres. Stiffness, not strength, decides this arm.
:::

### Part A: Shear force and bending moment analysis (25 points)

<Steps>
1. **Loads.** Calculate the distributed load from the weight of the arm and the point loads from the payload, the end-effector and the secondary arm. Multiply all of them by the dynamic factor.

2. **Diagrams.** Draw the shear force and bending moment diagrams of the main arm, and mark the cross-section that the stress analysis must use.

3. **Critical values.** Give the largest shear force and the largest bending moment with their positions, and check them with equilibrium.
</Steps>

### Part B: Bending stress analysis (25 points)

<Steps>
1. **Cross-section.** Calculate the second moment of area and the section modulus of the hollow rectangle, and locate the neutral axis. Allow for stress concentration at the connections.

2. **Stresses.** Calculate the largest tensile and compressive bending stresses, draw their distribution across the critical section and compare them with the yield strength.

3. **Safety factor.** Calculate the safety factor on yield, judge if it suits an industrial robot, and recommend changes if it does not.
</Steps>

### Part C: Deflection and precision analysis (25 points)

<Steps>
1. **Deflection.** Calculate the deflection at the end-effector by superposition, and the slope at the tip. Include bending and shear deformation.

2. **Precision.** Compare the deflection with the $\pm 0.1\ \text{mm}$ requirement, and estimate the effect of thermal expansion on the position.

3. **Design.** Calculate the second moment of area that would meet the requirement, propose a change, and weigh the added stiffness against the added weight.
</Steps>

### Part D: Combined loading analysis (25 points)

<Steps>
1. **Bending with torsion.** Add the torque from the wrist joint and calculate the von Mises equivalent stress at the critical point.

2. **Failure prediction.** Choose a failure theory for ductile steel, compare the equivalent stress with the yield strength, and comment on fatigue in continuous operation.

3. **Joints.** Examine the stress concentration at the bearing connections, design the fillets and transitions, and check the bolted joint at the mounting.
</Steps>

**What to hand in:**
- All calculations, with free body diagrams
- Shear force and bending moment diagrams with the critical values
- Stress distribution across the critical cross-section
- Deflection compared with the precision requirement
- Design recommendations with their calculations

## Assignment 2: CNC machine tool spindle system

<InArticleAd />


### System description

The spindle of a CNC milling machine is the shaft that turns the cutting tool. It runs in two bearings, with the tool hanging out beyond the front one; when the cutting force bends it, the tool tip leaves its path and the part is cut to the wrong size. This error, the runout, must stay below $5\ \mu\text{m}$ at 20,000 RPM.

**Given:**
- 800 mm between the bearings, tool overhang 150 mm beyond the front bearing
- Cutting force at the tool tip: 5000 N radial (across the spindle), 2000 N axial (along it)
- Residual unbalance 0.1 g·mm
- Tool steel: $\sigma_{\text{yield}} = 1200\ \text{MPa}$, $E = 210\ \text{GPa}$
- The spindle diameter is not given. Choose it and state it

:::tip[Is your answer reasonable?]
The moment at the front bearing is $5000 \times 0.15 = 750\ \text{N·m}$. A solid 60 mm spindle has a stress of only 35 MPa, but its tool tip moves about 270 μm. For 5 μm with rigid bearings the diameter must be near 160 mm. The unbalance force is below 1 N.
:::

### Part A: Dynamic loading analysis (30 points)

<Steps>
1. **Cutting forces.** Model the cutting forces as loads on the spindle, allow for dynamic amplification, and find the reactions at the two bearings.

2. **Rotation.** Calculate the force from the residual unbalance at 20,000 RPM, and estimate the gyroscopic moment when the machine accelerates.

3. **Critical speed.** Calculate the first critical speed of the spindle, the speed at which it resonates in bending. Show that the operating speed is safely below it, and show how the mass of the tool changes it.
</Steps>

### Part B: Stress and deflection analysis (35 points)

<Steps>
1. **Bending stress.** Calculate the largest bending stress in the spindle and how it changes along the length. Include the stress concentration at the bearing seats and at the tool holder.

2. **Tool tip deflection.** Calculate the deflection at the tool tip by beam theory, add the effect of bearings that are not rigid, and compare the result with the $5\ \mu\text{m}$ runout limit.

3. **Thermal effects.** Estimate how much the spindle grows from bearing heat at high speed, and the stress from a temperature difference along it.
</Steps>

### Part C: Bearing design integration (35 points)

<Steps>
1. **Bearing loads.** Calculate the radial and axial loads on the front and rear bearings, choose bearing types, and calculate their $L_{10}$ life (the life that 90% of such bearings reach).

2. **Stiffness.** Combine the stiffness of the bearings and of the spindle, show the effect of bearing preload, and choose the arrangement that is stiffest.

3. **Vibration.** Calculate the natural frequencies of the spindle on its bearings, name the speeds that could excite them, and propose damping.
</Steps>

**What to hand in:**
- Force diagrams
- Stress and deflection with the safety factor
- Bearing selection and life
- Critical speed
- Thermal growth of the spindle

## Assignment 3: Automated overhead crane system

<InArticleAd />


### System description

An overhead crane in an automated warehouse lifts loads with a trolley that runs along a bridge girder, a beam on a support at each end. The load moves, so you must find the trolley position that is worst for the girder; the crane works without rest, so the girder must also survive 2 million load cycles without a fatigue crack.

**Given:**
- Girder: steel I-beam, simply supported, span 25 m
- Lifting capacity 50 tons, trolley 8 tons (1 ton = 1000 kg)
- Steel: $\sigma_{\text{yield}} = 250\ \text{MPa}$, $E = 200\ \text{GPa}$
- Heavy duty, 2 million cycles
- The girder section and the impact and load factors are not given. Choose them from your design code and state them

:::tip[Is your answer reasonable?]
The trolley and full load weigh $58 \times 9.81 = 569\ \text{kN}$. At midspan the moment is $PL/4 = 3556\ \text{kN·m}$, before the weight of the girder and any factor. At 250 MPa this needs a section modulus of $14.2 \times 10^6\ \text{mm}^3$, so your section must be larger.
:::

### Part A: Moving load analysis (25 points)

<Steps>
1. **Load cases.** Find the moment with the trolley and full load at midspan, then show how the moment under the trolley changes as it crosses the span. Choose an impact factor.

2. **Influence lines.** Draw the influence line for the largest positive moment (the moment at one section against the trolley position), and find the trolley position for the largest shear.

3. **Load combinations.** Combine the weight of the girder, the moving load and the dynamic effect, consider wind and earthquake, and apply the load factors of your design code.
</Steps>

### Part B: Beam design and analysis (25 points)

<Steps>
1. **Cross-section.** Select an I-beam for the moment, calculate its second moment of area $I$, section modulus $S$ and radius of gyration $r$, and check strength and deflection.

2. **Lateral-torsional buckling.** A deep girder can twist and move sideways before it yields. Calculate the reduced moment capacity and the spacing of the lateral bracing.

3. **Local buckling.** Check the width to thickness ratios of the web and flanges against your code, and design web stiffeners if needed.
</Steps>

### Part C: Fatigue analysis (25 points)

<Steps>
1. **Stress range.** Calculate the stress range in each cycle as the trolley moves, and name the details where a fatigue crack could start.

2. **Fatigue life.** Use the S-N curve for each detail (stress range against the number of cycles to failure) and Miner's rule for the damage that adds up, and show that the life is more than 2 million cycles.

3. **Improvements.** Design smooth transitions, specify the welding and inspection, and recommend maintenance for the details that fatigue decides.
</Steps>

### Part D: Control system integration (25 points)

<Steps>
1. **Deflection.** Calculate the deflection limit that automated positioning needs, and describe how to measure the deflection in service and correct for it in the control.

2. **Dynamic response.** Estimate the natural frequencies of the girder, and choose acceleration and braking profiles that keep the dynamic effect small.

3. **Safety.** Describe the overload protection and the monitoring of the structure, and say how your analysis sets the maintenance plan.
</Steps>

**What to hand in:**
- Moving load analysis with influence lines
- Beam design with the code checks
- Fatigue life
- Dynamic response
- Safety system recommendations

## Assignment 4: Hybrid manufacturing platform structure

<InArticleAd />


### System description

A hybrid machine prints a part in 3D and then mills it, on one bed. The bed is a composite beam: an aluminum casting stiffened by steel sections. The two metals share the load by their stiffness and expand by different amounts when the machine warms. If the bed bends or warps, every part made on it is out of tolerance.

**Given:**
- Bed length 4.5 m
- Steel: W21×68 sections (depth 537 mm, $I = 616 \times 10^6\ \text{mm}^4$), $E_{\text{steel}} = 200\ \text{GPa}$
- Aluminum casting: 6061-T6, $E_{\text{aluminum}} = 69\ \text{GPa}$
- Operating range 20 °C to 80 °C. Expansion per °C: steel $12 \times 10^{-6}$, aluminum $23.6 \times 10^{-6}$ (handbook values)
- Vibration amplitude below $1\ \mu\text{m}$ at cutting frequencies
- The size of the casting and the loads are not given. Choose them and state them

:::tip[Is your answer reasonable?]
The modular ratio is $n = 200/69 = 2.9$. Over 60 °C, 4.5 m of free aluminum grows about 3.1 mm more than 4.5 m of free steel. The joint must take up this difference or resist it.
:::

### Part A: Composite section analysis (30 points)

<Steps>
1. **Transformed section.** Calculate the modular ratio $n = E_{\text{steel}}/E_{\text{aluminum}}$, replace the aluminum by the steel area that is equally stiff, and locate the neutral axis of the composite section.

2. **Section properties.** Calculate the second moment of area of the transformed section with the parallel axis theorem, and the section modulus for the steel and for the aluminum.

3. **Interface.** Calculate the shear stress where the steel meets the aluminum and the bond strength it needs. Design bolts or studs if an adhesive is not strong enough.
</Steps>

### Part B: Stress analysis under service loads (35 points)

<Steps>
1. **Loads.** Model the loads on the bed from the printing process, from the machining forces and from the weight of the machine parts (spindles, linear guides).

2. **Stresses.** Calculate the bending stress in the steel and in the aluminum, and show that both stay within their allowable limits.

3. **Interface shear.** Calculate the horizontal shear force at the interface, design the shear connectors, and check for slip and separation.
</Steps>

### Part C: Thermal analysis (35 points)

<Steps>
1. **Expansion.** Calculate how much more the aluminum expands than the steel, and the stress this causes when the two are joined. Design expansion joints or flexible connections where they are needed.

2. **Distortion.** Calculate how the bed bends when it warms, state the effect on the tolerance of the parts, and propose a way to compensate.

3. **Thermal cycling.** Calculate the stress range from cycles between 20 °C and 80 °C and the fatigue life, and reduce the restraint that causes the stress.
</Steps>

**What to hand in:**
- Transformed section properties
- Stresses in both materials under service loads
- Thermal expansion and distortion
- Interface design for shear and thermal movement
- Thermal compensation recommendations

## Assignment 5: Mechatronic joint failure analysis and redesign

<InArticleAd />


### System description

A shaft in a joint of an assembly robot broke in service, and not from one overload. It cracked by fatigue: a small crack started at the step where the shaft changes diameter and the stress is concentrated, and grew with every load cycle. Find out why it failed after 180,000 cycles, then design a joint that lasts 2 million.

**Given:**
- 4140 steel, heat-treated: $\sigma_{\text{yield}} = 850\ \text{MPa}$, $\sigma_{\text{ultimate}} = 1000\ \text{MPa}$
- Bending moment $M = 250{,}000\ \text{N·mm}$, fully reversed in every cycle ($\pm M$)
- Torque $T = 130{,}000\ \text{N·mm}$, steady
- Stepped shaft, $d = 25\ \text{mm}$ and $D = 50\ \text{mm}$, fillet radius at the step $r = 1\ \text{mm}$, machined surface
- 2 Hz, continuous. Failed at the root of the step after 180,000 cycles

:::tip[Is your answer reasonable?]
In the 25 mm section the nominal bending stress is $32M/(\pi d^3) = 163\ \text{MPa}$ and the nominal shear stress is $16T/(\pi d^3) = 42\ \text{MPa}$. The sharp fillet multiplies the bending stress by more than 2. At 2 Hz, 180,000 cycles is only 25 hours of work. A stress of a few tens of MPa cannot explain this failure.
:::

### Part A: Failure analysis investigation (25 points)

<Steps>
1. **Stress concentration.** Find the stress concentration factor $K_t$ of the step from a chart, then the fatigue factor $K_f$ from the notch sensitivity of the material, and calculate the stress at the place of failure.

2. **Principal stresses.** Draw Mohr's circle for the combined bending and torsion, and give the principal stresses, the largest shear stress and the angle of the principal planes.

3. **Fatigue life.** Calculate the stress amplitude (from the reversed bending) and the mean stress (from the steady torque). Predict the life with the S-N curve of 4140 steel and the Goodman or Gerber criterion, and compare it with the real 180,000 cycles.
</Steps>

### Part B: Failure mode identification (25 points)

<Steps>
1. **Crack start.** From your stress analysis, say where the crack most probably started and in which direction it first grew. Include the effect of the surface finish.

2. **Crack growth.** Calculate the stress intensity factor of the growing crack and estimate the growth life with the Paris law.

3. **Final fracture.** Find the crack size at which the rest of the section breaks, and say if the failure was sudden or gradual. Consider corrosion and temperature.
</Steps>

### Part C: Redesign solutions (25 points)

<Steps>
1. **Geometry.** Choose a larger fillet radius, or another shape such as a taper or several small steps, and calculate its stress concentration factor.

2. **Material.** Compare other materials and surface treatments (shot peening, case hardening, coatings) for fatigue strength against their cost.

3. **Final design.** Choose the geometry with the lowest stress concentration that can still be made and that fits the space and weight limits of the joint.
</Steps>

### Part D: Verification and validation (25 points)

<Steps>
1. **Stress in the new design.** Calculate the stress, show how far the stress concentration fell, and give the safety factor.

2. **Fatigue life.** Predict the life of the new joint and compare it with the requirement of 2 million cycles, with a safety factor that suits a critical part.

3. **Test plan.** Plan an accelerated fatigue test, and specify the inspection and the monitoring in service.
</Steps>

**What to hand in:**
- Failure analysis that names the root cause
- Principal stresses with Mohr's circle
- The redesign, with its stress concentration factor
- Fatigue life of the old and the new design
- Test plan for the new joint
