A steel rod inside an aluminum tube, a steel core in a light column: when two materials carry one load together, they do not share it by their size. The stiffer material takes more than its share of the area, and it can reach its yield strength while the other is hardly loaded. This lesson shows how to find the share of each material when they work side by side, and the stretch of each when they are joined end to end. You will work three parts: a two-section actuator rod, a steel-core aluminum-tube column and a stepped tie rod. #CompoundBars #LoadSharing #MultiMaterial

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

1. **Distinguish** between parallel (equal-deformation) and series (equal-force) compound bar arrangements
2. **Apply** equilibrium and deformation compatibility to find the load carried by each material in a parallel system
3. **Calculate** the stress in each segment and the common deflection when materials share the same shortening
4. **Sum** segment elongations to find total deflection in a series compound bar carrying the same axial force throughout

## Real-World System Problem: Multi-material actuator and column design

<InArticleAd />


Machines combine materials on purpose: a steel core carries most of a load while an aluminum tube around it adds area at low weight, or a rod is steel where space is tight and aluminum where weight matters. A bar of two or more materials is a compound bar.

### The compound bar problem

> **Engineering Question:** One axial load acts on a part made of two materials. How much of the load does each material carry, and how much does each one deform?

The answer depends on how the materials are joined: side by side (parallel) or end to end (series).

## Fundamental Theory: Parallel and series compound bars

<InArticleAd />


**What this lesson takes from the earlier ones.** Stress is force over area, $\sigma = F/A$, in MPa. Strain $\varepsilon$ is change in length over length. Hooke's law, $\sigma = E\varepsilon$, links them through Young's modulus $E$, the stiffness of the material in GPa. A uniform bar changes length by $\delta = FL/(AE)$. The safety factor (SF), also called the factor of safety, is yield strength over stress. See [Fundamental Stress Concepts](/education/mechanics-of-materials/fundamental-stress-concepts/).

Think of each bar as a stiff spring. Two springs side by side under one pressed plate are squeezed by the same amount, and the stiffer one pushes back harder, so it carries more of the load. Two springs joined end to end and pulled carry the same force, and the softer one stretches more. A compound bar behaves the same way while every material stays below its yield strength.

![Panel (a): a parallel compound bar. Two bars of different materials lie side by side between two rigid plates, and a load P presses on one plate. A cut through both bars shows the two internal forces F1 and F2, which add to P. Both bars shorten by the same delta. Panel (b): a series compound bar. Two segments are joined end to end, fixed at the left and pulled by F at the right. Cuts in both segments show the same internal force F, and the two deflections add](./images/compound-bar-parallel-series.svg)

For each panel, ask two questions: what is the same in both materials, and what adds up? In parallel the deformation is the same and the forces add. In series the force is the same and the deformations add.

### Case 1: Parallel arrangement (equal deformation)

The usual example is a steel rod inside an aluminum sleeve, squeezed between the same two rigid end plates. The plates stay flat, so neither material can shorten more than the other. This condition, that the parts still fit together after they deform, is compatibility of deformation. The second condition is equilibrium: the forces in the two materials add up to the applied load.

<Card title="Parallel: compatibility and load split" icon="document">
Compatibility: both materials shorten by $\delta$ (mm) over the same length $L$ (mm), so they have the same strain:

$$\varepsilon = \frac{\delta}{L} \quad \text{(same for all materials)}$$

Hooke's law then gives the stress in material $i$, which is different for each material:

$$\sigma_i = E_i \, \varepsilon$$

Equilibrium: the forces $F_i$ (N), each a stress times an area $A_i$ (mm²), add up to the applied load $P_{total}$ (N):

$$P_{total} = \sum_i F_i = \sum_i \sigma_i A_i = \varepsilon \sum_i E_i A_i$$

Solve for the strain, and the force in material $i$ follows:

$$F_i = P_{total} \cdot \frac{E_i A_i}{\sum_j E_j A_j}$$

In words: each material carries the part of the load that its $EA$ is of the total $EA$. The product $EA$ is the **axial stiffness** of the material.
</Card>

:::note[Why the stiffer material carries more]
With $E = 200$ GPa for steel and $E = 70$ GPa for aluminum, a steel bar carries $200/70 \approx 2.86$ times the load of an aluminum bar of the same area beside it. The aluminum carries an equal share only if its area is 2.86 times as large.
:::

### Case 2: Series arrangement (equal force)

In a series compound bar the segments are joined end to end. Cut the bar anywhere between the loads: the part on one side is held in balance only by the force on the cut face, so that force equals the applied force $F$ in every segment. Each segment stretches by its own amount, and the stretches add.

<Card title="Series: same force, deflections add" icon="document">
For each segment $i$ with length $L_i$, area $A_i$, and modulus $E_i$, the stress is force over area and the stretch is the uniform-bar formula:

$$\sigma_i = \frac{F}{A_i}$$

$$\delta_i = \frac{F L_i}{A_i E_i}$$

Total elongation:

$$\delta_{total} = \sum_i \delta_i = F \sum_i \frac{L_i}{A_i E_i}$$

The force is the same in every segment, but each segment has its own stress, because the areas differ.
</Card>

:::tip[Checking which case applies]
Both materials fixed at both ends to the same rigid plates, so that neither can shorten more than the other: parallel. One material joined to the end of the other, so that the load passes through one and then the other: series.
:::

## Application 1: Linear actuator rod with steel and aluminum sections

<InArticleAd />


A linear actuator rod has two sections joined end to end: a short steel section where space is tight and a longer aluminum section that saves weight. One end is held and the other pulled, so the same force passes through both: a series arrangement.

:::note[System Problem Statement]
- Steel section: length $L_1 = 200$ mm, diameter $d_1 = 25$ mm, Steel 1045, $E_1 = 200$ GPa, $\sigma_{y1} = 530$ MPa
- Aluminum section: length $L_2 = 300$ mm, diameter $d_2 = 30$ mm, Aluminum 6061-T6, $E_2 = 70$ GPa, $\sigma_{y2} = 270$ MPa
- Applied tensile force: $F = 20{,}000$ N
- Required safety factor: 3.0

**Key Question:** What is the stress in each section, and how much does the rod stretch in total?
:::

```text
 fixed                                       F = 20,000 N
  ┃┌──────────────┬───────────────────────┐
  ┃│ steel        │ aluminum              │ ───────►
  ┃│ d1 = 25 mm   │ d2 = 30 mm            │
  ┃└──────────────┴───────────────────────┘
  ┃◄ L1 = 200 mm ►│◄──── L2 = 300 mm ────►│
```

### Step 1: Cross-sectional areas

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the area calculations**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Steel section area**, the area of a circle of diameter $d_1$:

   $$A_1 = \frac{\pi d_1^2}{4} = \frac{\pi (25 \text{ mm})^2}{4} = 490.9 \text{ mm}^2$$ ✅

2. **Aluminum section area:**

   $$A_2 = \frac{\pi d_2^2}{4} = \frac{\pi (30 \text{ mm})^2}{4} = 706.9 \text{ mm}^2$$ ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 2: Axial stiffness and load distribution

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the stiffness and load calculations**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Axial stiffness of each section.** This is the force that stretches the section by 1 mm, $k = AE/L$, in N/mm, with $E$ in MPa (200 GPa = 200,000 MPa):

   $$k_1 = \frac{A_1 E_1}{L_1} = \frac{490.9 \times 200{,}000}{200} = 490{,}900 \text{ N/mm}$$ ✅

   $$k_2 = \frac{A_2 E_2}{L_2} = \frac{706.9 \times 70{,}000}{300} = 164{,}900 \text{ N/mm}$$ ✅

2. **Load in each section.** Cut the rod in either section. The part to the right of the cut is pulled by 20,000 N, so the cut face carries 20,000 N:

   In series, $F_1 = F_2 = 20{,}000$ N. The stiffnesses do not share the force; they share the stretch, and the section with the smaller $k$ stretches more. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 3: Stress and safety factors

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the stress calculations**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Stress in the steel section**, force over area:

   $$\sigma_1 = \frac{F}{A_1} = \frac{20{,}000 \text{ N}}{490.9 \text{ mm}^2} = 40.7 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

2. **Stress in the aluminum section:**

   $$\sigma_2 = \frac{F}{A_2} = \frac{20{,}000 \text{ N}}{706.9 \text{ mm}^2} = 28.3 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

3. **Safety factors**, yield strength over stress, for each material with its own yield strength:

   $$\text{SF}_1 = \frac{530 \text{ MPa}}{40.7 \text{ MPa}} = 13.0 \qquad \text{SF}_2 = \frac{270 \text{ MPa}}{28.3 \text{ MPa}} = 9.5$$ ✅

   Both are far above the required 3.0. The reserve is large because an actuator rod also carries changing loads. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 4: Elongation of each section and total stretch

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the deflection calculation**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Elongation of the steel section**, from $\delta = FL/(AE)$ with N, mm and MPa:

   $$\delta_1 = \frac{F L_1}{A_1 E_1} = \frac{20{,}000 \times 200}{490.9 \times 200{,}000} = 0.0407 \text{ mm}$$ ✅

2. **Elongation of the aluminum section:**

   $$\delta_2 = \frac{F L_2}{A_2 E_2} = \frac{20{,}000 \times 300}{706.9 \times 70{,}000} = 0.1213 \text{ mm}$$ ✅

3. **Total rod elongation**, the sum of the two:

   $$\delta_{total} = \delta_1 + \delta_2 = 0.0407 + 0.1213 = 0.162 \text{ mm}$$ ✅

   The aluminum section stretches three times as much as the steel section under the same force. It is longer, and its stiffness $k_2$ is one third of $k_1$. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

:::note[Engineering Insight]
In an actuator that must position its load closely, 0.16 mm of stretch matters, and most of it comes from the softer section.
:::

## Application 2: Steel core inside an aluminum tube under shared compression

<InArticleAd />


A column is a solid steel rod inside an aluminum tube, both squeezed between the same two rigid end plates, so both shorten by the same amount: a parallel arrangement, with the load shared by axial stiffness.

:::note[System Problem Statement]
- Steel solid rod: diameter $d_s = 30$ mm, $E_s = 200$ GPa, $\sigma_{ys} = 350$ MPa
- Aluminum tube: inner diameter $d_{ai} = 30$ mm, outer diameter $d_{ao} = 50$ mm, $E_a = 70$ GPa, $\sigma_{ya} = 270$ MPa
- Shared length: $L = 250$ mm
- Total compressive load applied to the end plates: $P = 120{,}000$ N

**Key Question:** How is the 120 kN shared between the steel and the aluminum, what is the stress in each, and by how much does the column shorten?
:::

```text
            P = 120,000 N
                  │
                  ▼
  ┌──────────────────────────────┐  rigid end plate
  ├────────┬────────────┬────────┤
  │aluminum│ steel rod  │aluminum│  L = 250 mm
  │  tube  │ d = 30 mm  │  tube  │
  ├────────┴────────────┴────────┤
  └──────────────────────────────┘  rigid end plate
```

In the simulator, a compound steel-and-aluminum bar shows the stiffer steel taking more than its share of the area:

<div style={{ display: 'flex', justifyContent: 'center', width: '100%', margin: '0.5rem 0 1rem' }}>
  <LinkButton href="/product-development/axial-loading-simulator/" target="_blank" variant="primary">Open the Axial Loading Simulator</LinkButton>
</div>

### Step 1: Cross-sectional areas

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the area calculations**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Steel rod area:**

   $$A_s = \frac{\pi d_s^2}{4} = \frac{\pi (30)^2}{4} = 706.9 \text{ mm}^2$$ ✅

2. **Aluminum tube area**, the outer circle minus the hole:

   $$A_a = \frac{\pi (d_{ao}^2 - d_{ai}^2)}{4} = \frac{\pi (50^2 - 30^2)}{4} = \frac{\pi \times 1600}{4} = 1256.6 \text{ mm}^2$$ ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 2: Axial stiffness of each material

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the axial stiffness values**</summary>

<Steps>

1. Both materials have the same length $L$, so the sharing of the load depends only on $EA$. Multiply $E$ in MPa (N/mm²) by the area in mm², and the product is in newtons:

   $$EA_s = 200{,}000 \times 706.9 = 141.4 \times 10^6 \text{ N}$$ ✅

   $$EA_a = 70{,}000 \times 1256.6 = 88.0 \times 10^6 \text{ N}$$ ✅

   $$EA_{total} = 141.4 \times 10^6 + 88.0 \times 10^6 = 229.4 \times 10^6 \text{ N}$$ ✅

2. The aluminum tube has nearly twice the area of the steel rod ($1256.6$ against $706.9$ mm$^2$), but its lower modulus keeps its $EA$ below that of the steel. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 3: Load carried by each material

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the load distribution**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Load on the steel rod**, the total load times the steel's part of the total $EA$:

   $$P_s = P \cdot \frac{EA_s}{EA_{total}} = 120{,}000 \times \frac{141.4}{229.4} = 74{,}000 \text{ N} \quad (61.6\%)$$ ✅

2. **Load on the aluminum tube**, the rest:

   $$P_a = P - P_s = 120{,}000 - 74{,}000 = 46{,}000 \text{ N} \quad (38.4\%)$$ ✅

3. Steel carries 61.6 percent of the load with only $706.9 / (706.9 + 1256.6) = 36\%$ of the area, because its modulus is $200/70 \approx 2.86$ times as high. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 4: Stress in each material and safety factors

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the stress and safety factor calculations**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Compressive stress in the steel rod**, its own load over its own area:

   $$\sigma_s = \frac{P_s}{A_s} = \frac{74{,}000}{706.9} = 104.7 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

2. **Compressive stress in the aluminum tube:**

   $$\sigma_a = \frac{P_a}{A_a} = \frac{46{,}000}{1256.6} = 36.6 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

3. **Safety factors**, each with its own yield strength:

   $$\text{SF}_s = \frac{350}{104.7} = 3.34 \qquad \text{SF}_a = \frac{270}{36.6} = 7.38$$ ✅

   Both materials have enough reserve. The steel rod has the smaller safety factor, 3.34, so it decides the design. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 5: Common deflection

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the deflection calculation**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Common strain**, stress over $E$ for the steel, both in MPa:

   $$\varepsilon = \frac{\sigma_s}{E_s} = \frac{104.7}{200{,}000} = 5.23 \times 10^{-4}$$ ✅

2. **Shortening of the column**, strain times length:

   $$\delta = \varepsilon \, L = 5.23 \times 10^{-4} \times 250 = 0.131 \text{ mm}$$ ✅

3. **Check with the aluminum:**

   $$\varepsilon_a = \frac{\sigma_a}{E_a} = \frac{36.6}{70{,}000} = 5.23 \times 10^{-4} \checkmark$$ ✅

   The two materials give the same strain. This is the compatibility check: if the two strains differ, the load split is wrong. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

:::note[Engineering Insight]
The aluminum here has a safety factor of 7.38 and could be thinner. But a thinner tube has a smaller $EA$ and passes more of the load to the steel, which already decides the design. In a parallel bar you cannot change one material without changing the stress in the other.
:::

## Application 3: Stepped steel-aluminum tie rod in tension

<InArticleAd />


A stepped tie rod carries one tensile force through two segments of different material and diameter: a series arrangement. The force is the same in both, the stresses differ with the areas, and the total elongation is the sum of the two.

:::note[System Problem Statement]
- Steel segment: diameter $d_s = 20$ mm, length $L_s = 300$ mm, $E_s = 200$ GPa, $\sigma_{ys} = 350$ MPa
- Aluminum segment: diameter $d_a = 30$ mm, length $L_a = 400$ mm, $E_a = 70$ GPa, $\sigma_{ya} = 270$ MPa
- Applied tensile force: $F = 30{,}000$ N throughout

**Key Question:** What stress does each segment carry, and what is the total elongation of the rod?
:::

```text
  30,000 N                                         30,000 N
 ◄──────── ┌────────────┬──────────────────────┐ ────────►
           │ steel      │ aluminum             │
           │ d = 20 mm  │ d = 30 mm            │
           └────────────┴──────────────────────┘
           │◄─ 300 mm ─►│◄────── 400 mm ──────►│
```

### Step 1: Cross-sectional areas

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the area calculations**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Steel segment area:**

   $$A_s = \frac{\pi (20)^2}{4} = 314.2 \text{ mm}^2$$ ✅

2. **Aluminum segment area:**

   $$A_a = \frac{\pi (30)^2}{4} = 706.9 \text{ mm}^2$$ ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 2: Stress in each segment

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the stress calculations**</summary>

<Steps>

1. The same force $F = 30{,}000$ N acts on every cross-section, so each stress is that force over the area of the segment:

   $$\sigma_s = \frac{F}{A_s} = \frac{30{,}000}{314.2} = 95.5 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

   $$\sigma_a = \frac{F}{A_a} = \frac{30{,}000}{706.9} = 42.4 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

2. **Safety factors:**

   $$\text{SF}_s = \frac{350}{95.5} = 3.67 \qquad \text{SF}_a = \frac{270}{42.4} = 6.37$$ ✅

   The steel segment has the smaller safety factor, 3.67, so it is the critical one. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 3: Elongation of each segment and total elongation

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the elongation calculations**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Elongation of the steel segment**, from $\delta = FL/(AE)$:

   $$\delta_s = \frac{F L_s}{A_s E_s} = \frac{30{,}000 \times 300}{314.2 \times 200{,}000} = 0.143 \text{ mm}$$ ✅

2. **Elongation of the aluminum segment:**

   $$\delta_a = \frac{F L_a}{A_a E_a} = \frac{30{,}000 \times 400}{706.9 \times 70{,}000} = 0.243 \text{ mm}$$ ✅

3. **Total elongation:**

   $$\delta_{total} = \delta_s + \delta_a = 0.143 + 0.243 = 0.386 \text{ mm}$$ ✅

   The aluminum segment stretches 70 percent more than the steel segment, although it is only 33 percent longer and has more than twice the area. Its modulus is about a third of the modulus of steel. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

:::note[Engineering Insight]
The aluminum segment gives 63 percent of the total elongation with 57 percent of the length. If this rod positions a tool or a sensor, a larger aluminum diameter is the most direct way to reduce the stretch.
:::

## Design guidelines for compound systems

<InArticleAd />


1. Decide first whether the arrangement is parallel or series. The equations are different.
2. In parallel, share the load by $EA$, never by area alone.
3. Check each material against its own yield strength. The critical one is not always the one with the highest stress.

:::tip[Instrument it]
You cannot see how a parallel system shares its load, but you can measure it. Bond a strain gauge (a thin resistor whose resistance changes when it is stretched) to each material; both must read the same strain. That strain times each $EA$ is the force in each material. Read the gauges with an [ESP32 or STM32](/education/sensor-actuator-interfacing-stm32/) and you can [watch the split live on siliconwit.io](https://siliconwit.io). Two readings that differ tell you that the end plates are not seating flat.
:::

## Summary and Next Steps

<InArticleAd />


### Key concepts

1. **Parallel compound bars** deform by the same amount. The load is shared by axial stiffness: $F_i = P \cdot EA_i / \sum EA_j$.
2. **Series compound bars** carry the same force in every segment. The stress in a segment is $\sigma_i = F/A_i$, and the total elongation is the sum $\delta_{total} = \sum F L_i/(A_i E_i)$.
3. **Compatibility of deformation** is the condition that the materials still fit together after they deform. With equilibrium, it gives the load split.
4. **Every material needs its own safety factor check.**

Next, [Thermal Stresses and Strains](/education/mechanics-of-materials/thermal-stresses-and-strains/) uses the same two conditions, equilibrium and compatibility, when the load comes from a change of temperature and not from an applied force.
