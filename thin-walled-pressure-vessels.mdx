When a cylinder of compressed air bursts, it splits along its length, and the energy stored in the air throws the pieces. The split runs lengthwise for a reason: the wall carries two stresses at once, and the stress around the circle is twice the stress along the length. This lesson finds both stresses by cutting the vessel in two and balancing forces. You then size a wall for a given pressure, see why a sphere needs half the wall of a cylinder, learn where the "thin wall" formulas stop being true, and check how far a vessel grows and how much a seam weakens it. #PressureVessels #HoopStress #PneumaticDesign

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

1. **Derive** the hoop and longitudinal stress formulas for a thin-walled cylinder from a free-body cut
2. **Calculate** both stresses in a cylindrical pressure vessel and identify which one governs the design
3. **Size** the minimum wall thickness for a given internal pressure and allowable stress
4. **Evaluate** the safety factor of an existing wall against the material yield strength
5. **Explain** why a spherical vessel has the same wall stress in every direction and needs about half the wall of a cylinder
6. **Compute** the circumferential and longitudinal strains and the changes in diameter, length and volume of a pressurized cylinder
7. **Size** the wall of a vessel with a seam, using a joint efficiency and a safety factor on the ultimate strength

:::tip[Try it as you read]

:::caution[This lesson is for understanding, not for approving a vessel]
A vessel under pressure stores energy, and a vessel filled with gas stores far more than one filled with liquid. If the wall fails, that energy is released at once. Pressure equipment is designed, built, tested and inspected under the pressure vessel code that applies where you work, by a competent person. Do not build, modify, repair or approve a vessel from the calculations in this lesson.
:::

## Real-World System Problem: Pneumatic Actuator Casing

<InArticleAd />


Pneumatic actuators move grippers, clamps, slides and valves in automated machines. The cylindrical casing holds air under pressure whenever the machine works, so an error in the wall stress is a safety matter.

### The pressure vessel problem

> **Engineering Question:** How do we determine the minimum wall thickness for a pneumatic actuator casing operating at 0.6 MPa gauge pressure, and what stresses does that wall actually carry?

Gauge pressure is the pressure above the surrounding atmosphere. It is the pressure difference that the wall must carry. With a stress model you can choose the wall thickness, say how the vessel would fail (a split along its length, not an end that blows off), and see what the rules of a pressure vessel code are built on.

## Fundamental Theory: Pressure Vessel Mechanics

<InArticleAd />


A wooden barrel is held together by steel hoops. The liquid pushes the staves outward, and each hoop is pulled in tension around the circle. The wall of a pressure vessel does the work of those hoops, and the stress around the circle is named the hoop stress. The barrel picture stops there: a closed vessel also has ends, the pressure pushes them apart, and the wall must hold them too. That gives a second stress along the length, the longitudinal stress.

<Card title="Thin-wall assumption" icon="document">
The formulas of this lesson treat the stress as even through the thickness of the wall. That is close to true when the internal radius $r$ is at least ten times the wall thickness $t$:

$$\frac{r}{t} \geq 10$$

In a thicker wall the inner surface carries more stress than the outer surface, and the simple formula reads low: by about 5% at $r/t = 10$ and about 11% at $r/t = 5$. Such walls need the thick-wall (Lamé) equations.
</Card>

![Three free-body diagrams. Panel (a): half of a cylinder cut lengthwise. Pressure p acts on the flat area 2rL and the two cut wall faces of area tL resist, which gives the hoop stress pr/t. Panel (b): a cylinder cut across its axis. Pressure acts on the end area pi r squared and the cut ring of wall of area 2 pi r t resists, which gives the longitudinal stress pr/2t. Panel (c): half of a sphere, which gives pr/2t in every direction](./images/pressure-vessel-fbd.svg)

Each formula below is one of these cuts. Cut the vessel, ask what area the pressure pushes on and what area of wall resists, and balance the two forces. Pressure on a curved surface pushes in many directions, but the sideways parts cancel, so the net push on a cut half is the pressure times the flat area of the opening.

<Card title="Hoop (circumferential) stress in a cylinder" icon="document">
Cut the cylinder lengthwise through a diameter. The pressure $p$ pushes on the flat area $2rL$. Two cut faces of wall resist, each of area $tL$. So $p \, (2rL) = \sigma_{hoop} \, (2tL)$, and:

$$\sigma_{hoop} = \frac{p \, r}{t}$$

Where $p$ is the gauge pressure (MPa), $r$ is the internal radius (mm), $t$ is the wall thickness (mm) and $L$ is the length (mm). The stress is then in MPa. This is the larger stress, so it governs the design.
</Card>

<Card title="Longitudinal (axial) stress in a cylinder" icon="document">
Cut the cylinder across its axis. The pressure pushes on the end area $\pi r^2$. A full ring of wall resists, of area $2\pi r t$. So $p \, (\pi r^2) = \sigma_{long} \, (2\pi r t)$, and:

$$\sigma_{long} = \frac{p \, r}{2t}$$

This is half the hoop stress: $\sigma_{hoop} = 2\,\sigma_{long}$. The pressure is the same in both cuts. The wall area that holds it is not.
</Card>

<Card title="Stress in a spherical vessel" icon="document">
Every cut through the centre of a sphere is the same. The pressure pushes on the area $\pi r^2$ and a ring of wall of area $2\pi r t$ resists, so one formula holds in every direction:

$$\sigma_{sphere} = \frac{p \, r}{2t}$$

For the same pressure, radius and wall thickness, the sphere wall carries half the hoop stress of the cylinder wall.
</Card>

<Card title="Strains and change in dimensions" icon="document">
A wall that is stretched in one direction becomes a little narrower in the direction across it. Poisson's ratio $\nu$ (about 0.3 for steel, no unit) is the size of that effect. The wall is pulled in two directions at once, so each strain is its own stress over Young's modulus $E$ (MPa), less the Poisson effect of the other stress:

$$\varepsilon_{hoop} = \frac{\sigma_{hoop} - \nu\,\sigma_{long}}{E}, \qquad \varepsilon_{long} = \frac{\sigma_{long} - \nu\,\sigma_{hoop}}{E}$$

The circumference and the diameter grow by the same fraction, so the diameter $d$ and the length $L$ change by:

$$\Delta d = \varepsilon_{hoop}\, d, \qquad \Delta L = \varepsilon_{long}\, L$$

The volume is $V = \pi r^2 L$. The radius is squared in it, so its strain counts twice:

$$\varepsilon_V = \frac{\Delta V}{V} = 2\,\varepsilon_{hoop} + \varepsilon_{long}, \qquad \Delta V = \varepsilon_V\, V$$

For a sphere the strain is $\varepsilon = \dfrac{\sigma(1-\nu)}{E}$ in every direction, and $\varepsilon_V = 3\varepsilon$ with $V = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3$.
</Card>

<Card title="Joint efficiency and design thickness" icon="document">
A vessel is rolled from plate and closed by a riveted or welded seam, which is weaker than the plate. The joint efficiency $\eta$ (between 0 and 1) is the fraction of the plate strength that the seam keeps. The seam that runs along the length is pulled open by the hoop stress, so it sets the wall thickness:

$$t_{min} = \frac{p\,r}{\sigma_{allow}\,\eta}, \qquad \sigma_{allow} = \frac{\sigma_{UTS}}{SF}$$

Here $\sigma_{UTS}$ is the ultimate tensile strength, the largest stress the material carries before it breaks, and $SF$ is the safety factor. The seam around the circle carries only the longitudinal stress, so it needs $t = pr/(2\,\sigma_{allow}\,\eta_c)$ and seldom governs. With $\eta = 1$ the result is the solid wall value $t_{min} = pr/\sigma_{allow}$.
</Card>

## Application 1: Pneumatic Actuator Casing

<InArticleAd />


The casing of a small pneumatic gripper is a cylinder that must hold the working pressure and stay light, because a robot arm carries it.

:::note[System Problem Statement]
- **Component:** aluminum 6061-T6 pneumatic actuator casing (cylindrical)
- **Working pressure:** $p = 0.6$ MPa gauge (6 bar)
- **Internal diameter:** 100 mm, so radius $r = 50$ mm
- **Material:** aluminum 6061-T6, $\sigma_{yield} = 270$ MPa (the stress at which permanent deformation starts)
- **Required safety factor:** SF = 4.0
- **Task:** find the minimum wall thickness, then verify the actual stresses with a chosen standard thickness of 3.0 mm

**Key Question:** What wall thickness does the pressure require, and how large is the safety margin with a 3 mm wall?
:::

### Step 1: Minimum wall thickness from hoop stress

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the wall thickness calculation**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Set the allowable stress.** The hoop stress must not exceed the yield strength divided by the safety factor:

   $$\sigma_{allow} = \frac{\sigma_{yield}}{SF} = \frac{270 \text{ MPa}}{4.0} = 67.5 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

2. **Solve for minimum thickness.** From $\sigma_{hoop} = pr/t$, rearranging gives:

   $$t_{min} = \frac{p \, r}{\sigma_{allow}} = \frac{0.6 \text{ MPa} \times 50 \text{ mm}}{67.5 \text{ MPa}} = \frac{30}{67.5} \text{ mm} = 0.44 \text{ mm}$$ ✅

3. **Select a standard thickness.** A wall of 0.44 mm cannot be machined, threaded or handled. A 3.0 mm wall is chosen, which also leaves margin for machining tolerances and for the higher stress around the port holes. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 2: Verify actual stresses with t = 3.0 mm

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the stress verification**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Hoop stress** ($r/t = 50/3.0 = 16.7$, so the thin-wall formulas apply):

   $$\sigma_{hoop} = \frac{p \, r}{t} = \frac{0.6 \text{ MPa} \times 50 \text{ mm}}{3.0 \text{ mm}} = 10.0 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

2. **Longitudinal stress:**

   $$\sigma_{long} = \frac{p \, r}{2t} = \frac{0.6 \text{ MPa} \times 50 \text{ mm}}{2 \times 3.0 \text{ mm}} = 5.0 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

3. **Safety factors with the chosen wall:**

   $$SF_{hoop} = \frac{270 \text{ MPa}}{10.0 \text{ MPa}} = 27.0 \qquad SF_{long} = \frac{270 \text{ MPa}}{5.0 \text{ MPa}} = 54.0$$ ✅

   Both well above the required 4.0. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 3: Interpret the design

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the design interpretation**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Why is the margin so large?** The calculated minimum is 0.44 mm, and the 3.0 mm wall is 6.8 times thicker. The extra wall is there for practical reasons: threads for the ports, handling, corrosion, and the stress around a port hole, which is 2 to 4 times the stress in the plain wall. ✅

2. **What the numbers confirm.** The hoop stress governs (10.0 MPa against 5.0 MPa longitudinal), so the casing would split lengthwise before an end came off. The margin for a steady pressure is large. A casing that is pressurized and released many times also needs a separate fatigue check. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

:::note[Engineering Insight]
The calculated minimum is what the pressure alone requires. The chosen thickness is decided by manufacture, by the higher stress around holes, and by the minimum values in the code. In low-pressure pneumatics the two numbers differ by a large factor.
:::

## Application 2: Compressed-Air Receiver Tank

<InArticleAd />


A compressed-air receiver is the tank between a compressor and the air lines of a workshop. It is a plain cylinder at a higher pressure than a pneumatic actuator, so the stress in the plain wall is the main calculation.

:::note[System Problem Statement]
- **Component:** steel receiver tank (cylindrical pressure vessel)
- **Internal pressure:** $p = 1.6$ MPa gauge
- **Internal diameter:** 400 mm, so radius $r = 200$ mm
- **Wall thickness:** $t = 10$ mm (as manufactured)
- **Material:** carbon steel, $\sigma_{yield} = 350$ MPa
- **Task:** calculate the actual hoop and longitudinal stresses, check the safety factor against yield, and then find the minimum wall thickness for SF = 4.0

**Key Question:** Is the 10 mm wall adequately safe, and how thin could the wall theoretically be at this pressure?
:::

Set this cylinder in the simulator and read the hoop and longitudinal stresses and the safety factor:

  <LinkButton href="/product-development/pressure-vessel-simulator/" target="_blank" variant="primary">Open the Pressure Vessel Simulator</LinkButton>
</div>

### Step 1: Verify the thin-wall assumption

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the thin-wall check**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Check $r/t$:**

   $$\frac{r}{t} = \frac{200 \text{ mm}}{10 \text{ mm}} = 20$$ ✅

   This is above the limit of 10, so the thin-wall formulas apply. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 2: Hoop and longitudinal stress

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the stress calculations**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Hoop stress:**

   $$\sigma_{hoop} = \frac{p \, r}{t} = \frac{1.6 \text{ MPa} \times 200 \text{ mm}}{10 \text{ mm}} = 32.0 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

2. **Longitudinal stress:**

   $$\sigma_{long} = \frac{p \, r}{2t} = \frac{1.6 \text{ MPa} \times 200 \text{ mm}}{2 \times 10 \text{ mm}} = 16.0 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

3. **Confirm the 2:1 ratio.** $32.0 / 16.0 = 2.0$, as the two cuts predict. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 3: Safety factor and minimum wall thickness

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the safety factor and wall sizing**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Safety factor of the 10 mm wall against yield:**

   $$SF = \frac{\sigma_{yield}}{\sigma_{hoop}} = \frac{350 \text{ MPa}}{32.0 \text{ MPa}} = 10.9$$ ✅

   The hoop stress is 10.9 times below the stress at which the wall would start to yield. The factor is large because the wall also allows for corrosion and for many cycles of pressure. ✅

2. **Allowable stress for SF = 4.0:**

   $$\sigma_{allow} = \frac{350 \text{ MPa}}{4.0} = 87.5 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

3. **Minimum wall thickness at SF = 4.0:**

   $$t_{min} = \frac{p \, r}{\sigma_{allow}} = \frac{1.6 \text{ MPa} \times 200 \text{ mm}}{87.5 \text{ MPa}} = \frac{320}{87.5} \text{ mm} = 3.66 \text{ mm}$$ ✅

   The 10 mm wall is about 2.7 times the calculated minimum at SF = 4.0. The extra thickness covers a corrosion allowance (typically 1 to 2 mm for a steel receiver), manufacturing tolerance, and the margins that the pressure vessel code requires. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

:::note[Engineering Insight]
A safety factor of 10.9 against yield is not waste on a receiver. A receiver works for decades. The pressure rises and falls each time the compressor loads and unloads, water collects inside and rusts the wall, and the pressure sometimes rises above the working value. Each of these reduces the real margin.
:::

## Application 3: Spherical Gas Storage Vessel

<InArticleAd />


Large gas stores at refineries and gas terminals are often spheres. The reason is mechanical: at the same pressure and radius, the wall stress in a sphere is half the hoop stress in a cylinder, so the wall can be about half as thick. On a vessel of hundreds of tonnes that is a large saving.

:::note[System Problem Statement]
- **Component:** spherical pressure vessel (for example a gas storage sphere)
- **Internal pressure:** $p = 1.6$ MPa gauge (same as Application 2)
- **Internal radius:** $r = 200$ mm (same as Application 2)
- **Wall thickness:** $t = 10$ mm (same wall for direct comparison)
- **Material:** carbon steel, $\sigma_{yield} = 350$ MPa
- **Task:** compute the sphere wall stress, compare it with the cylinder hoop stress from Application 2, and find the minimum wall thickness the sphere needs at SF = 4.0

**Key Question:** By how much does changing from a cylinder to a sphere reduce the wall stress and the required wall thickness?
:::

### Step 1: Sphere wall stress

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the sphere stress calculation**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Apply the sphere formula.** Every cut through the centre of a sphere gives the same result:

   $$\sigma_{sphere} = \frac{p \, r}{2t} = \frac{1.6 \text{ MPa} \times 200 \text{ mm}}{2 \times 10 \text{ mm}} = 16.0 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

2. **Compare with the cylinder.** At identical $p$, $r$, and $t$:

   - Cylinder hoop stress (from Application 2): 32.0 MPa ✅
   - Sphere wall stress: 16.0 MPa ✅
   - Ratio: $32.0 / 16.0 = 2.0$ ✅

   The sphere wall carries half the hoop stress of the cylinder. In a sphere, a full ring of wall resists the pressure in every direction. In a cylinder, only two strips of wall resist the lengthwise cut. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 2: Safety factor for the sphere

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the sphere safety factor**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Safety factor of the 10 mm sphere wall:**

   $$SF = \frac{\sigma_{yield}}{\sigma_{sphere}} = \frac{350 \text{ MPa}}{16.0 \text{ MPa}} = 21.9$$ ✅

   With the same 10 mm wall, the safety factor of the sphere against yield is 21.9, against 10.9 for the cylinder. So the sphere can have a thinner wall for the same safety factor. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 3: Minimum sphere wall thickness at SF = 4.0

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the minimum wall comparison**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Allowable stress (same as Application 2):** $\sigma_{allow} = 87.5$ MPa. ✅

2. **Minimum sphere wall thickness:**

   $$t_{min,sphere} = \frac{p \, r}{2 \, \sigma_{allow}} = \frac{1.6 \text{ MPa} \times 200 \text{ mm}}{2 \times 87.5 \text{ MPa}} = \frac{320}{175} \text{ mm} = 1.83 \text{ mm}$$ ✅

3. **Compare with the cylinder minimum (Application 2):** 3.66 mm.

   $$\frac{t_{min,cylinder}}{t_{min,sphere}} = \frac{3.66}{1.83} = 2.0$$ ✅

   The sphere needs half the wall thickness of the cylinder at the same pressure, radius and safety factor. This follows from the factor of 2 in the two stress formulas. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

:::note[Engineering Insight]
The advantage of the sphere comes from its shape alone. A sphere curves equally in every direction, so every direction shares the pressure equally. A cylinder is curved around and straight along, so the wall is loaded twice as hard around the circle as along the length. A sphere is harder and more costly to make, which is why most small vessels are cylinders.
:::

## Application 4: Steam Drum Strains, Volume Change, and a Riveted Seam

<InArticleAd />


A steam drum is the cylinder at the top of a boiler where steam separates from water. It adds two questions. How far does the drum stretch under pressure? That sets clearances, the alignment of the connected pipes, and the volume it holds. And is it the seam, not the solid plate, that decides the wall thickness?

```text
              seam around the circle
       (pulled open by longitudinal stress)
                       │
  ╭────────────────────┼────────────────────╮
  │                    │                    │
  │ ═══════════════════╪═══════════════════ │
  │   ▲                │                    │
  ╰───┼────────────────┼────────────────────╯
      │
      seam along the length
      (pulled open by hoop stress)
```

The wall is pulled around the circle by the hoop stress, and that pull acts across the seam that runs along the length.

:::note[System Problem Statement]
- **Component:** steel boiler steam drum (cylindrical)
- **Internal pressure:** $p = 2.5$ MPa gauge
- **Internal diameter:** 800 mm, so radius $r = 400$ mm
- **Length:** $L = 3.0$ m
- **Wall thickness:** $t = 16$ mm (as built)
- **Material:** steel, $E = 200$ GPa, $\nu = 0.3$, $\sigma_{UTS} = 400$ MPa
- **Seam:** longitudinal riveted joint, efficiency $\eta = 75\%$; required safety factor SF = 5 on the ultimate strength
- **Task:** find the two stresses and strains, the change in diameter, length, and volume, and the minimum wall thickness the seam requires

**Key Question:** How far does the drum grow under pressure, and is the 16 mm wall thick enough once the seam efficiency is accounted for?
:::

### Step 1: Stresses and strains

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the stress and strain calculation**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Stresses**, with $p$ in MPa and $r$ and $t$ in mm (thin-wall check $r/t = 400/16 = 25 \geq 10$):

   $$\sigma_{hoop} = \frac{p r}{t} = \frac{2.5 \times 400}{16} = 62.5 \text{ MPa}, \qquad \sigma_{long} = \frac{p r}{2t} = 31.25 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

2. **Circumferential strain**, with stresses in MPa and $E = 200$ GPa = 200,000 MPa:

   $$\varepsilon_{hoop} = \frac{\sigma_{hoop} - \nu\,\sigma_{long}}{E} = \frac{62.5 - 0.3(31.25)}{200{,}000} = 2.66 \times 10^{-4}$$ ✅

3. **Longitudinal strain:**

   $$\varepsilon_{long} = \frac{\sigma_{long} - \nu\,\sigma_{hoop}}{E} = \frac{31.25 - 0.3(62.5)}{200{,}000} = 6.25 \times 10^{-5}$$ ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 2: Change in diameter, length, and volume

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the dimensional changes**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Change in diameter** (the diameter grows by the hoop strain), $d$ = 800 mm:

   $$\Delta d = \varepsilon_{hoop}\, d = (2.66 \times 10^{-4})(800) = 0.213 \text{ mm}$$ ✅

2. **Change in length**, $L$ = 3.0 m = 3,000 mm:

   $$\Delta L = \varepsilon_{long}\, L = (6.25 \times 10^{-5})(3000) = 0.188 \text{ mm}$$ ✅

3. **Volumetric strain and change in volume** (1 cm³ = 1,000 mm³):

   $$\varepsilon_V = 2\varepsilon_{hoop} + \varepsilon_{long} = 2(2.66 \times 10^{-4}) + 6.25 \times 10^{-5} = 5.94 \times 10^{-4}$$

   $$V = \pi r^2 L = \pi (400)^2 (3000) = 1.51 \times 10^{9} \text{ mm}^3, \qquad \Delta V = \varepsilon_V\, V = 8.95 \times 10^{5} \text{ mm}^3 \approx 895 \text{ cm}^3$$ ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 3: Minimum wall thickness for the riveted seam

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the seam thickness check**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Allowable stress** on the ultimate strength:

   $$\sigma_{allow} = \frac{\sigma_{UTS}}{SF} = \frac{400 \text{ MPa}}{5} = 80 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

2. **The longitudinal seam carries the hoop stress**, so it sets the thickness through the joint efficiency ($\eta = 75\% = 0.75$):

   $$t_{min} = \frac{p\,r}{\sigma_{allow}\,\eta} = \frac{2.5 \times 400}{80 \times 0.75} = 16.7 \text{ mm}$$ ✅

3. **Interpret.** The seam needs 16.7 mm, so the 16 mm wall as built is a little too thin at SF = 5 with a 75% joint. The design needs the next standard plate (for example 18 mm) or a seam of higher efficiency. The solid plate alone would need only $pr/\sigma_{allow} = 2.5 \times 400 / 80 = 12.5$ mm, so the seam governs, not the plate. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

:::note[Engineering Insight]
The strains are small: a fifth of a millimetre on an 800 mm diameter. They still open gaps at flanges, move connected pipes and change the volume, so a design allows for them. The joint efficiency raised the required wall by a third here. A vessel that passes the solid plate formula can still fail at the seam, and this is why a pressure vessel code rates the joint separately.
:::

## Design Guidelines for Thin-Walled Pressure Vessels

<InArticleAd />


<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Use the radius, not the diameter" icon="warning">
  The formulas $\sigma = pr/t$ and $\sigma = pr/(2t)$ take the internal radius $r$. The diameter in its place doubles the calculated stress.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Hoop stress governs a cylinder" icon="setting">
  Size the wall of a cylinder for the hoop stress. The longitudinal stress then has twice the safety factor. A wall sized for the longitudinal stress alone is half as thick as it must be.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Verify the thin-wall assumption" icon="puzzle">
  Confirm $r/t \geq 10$ first. Below that ratio the simple formulas read lower than the real stress at the inner surface, which is the unsafe direction.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Add the practical margins" icon="rocket">
  The value $t = pr/\sigma_{allow}$ is a lower limit. Add the corrosion allowance (1 to 2 mm for steel), check the minimum values in the code, and allow for the higher stress around ports and welds.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Rate the seam, not only the plate" icon="warning">
  A riveted or welded joint is weaker than the plate. Divide the required thickness by the joint efficiency $\eta$, because the seam usually governs.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Allow for the growth" icon="setting">
  Use $\Delta d = \varepsilon_{hoop} d$, $\Delta L = \varepsilon_{long} L$ and $\Delta V = \varepsilon_V V$ to check flange gaps, pipe alignment and volume.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

:::tip[Instrument it]
A pressure sensor reads the internal pressure, and a strain gauge bonded around the circle reads the hoop strain. A second gauge along the length reads the longitudinal strain. On the steam drum above the two strains are $2.66 \times 10^{-4}$ and $6.25 \times 10^{-5}$, a ratio of about 4 to 1, not 2 to 1: the stresses are 2 to 1, and the Poisson effect changes the ratio of the strains. Read both gauges with an [ESP32 or STM32](/education/sensor-actuator-interfacing-stm32/) and [stream the readings to siliconwit.io](https://siliconwit.io) to compare them with your calculation. A monitor does not replace the safety valve or the inspections that the code requires.
:::

### Key concepts

1. **The thin-wall formulas** apply when $r/t \geq 10$. They treat the stress as even through the wall.
2. **A cylinder** has $\sigma_{hoop} = pr/t$ around the circle and $\sigma_{long} = pr/(2t)$ along the length. The hoop stress is twice as large, so a cylinder splits lengthwise.
3. **A sphere** has $\sigma_{sphere} = pr/(2t)$ in every direction, so it needs half the wall of a cylinder.
4. **The wall thickness** is $t_{min} = pr/\sigma_{allow}$ for a cylinder, with $\sigma_{allow}$ the strength divided by the safety factor.
5. **The strains** need both stresses and Poisson's ratio. They give the change in diameter, length and volume.
6. **A seam** with joint efficiency $\eta$ raises the wall to $t_{min} = pr/(\sigma_{allow}\,\eta)$.

### Results at a glance

| Application | Geometry | $\sigma_{hoop}$ or $\sigma_{sphere}$ | $\sigma_{long}$ | SF (as built) | $t_{min}$ at SF = 4 |
|-------------|----------|--------------------------------------|-----------------|:-------------:|---------------------|
| Pneumatic actuator casing | Cylinder | 10.0 MPa | 5.0 MPa | 27.0 | 0.44 mm |
| Compressed-air receiver | Cylinder | 32.0 MPa | 16.0 MPa | 10.9 | 3.66 mm |
| Spherical accumulator | Sphere | 16.0 MPa | 16.0 MPa | 21.9 | 1.83 mm |

The steam drum of Application 4, at $p = 2.5$ MPa, grows by $\Delta d = 0.213$ mm and $\Delta V \approx 895$ cm³. Its 75% riveted seam raises the required wall to 16.7 mm, above the 12.5 mm that the solid plate alone would need.

These formulas give the stress in the plain wall under steady pressure. They do not cover ports, welds, supports, fatigue or corrosion, which is the work of the pressure vessel code and of the competent person who applies it.
