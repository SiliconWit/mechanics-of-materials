A steel rod clamped at both ends inside a machine carries no load. Warm it by 60 °C and it pushes on its clamps with 28.8 kN, about the weight of three small cars, and no load diagram shows that force. Give the same rod one free end and it grows by 0.36 mm with no stress at all. Heat does not make stress. Stopping the growth does. This lesson shows you how to calculate how far a part grows when it is free, the stress when the growth is stopped, how the clearance between a piston and its cylinder changes when both are hot, and the forces inside two different metals that are joined and heated together. #ThermalStress #ThermalExpansion #MechatronicDesign

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

## Real-World System Problem: Heated Piston-Cylinder Interface

<InArticleAd />


In the heated head of a 3D printer or an injection moulding machine, a piston slides in a hot cylinder. The two parts are often different metals that grow by different amounts, and if the gap between them closes, the piston locks in the bore.

### The thermal challenge

> **Engineering Question:** How do we predict the clearance change and the thermal stresses when an aluminum cylinder and a steel piston are heated from room temperature to operating temperature?

## Fundamental Theory: Thermal Expansion and Stress

<InArticleAd />


Almost every solid grows a little in every direction when it is heated, and shrinks when it is cooled. Three words are needed before the formulas:

- **Strain** is the change in length divided by the original length. It has no unit.
- **Stress** is the internal force divided by the area that carries it, in MPa (1 MPa = 1 N/mm²).
- **Young's modulus** $E$ is the stiffness of the material: the stress needed for each unit of strain, in GPa (1 GPa = 1,000 MPa).

### Free thermal strain and expansion

Each material grows by a fixed fraction of its length for each degree. That fraction is the coefficient of linear thermal expansion, $\alpha$. For steel it is $12 \times 10^{-6}$ per °C: a 1 m steel bar grows 0.012 mm for each degree. Aluminum grows about twice as much.

<Card title="Thermal strain and free expansion" icon="document">
The strain is the fraction per degree times the number of degrees. The growth is that strain times the length.

$$\epsilon_T = \alpha \, \Delta T$$

$$\delta_T = \alpha \, L \, \Delta T$$

Where:
- $\epsilon_T$ = thermal strain (no unit)
- $\delta_T$ = free thermal expansion (mm)
- $\alpha$ = coefficient of linear thermal expansion (1/°C)
- $L$ = original length (mm)
- $\Delta T$ = temperature change (°C), positive for heating

A member with nothing to stop it grows by $\delta_T$ and has zero stress.
</Card>

### Fully restrained member: thermal stress

Now fix both ends of the bar to rigid walls and heat it. Think of two steps: let the bar grow freely by $\delta_T$, then push it back to its old length, as you would compress a stiff spring. The stress in the bar is the stress of that push. The strain pushed out is $\delta_T / L = \alpha \Delta T$, and Hooke's law (stress = $E$ × strain) gives the stress:

<Card title="Thermal stress in a fully restrained member" icon="document">
$$\sigma_T = E \, \alpha \, \Delta T$$

Where:
- $\sigma_T$ = thermal stress (MPa), compressive for heating and tensile for cooling
- $E$ = Young's modulus (MPa)
- $\alpha$ = coefficient of linear thermal expansion (1/°C)
- $\Delta T$ = temperature change (°C)

The length is not in the formula. A long bar grows further, but that growth is spread over more length, so the strain is the same.
</Card>

The spring picture has two limits. Real walls and clamps give way a little, so the real stress is somewhat lower. And the formula holds only below the yield strength, the stress at which the material starts to deform permanently.

:::note[A gap before the wall]
If the member can grow by a gap $g$ before it touches a rigid wall, only the growth beyond the gap is prevented:

$$\delta_{restrained} = \delta_T - g = \alpha L \Delta T - g$$

$$\sigma_T = E \, \frac{\delta_{restrained}}{L} = E \left(\alpha \Delta T - \frac{g}{L}\right) \qquad \text{valid when } \alpha L \Delta T > g$$

If $\alpha L \Delta T \leq g$ the member does not reach the wall and the thermal stress is zero.
:::

### Compound (bimetallic) assembly: compatibility

A compound or bimetallic assembly is two materials joined so that they change length together. When heated, the material with the larger $\alpha$ tries to grow more, the joint stops it, and the two settle at one common length between their free lengths.

![Three panels. Panel 1: bars A and B grow freely by different amounts when heated, A more than B. Panel 2: the mismatch between the two free ends, equal to the difference of the alpha values times L times delta T. Panel 3: a compressive force P in bar A and an equal tensile force P in bar B bring both to a common final length. The compatibility equation and the formula for P are written below](./images/thermal-compatibility-superposition.svg)

Read the picture as three steps: let both parts grow freely, measure the mismatch, then apply the pair of internal forces that closes it. The part with the larger $\alpha$ ends in compression and the other in tension, with no external load on the assembly.

<Card title="Compatibility method for compound assemblies" icon="document">
Material A (higher $\alpha$) and material B (lower $\alpha$) have the same length $L$ and are joined at both ends. Both are heated by $\Delta T$. An internal force $P$ (N) compresses A, and an equal force $P$ stretches B. A force $P$ changes the length of a bar of area $A$ (mm²) by $PL/(AE)$.

**Compatibility** means the two parts end at the same length. A grows freely and is pushed back. B grows freely and is pulled further:

$$\alpha_A L \Delta T - \frac{P L}{A_A E_A} = \alpha_B L \Delta T + \frac{P L}{A_B E_B}$$

Solving for $P$ (the length cancels):

$$P = \frac{(\alpha_A - \alpha_B) \Delta T}{\dfrac{1}{A_A E_A} + \dfrac{1}{A_B E_B}}$$

Then $\sigma_A = P / A_A$ (compression) and $\sigma_B = P / A_B$ (tension).
</Card>

## Application 1: Piston-Cylinder Thermal Analysis

<InArticleAd />


A heated 3D printer extruder has a steel piston sliding in an aluminum cylinder. Neither part is held, so both grow freely. The clearance is the gap between bore and piston, measured on the diameter. Does it open or close when both are hot, and what stress would appear if the parts were locked?

```text
        aluminum cylinder (grows more)
   ┌──────────────────────────────────┐
   │▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒│
   ├──────────────────────────────────┤
   │   ┌────────────────────┐ ◄─────────── clearance
   │   │    steel piston    │  bore   │
   │   │    19.95 mm cold   │ 20.00 mm│
   │   └────────────────────┘         │
   ├──────────────────────────────────┤
   │▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒│
   └──────────────────────────────────┘
     both heated from 20 °C to 200 °C
```

A hole in a heated part grows as if it were filled with the same material; it does not close. So the bore diameter grows by $D \alpha \Delta T$, like the piston diameter.

:::note[System Problem Statement]
- **Aluminum cylinder:** inner diameter $D_{Al} = 20.00$ mm, length 100 mm, material Al 6061 ($\alpha_{Al} = 23.6 \times 10^{-6}$ /°C, $E_{Al} = 70$ GPa, $\sigma_{yield,Al} = 270$ MPa)
- **Steel piston:** outer diameter $D_{st} = 19.95$ mm, length 80 mm, material Steel 1045 ($\alpha_{st} = 12.0 \times 10^{-6}$ /°C, $E_{st} = 200$ GPa, $\sigma_{yield,st} = 530$ MPa)
- **Temperature change:** $\Delta T = 180$ °C (from 20 °C to 200 °C)
- **Cold running clearance:** $20.00 - 19.95 = 0.05$ mm

**Key Question:** How does the clearance change at operating temperature, and would either part yield if the expansion were fully constrained?
:::

### Step 1: Thermal expansion of each part

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the thermal expansion calculations**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Aluminum cylinder inner diameter expansion** (diameter in mm × $\alpha$ in 1/°C × $\Delta T$ in °C):

   $$\Delta D_{Al} = D_{Al} \, \alpha_{Al} \, \Delta T = 20.00 \times 23.6 \times 10^{-6} \times 180 = 0.0850 \text{ mm}$$ ✅

   Hot inner diameter: $20.00 + 0.0850 = 20.0850$ mm ✅

2. **Steel piston outer diameter expansion:**

   $$\Delta D_{st} = D_{st} \, \alpha_{st} \, \Delta T = 19.95 \times 12.0 \times 10^{-6} \times 180 = 0.0431 \text{ mm}$$ ✅

   Hot outer diameter: $19.95 + 0.0431 = 19.9931$ mm ✅

3. **Hot running clearance:**

   $$\text{Clearance}_{hot} = D_{Al,hot} - D_{st,hot} = 20.0850 - 19.9931 = 0.0919 \text{ mm}$$ ✅

   The clearance grows from 0.05 mm cold to 0.092 mm hot. The aluminum grows about twice as much as the steel for each degree, so the bore opens faster than the piston grows. This pair of materials cannot seize by heating. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 2: Thermal stress if expansion were fully constrained

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the thermal stress calculations**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Constrained thermal stress in the aluminum cylinder wall** ($E_{Al} = 70$ GPa = 70,000 MPa):

   $$\sigma_{T,Al} = E_{Al} \, \alpha_{Al} \, \Delta T = 70{,}000 \times 23.6 \times 10^{-6} \times 180 = 297 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

2. **Constrained thermal stress in the steel piston** ($E_{st} = 200$ GPa = 200,000 MPa):

   $$\sigma_{T,st} = E_{st} \, \alpha_{st} \, \Delta T = 200{,}000 \times 12.0 \times 10^{-6} \times 180 = 432 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

3. **Compare with yield strengths:**

   - Aluminum: 297 MPa compared with $\sigma_{yield} = 270$ MPa. Would yield if fully constrained. ✅
   - Steel: 432 MPa compared with $\sigma_{yield} = 530$ MPa. Would remain elastic. ✅

   These stresses exist only if neither part can move. Here both grow freely, so neither has them. The calculation is a warning: do not bolt or press a material with a high $\alpha$ into a place where it cannot grow. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 3: Clearance design for reliable operation

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the design clearance calculation**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Minimum required hot clearance** (to prevent contact under worst case): 0.05 mm. ✅

2. **Differential expansion**, the difference between the growth of the two diameters:

   $$\Delta_{differential} = \Delta D_{Al} - \Delta D_{st} = 0.0850 - 0.0431 = 0.0419 \text{ mm}$$ ✅

3. **Required cold clearance** to guarantee 0.05 mm hot clearance:

   The aluminum bore grows more, so heating opens the clearance: 0.05 mm cold becomes 0.092 mm hot. No change is needed here.

   Now reverse the materials: a steel bore and an aluminum piston. Heating would close the clearance by about 0.042 mm, so the cold clearance would have to be at least $0.05 + 0.042 = 0.092$ mm to avoid seizure. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

:::note[Engineering Insight]
In any heated sliding fit, first ask which part has the higher $\alpha$. If it is the bore, the clearance opens with temperature. If it is the piston, the clearance closes and seizure is the risk. A metal jar lid shows the first case: hot water loosens it because the lid grows more than the glass.
:::

## Application 2: Fully Restrained Steel Rod

<InArticleAd />


A steel actuator rod is clamped rigidly at both ends inside a housing. As the machine warms up, the rod becomes 60 °C hotter than when it was clamped, the housing stops it growing, and a compressive stress appears.

:::note[System Problem Statement]
- **Component:** steel actuator rod, length $L = 500$ mm, cross-sectional area $A = 200$ mm²
- **Material:** structural steel, $\alpha = 12 \times 10^{-6}$ /°C, $E = 200$ GPa, $\sigma_{yield} = 250$ MPa
- **Temperature rise:** $\Delta T = 60$ °C
- **Boundary condition:** both ends rigidly fixed (fully restrained)

**Key Question:** What compressive stress develops in the rod, what would the free expansion have been, and is the rod safe against yield?
:::

In the simulator, clamp a steel bar and raise the temperature to see the stress rise toward yield:

<div style={{ display: 'flex', justifyContent: 'center', width: '100%', margin: '0.5rem 0 1rem' }}>
  <LinkButton href="/product-development/thermal-stress-simulator/" target="_blank" variant="primary">Open the Thermal Stress Simulator</LinkButton>
</div>

### Step 1: Would-be free expansion

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the free-expansion calculation**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Compute the thermal strain** ($\alpha$ in 1/°C × $\Delta T$ in °C):

   $$\epsilon_T = \alpha \, \Delta T = (12 \times 10^{-6})(60) = 7.2 \times 10^{-4}$$ ✅

2. **Compute the free expansion over 500 mm:**

   $$\delta_T = \alpha \, L \, \Delta T = (12 \times 10^{-6})(500)(60) = 0.36 \text{ mm}$$ ✅

   The rod would grow by this much if one end were free. Both ends are clamped, so the clamps push back and keep the rod at 500 mm. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 2: Thermal stress and force on the clamps

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the thermal stress calculation**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Thermal stress** (the length is not in the formula):

   $$\sigma_T = E \, \alpha \, \Delta T = (200 \times 10^3 \text{ MPa})(12 \times 10^{-6})(60) = 144 \text{ MPa (compressive)}$$ ✅

2. **Reaction force at each clamp** (stress × area, and 1 MPa × 1 mm² = 1 N):

   $$F = \sigma_T \times A = 144 \text{ MPa} \times 200 \text{ mm}^2 = 28{,}800 \text{ N} = 28.8 \text{ kN}$$ ✅

   Each clamp carries 28.8 kN although no external load acts on the rod. A calculation of the applied loads alone does not find this force. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 3: Safety factor against yield

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the safety factor check**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Compare the thermal stress with the yield strength.** The safety factor (SF) is the yield strength divided by the working stress:

   $$\text{SF} = \frac{\sigma_{yield}}{\sigma_T} = \frac{250 \text{ MPa}}{144 \text{ MPa}} = 1.74$$ ✅

2. **Interpret the result.** 1.74 is a small margin. At a rise of 90 °C the stress would be $200{,}000 \times 12 \times 10^{-6} \times 90 = 216$ MPa, with $SF = 1.16$; at 104 °C the rod would yield. A rod that has yielded in compression is permanently shorter, so it is pulled in tension when it cools, and each further heating and cooling cycle can add permanent deformation until the rod or its clamps fail. ✅

   A rod that must stay elastic over its whole temperature range needs a material with a higher yield strength, or a mounting that lets one end slide. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

:::note[Engineering Insight]
Length sets how far the rod would have grown, not the stress. A long held rail is still more dangerous than a short one: a long thin member in compression can buckle (bend sideways suddenly) well below yield.
:::

## Application 3: Compound Assembly (Aluminum Rod in Steel Housing)

<InArticleAd />


An aluminum rod sits inside a steel tube, pinned together at both ends so they change length together. When heated, the aluminum tries to grow more than the steel, and one internal force compresses the aluminum and stretches the steel.

```text
  pin                                   pin
   ┃ ┌───────────────────────────────┐ ┃
   ┃ │  steel tube     ◄── P ──►     │ ┃   pulled
   ┃ ├───────────────────────────────┤ ┃
   ┃ │  aluminum rod   ──► P ◄──     │ ┃   pushed
   ┃ ├───────────────────────────────┤ ┃
   ┃ │  steel tube                   │ ┃
   ┃ └───────────────────────────────┘ ┃
        L = 200 mm, heated by 80 °C
```

:::note[System Problem Statement]
- **Aluminum rod:** diameter $d_{Al} = 20$ mm, so $A_{Al} = \pi (20)^2 / 4 = 314.2$ mm², length $L = 200$ mm, $\alpha_{Al} = 23.6 \times 10^{-6}$ /°C, $E_{Al} = 70$ GPa
- **Steel housing (tube):** same wall area $A_{st} = 314.2$ mm², length $L = 200$ mm, $\alpha_{st} = 12 \times 10^{-6}$ /°C, $E_{st} = 200$ GPa
- **Temperature rise:** $\Delta T = 80$ °C, both parts heat equally
- **Constraint:** ends pinned; both parts must have the same total elongation

**Key Question:** What internal force develops, and what stress does each material carry?
:::

### Step 1: Free expansions and the compatibility gap

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the free-expansion and compatibility analysis**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Free expansion of the aluminum rod** ($\alpha$ in 1/°C, $L$ in mm, $\Delta T$ in °C):

   $$\delta_{Al,free} = \alpha_{Al} \, L \, \Delta T = (23.6 \times 10^{-6})(200)(80) = 0.3776 \text{ mm}$$ ✅

2. **Free expansion of the steel housing:**

   $$\delta_{st,free} = \alpha_{st} \, L \, \Delta T = (12 \times 10^{-6})(200)(80) = 0.192 \text{ mm}$$ ✅

3. **Compatibility gap.** Free, the aluminum would grow 0.3776 mm and the steel only 0.192 mm. The pins hold them at one length, so this mismatch must be closed:

   $$\Delta \delta_{free} = 0.3776 - 0.192 = 0.1856 \text{ mm}$$ ✅

   An internal compressive force $P$ shortens the aluminum and an equal tensile force $P$ stretches the steel until the two meet at a common length. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 2: Solve for the internal force

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the compatibility equation solution**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Write the compatibility equation.** Both parts must reach the same elongation $\delta$:

   $$\delta_{Al,free} - \frac{P L}{A_{Al} E_{Al}} = \delta_{st,free} + \frac{P L}{A_{st} E_{st}}$$

2. **Rearrange to isolate $P$.** Put the two $P$ terms on one side and divide every term by $L$:

   $$(\alpha_{Al} - \alpha_{st}) \Delta T = P \left(\frac{1}{A_{Al} E_{Al}} + \frac{1}{A_{st} E_{st}}\right)$$

3. **Compute the two terms in the bracket.** Each says how easily a part stretches. Work in N and mm, with $E$ in MPa (N/mm²), so each term is in 1/N:

   $$\frac{1}{A_{Al} E_{Al}} = \frac{1}{314.2 \times 70{,}000} = 4.546 \times 10^{-8} \text{ 1/N}$$ ✅

   $$\frac{1}{A_{st} E_{st}} = \frac{1}{314.2 \times 200{,}000} = 1.591 \times 10^{-8} \text{ 1/N}$$ ✅

   $$\text{Sum} = 6.137 \times 10^{-8} \text{ 1/N}$$ ✅

4. **Solve for $P$:**

   $$P = \frac{(\alpha_{Al} - \alpha_{st}) \Delta T}{\text{sum}} = \frac{(23.6 - 12) \times 10^{-6} \times 80}{6.137 \times 10^{-8}} = \frac{9.28 \times 10^{-4}}{6.137 \times 10^{-8}} = 15{,}120 \text{ N} \approx 15.1 \text{ kN}$$ ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 3: Stresses in each material

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the stress results and yield check**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Stress in the aluminum rod** (compression, because it grows less than it would if free), force in N over area in mm²:

   $$\sigma_{Al} = \frac{P}{A_{Al}} = \frac{15{,}120}{314.2} = 48.1 \text{ MPa (compressive)}$$ ✅

2. **Stress in the steel housing** (tension, because it grows more than it would if free):

   $$\sigma_{st} = \frac{P}{A_{st}} = \frac{15{,}120}{314.2} = 48.1 \text{ MPa (tensile)}$$ ✅

   The two stresses are equal only because the two areas are equal here. With different areas the force is still the same in both parts, and the stresses differ. ✅

3. **Common actual elongation** (a check on the result):

   $$\delta = \delta_{Al,free} - \frac{P L}{A_{Al} E_{Al}} = 0.3776 - \frac{15{,}120 \times 200}{314.2 \times 70{,}000} = 0.3776 - 0.1375 = 0.2401 \text{ mm}$$ ✅

   Check via steel: $0.192 + \frac{15{,}120 \times 200}{314.2 \times 200{,}000} = 0.192 + 0.0481 = 0.2401$ mm. Both paths give 0.2401 mm. ✅

4. **Yield check:**
   - Aluminum ($\sigma_{yield} \approx 270$ MPa): $SF = 270 / 48.1 = 5.6$. Comfortable. ✅
   - Steel ($\sigma_{yield} \approx 250$ MPa): $SF = 250 / 48.1 = 5.2$. Comfortable. ✅

   If the temperature rise were much larger, the limit would come first in the steel here: the stresses are equal and the steel has the lower yield strength. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

:::note[Engineering Insight]
When two joined materials are heated by the same amount, the one with the higher $\alpha$ ends in compression and the other in tension. Making one part stiffer or thicker changes the size of the stresses, not their sign. On cooling, both signs reverse.
:::

## Design Guidelines for Thermal Systems

<InArticleAd />


<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Match expansion coefficients" icon="setting">
  Where two parts must keep a precise fit over a temperature range, choose materials with similar $\alpha$ values.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Provide room to grow" icon="rocket">
  Expansion joints, slotted holes and sliding ends let a member grow freely, and a free member has no thermal stress.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Check constrained stress against yield" icon="warning">
  Before you weld or bolt a member into a rigid frame, compare $\sigma = E \alpha \Delta T$ with the yield strength. If the margin is small, allow movement. A stronger material does not lower the stress.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Calculate the fit hot" icon="puzzle">
  Clearances and press fits are given at room temperature. Calculate them again at the operating temperature before you fix the cold dimensions.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

:::tip[Instrument it]
Put a thermocouple on each part and a displacement sensor across the clearance, and you can watch the gap change as the parts heat. An [ESP32 or STM32](/education/sensor-actuator-interfacing-stm32/) that reads both lets you [trend the clearance on siliconwit.io](https://siliconwit.io) and show that the part grows freely.
:::

## Summary and Next Steps

<InArticleAd />


### Key concepts

1. **Free expansion** is $\delta_T = \alpha L \Delta T$, with no stress.
2. **Full constraint** gives $\sigma_T = E \alpha \Delta T$, compressive for heating. The length is not in it.
3. **A sliding fit of two materials** opens when the bore has the higher $\alpha$ and closes when the piston has it.
4. **Two joined materials** reach one common elongation. One internal force acts on both, and the stress in each is that force over its area.
